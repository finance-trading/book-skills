# Chapter 8: 投资人"五不"原则 (Investor's Five Don'ts)

## Core Idea
Fisher outlines five common mistakes that investors make — errors that can cost them dearly. These aren't subtle analytical failures; they're fundamental misunderstandings about risk, market structure, and human psychology. Avoiding these five pitfalls is as important as mastering the 15 points.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Don't #1: Don't Buy Companies in the Startup Stage

**The Problem**:
Startup companies are often developing new products or entering high-growth markets — exactly the kind of opportunities Fisher advocates. But there's a critical difference: you can only see their "blueprint," not their actual operations.

**Why It's Dangerous**:
- **Incomplete management teams**: Startups are often dominated by 1-2 people who excel in one area (sales, invention, production) but lack other critical skills. A brilliant inventor may have no idea how to market a product. A great salesperson may not understand production or finance.
- **No track record**: You can't observe how the company handles real-world challenges — supply chain disruptions, customer complaints, employee disputes, competitive pressures. You're guessing based on a plan, not evaluating based on performance.
- **Higher error rate**: Even the most skilled investors make far more mistakes with startups than with established companies. The "batting average" is much lower.
- **You can't provide the help they need**: Startups often need hands-on management support — experienced executives who can fill gaps in the team. Individual investors can't provide this; only professional venture firms can.

**The Exception**:
Professional venture capitalists can invest in startups because they have excellent managers who can step in to fix problems as they arise. They can convince the young management team to accept help. Individual investors have no such leverage.

**The Rule**:
Leave startup investing to professionals. Established companies offer plenty of excellent opportunities. Don't be seduced by the "get in early" argument — the risk of total loss is too high, and the probability of success is too low.

### Don't #2: Don't Ignore Good Stocks Just Because They Trade "Over the Counter" (OTC)

**The Problem**:
Many investors dismiss stocks that don't trade on major exchanges (NYSE, AMEX) because they assume OTC stocks lack liquidity or are somehow inferior.

**The Reality**:
- **Market structure has changed**: In the 1920s, most stock buyers were wealthy individuals making large purchases, often on margin. The market was more like gambling than investing. Today, most buyers are institutional investors (pension funds, mutual funds, insurance companies) representing millions of small investors. They buy for the long term, not to flip.
- **The rise of "salespeople" vs. "order-takers"**: Exchange brokers primarily match buy and sell orders — they're order-takers. OTC dealers actively sell stocks to clients — they're salespeople. This requires more time and effort, so they charge higher commissions. But it also means they provide investment advice and carefully screen which stocks they recommend.
- **Liquidity is better than you think**: OTC dealers maintain inventory in stocks they believe in. If a stock has 2-3 reputable dealers making a market in it, there's usually sufficient liquidity for most investors. You can buy or sell without moving the price significantly.
- **The spread covers the salesperson**: The bid-ask spread in OTC stocks is wider than exchange commissions, but this covers the dealer's cost of maintaining inventory and paying salespeople. If you buy directly from a dealer (not through a salesperson), you can often get the stock at a price comparable to exchange commissions.

**The Rule**:
Focus on finding the right security and a competent dealer, not the trading venue. A good OTC stock with 2-3 reputable dealers making a market in it often has better liquidity than a poorly-traded exchange-listed stock. Don't let the "OTC" label dissuade you — evaluate the company on its merits, not where its stock trades.

### Don't #3: Don't Buy a Stock Just Because You Like the "Tone" of Its Annual Report

**The Problem**:
Investors often judge companies by the quality of their annual reports — the writing style, the photos, the charts, the "visionary" language from the CEO. This is like judging a product by its advertising.

**Why It's Misleading**:
- **Annual reports are PR documents**: They're carefully designed to create a positive impression. The CEO's "personal" letter may be written by the PR department. Beautiful photos and colorful charts don't reflect management quality.
- **They show the "good side"**: Annual reports rarely discuss real problems or difficulties. They're overly optimistic and tend to gloss over challenges.
- **Substance matters, not style**: A company with a poorly designed annual report may be run by brilliant operators who don't care about appearances. A company with a stunning annual report may be run by mediocre managers who excel at PR but not at business.

**The Exception**:
There's one important exception: if the annual report fails to disclose important information that you believe is material, that's a negative signal. A company that hides problems or refuses to discuss challenges is unlikely to provide the transparent communication you need to make informed investment decisions.

**The Rule**:
Don't judge a book by its cover. Look at facts, not presentation. Use scuttlebutt and the 15 points to evaluate the company's actual operations, not its marketing materials. The annual report is one data point — not the deciding factor.

### Don't #4: Don't Assume a High P/E Ratio Means Future Growth Is Already Priced In

**The Fallacy**:
Many investors reason: "If a stock's P/E ratio is 2× the market average, and earnings will double in 5 years, then the current price already reflects future earnings. The stock is overvalued."

**Why This Is Wrong**:
This reasoning assumes that after 5 years, the stock's P/E ratio will fall to the market average. But if the company has a 30-year track record of excellence and continues to develop new products, its P/E ratio will likely remain 2× the market average — just as it has for the past 30 years.

**The XYZ Company Example**:
- XYZ has consistently earned 2× the market P/E for 30 years due to exceptional management and continuous innovation.
- Management projects earnings will double in 5 years (a credible projection based on new products in development).
- Investors say: "The current price already reflects 5 years of growth, so the stock is overvalued."
- The error: Assuming the P/E will fall to the market average after 5 years.
- The reality: If XYZ continues to excel and develop new products, its P/E in 5 years will still be 2× the market average. The stock's price will double along with earnings — and the current price doesn't "reflect" that future growth.

**The ABC Company Example**:
- ABC is a younger company, but its fundamentals are now recognized as excellent.
- Its P/E is now 2× the market average.
- Investors say: "ABC never had such a high P/E before, so it's overvalued."
- The error: Assuming that because ABC didn't have a high P/E in the past, it shouldn't have one now.
- The reality: If ABC's fundamentals have improved and it now deserves a premium valuation, the high P/E reflects its intrinsic value, not overvaluation.

**When High P/E Is Justified**:
- The company has a long track record of excellence (like XYZ).
- The company is continuously developing new products and growth sources.
- The industry has favorable long-term growth prospects.
- Management has demonstrated the ability to execute and innovate.

**When High P/E Is Dangerous**:
- The company's recent growth was a one-time event (e.g., a temporary product boom).
- Management lacks the ability to develop new growth sources after the current momentum fades.
- The industry is cyclical or facing structural decline.
- In these cases, the high P/E does reflect future earnings — because the P/E will fall as growth stalls.

**The Rule**:
Don't blindly assume that high P/E = overvalued. Analyze the company's business characteristics, especially its ability to sustain growth over the long term. If the company has a durable competitive advantage and a track record of innovation, a high P/E may be justified — and may actually be a bargain.

### Don't #5: Don't Nickel-Dime Yourself (Penny Wise, Pound Foolish)

**The Real-Life Example**:
25 years ago, a gentleman decided to buy 100 shares of a NYSE stock. The stock closed at $35.00–$35.50 that day. The next day, the price was the same.

But the gentleman refused to pay $35.50. He placed a limit order at $35.00 to save $30 (100 shares × $0.50 = $50 savings, minus the commission difference). He wouldn't budge.

The stock never dropped to $35.00 again.

25 years later, the stock (adjusted for dividends and splits) is worth far more than $50 per share — many times the price he was unwilling to pay.

**The Lesson**:
In his attempt to save $30–$50, he missed out on thousands (or millions) of dollars in gains. This is the cost of being too price-conscious.

**Why Investors Do This**:
- **Anchoring**: They fixate on a specific price and refuse to pay more, even when the fundamentals justify it.
- **Loss aversion**: They'd rather miss an opportunity than "overpay" by a small amount.
- **False precision**: They believe they can predict short-term price movements and time their entry perfectly.
- **Ignoring the long-term**: They focus on saving a few dollars today, without considering the compounding gains they'll miss over decades.

**The Rule**:
If you've done your research and concluded that a company is excellent, don't haggle over a few cents or dollars. The difference between $35.00 and $35.50 is trivial compared to the difference between owning a great company for 25 years and missing it entirely.

**The Broader Principle**:
Don't let short-term price concerns override long-term value creation. If a company will be worth 10× or 100× its current price in 25 years, paying $0.50 more per share today is irrelevant. Focus on the quality of the business, not the precision of your entry price.

## Key Concepts

- **The startup trap**: Startup companies look like growth opportunities, but you can only see their "blueprint," not their actual operations. The error rate is too high for individual investors. Leave startup investing to professionals.
- **The OTC stigma is outdated**: Over-the-counter stocks can have excellent liquidity if 2-3 reputable dealers make a market in them. Don't dismiss good companies just because they don't trade on a major exchange.
- **Annual reports are PR, not substance**: Beautiful design and visionary language don't reflect management quality. Look at facts, not presentations.
- **High P/E ≠ overvalued**: If a company has a long track record of excellence and continuous innovation, a high P/E may be justified. The key is whether the company can sustain growth, not whether the P/E is "too high" by some arbitrary standard.
- **The cost of penny-pinching**: Trying to save a few dollars on the entry price can cost you thousands (or millions) in missed gains over decades. Focus on quality, not precision.

## Mental Models

- **Use the "blueprint vs. reality" test for startups**: If you can only evaluate a company based on its plan (not its actual operations), the error rate is too high. Wait until the company has a track record.
- **Apply the "dealer quality" test for OTC stocks**: If 2-3 reputable dealers make a market in the stock and maintain inventory, liquidity is likely sufficient. Don't let the "OTC" label dissuade you.
- **Use the "PR vs. substance" filter for annual reports**: Ask: "Does this report disclose real problems and challenges, or is it all sunshine and rainbows?" If it's all positive, be skeptical.
- **Apply the "P/E sustainability" test**: Ask: "Will this company's P/E ratio remain high in 5-10 years?" If yes (because of continuous innovation and excellence), the current high P/E is justified. If no (because growth will stall), the high P/E is dangerous.
- **Use the "25-year perspective" for price decisions**: Ask: "Will I care about the difference between $35.00 and $35.50 in 25 years?" If not, don't haggle — just buy the stock.

## Anti-patterns

- **Buying startups because they "look like" growth opportunities**: You can't evaluate a startup's actual operations — only its plan. The error rate is too high. Leave startup investing to professionals who can provide management support.
- **Dismissing OTC stocks as "illiquid" or "inferior"**: Many OTC stocks have excellent liquidity if reputable dealers make a market in them. Don't let the trading venue override the company's fundamentals.
- **Judging companies by their annual report design**: Beautiful photos and visionary language don't reflect management quality. Look at facts (scuttlebutt, 15 points), not presentations.
- **Assuming high P/E = overvalued**: If a company has a durable competitive advantage and a track record of innovation, a high P/E may be justified. Analyze whether the company can sustain growth, not whether the P/E is "too high."
- **Haggling over entry price**: Trying to save $0.50 per share can cost you thousands in missed gains over decades. If you've concluded the company is excellent, don't let price precision override long-term value creation.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Don't buy startups** — you can only see their "blueprint," not their actual operations. The error rate is too high. Leave startup investing to professionals.
2. **Don't dismiss OTC stocks** — many have excellent liquidity if reputable dealers make a market in them. Focus on the company's fundamentals, not the trading venue.
3. **Don't judge companies by their annual reports** — they're PR documents, not substance. Look at facts (scuttlebutt, 15 points), not presentations.
4. **Don't assume high P/E = overvalued** — if a company has a durable competitive advantage and continuous innovation, a high P/E may be justified. Analyze whether the company can sustain growth.
5. **Don't nickel-dime yourself** — trying to save a few dollars on entry price can cost you thousands in missed gains over decades. Focus on quality, not precision.
6. **The 25-year perspective** — ask: "Will I care about the difference between $35.00 and $35.50 in 25 years?" If not, don't haggle — just buy the stock.
