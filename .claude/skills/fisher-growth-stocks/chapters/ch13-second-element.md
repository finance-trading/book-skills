# Part 2 Chapter 2: 第二个要素 — 人的因素 (Second Element: The Human Factor)

## Core Idea
The second element addresses the cause behind the results discussed in the first element. What makes some companies excel in production, marketing, research, and financial control while others are mediocre or worse? The root cause is people. Fisher draws on Edward Heller's concept of "vivid spirit" — the drive, originality, and determination of an exceptional entrepreneur — but argues that for conservative investment, this quality must be institutionalized in a management team, not concentrated in one person.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Edward Heller's "Vivid Spirit" (朝气蓬勃)

**The Original Insight**: Early venture capitalist Edward F. Heller used the term "vivid spirit" to describe the type of person he was willing to back with large amounts of capital for business development. He said that behind every successful, extraordinary company was a resolute entrepreneur with drive, originality, and the necessary skills to make the company truly worth investing in.

**The Limitation for Conservative Investment**: Heller was absolutely right for very small companies growing into thriving large companies. But as a company grows toward becoming a suitable conservative investment, Heller became increasingly skeptical that one brilliant entrepreneur could sustain it alone. When a friend questioned whether a company's president was his personal crony, Heller's response revealed his concern: "My friend is one of the smartest people I know. He always does things very well. In a small company, this may be fine. But as the company grows, you sometimes have to use the right people."

**The Transition**: Individual brilliance is insufficient for conservative investment. The company must transition from one-person control to a management team.

### The Management Team Requirement

**The Core of the Second Element**: The CEO is committed to long-term growth, surrounded by a group of highly capable assistants, and extensively delegates authority to them to head the company's various departments and functions. These people must not engage in endless internal power struggles — instead, they must work together toward clearly defined corporate objectives.

**Key Requirements for the Management Team**:
1. **Long-term orientation**: Senior management must spend time finding and training qualified, high-morale personnel to replace senior managers when transitions are needed.
2. **Proper delegation**: At every level of the command structure, managers must ensure that what they're doing isn't exactly the same as what the next level down is doing. If it is, delegation has failed.
3. **Internal promotion as the norm**: Generally, companies with high investment value promote from within. This is because the best companies for investment have developed policies and approaches well-suited to their needs. If these special methods really work, it's very hard to retrain people already accustomed to old methods.

**The External Hiring Warning Signal**: The higher the organizational level of new external hires, the higher the cost of instilling new culture. Although no statistical data proves this, Fisher's observation is that among better-managed companies, the number of senior executives hired from outside who disappear after a few years is very high.

**The Definitive Test**: If a large public company must hire an executive from outside, it's definitely a very bad sign — it means current management is fundamentally broken, no matter how good the recent earnings reports look (they're just surface appearance). The new president may do things very well and eventually build a real management team. But rebuilding takes time and carries high risk. Investors who find this happening in their portfolio should review all their investment activities to confirm they acted on sound foundations.

**The Salary Structure Clue**: Public companies must disclose senior executive compensation in proxy statements. If the #1 person's salary is far higher than the #2 or #3 person, warning bells should ring. If compensation gradually decreases down the ranks, that's fine. But a large gap indicates one-person control rather than a real team.

### Motorola's Executive Institute: A Worked Example

**The Challenge (1967)**: Motorola's management recognized that rapid growth in coming years would inevitably require significant expansion of senior management personnel. They decided to face this problem directly.

**The Solution**: Established the Motorola Executive Institute in Oracle, Arizona — away from the daily distractions of offices and factories. Two goals:
1. Train the company's rising stars to handle responsibilities beyond their current roles, so they'd be capable of greater responsibilities in the future
2. Give senior management more important evidence about whether the same people were suitable for promotion

**The Skepticism**: When the Institute was created, some managers questioned whether spending so much money was worthwhile, primarily because they believed the entire Motorola company had only about 100 people with sufficient talent to justify such special training.

**The Results**: These doubters were proven completely wrong. The Institute ran 5-6 classes per year, 14 people per class. By mid-1974, approximately 400 Motorola employees had been trained. Many people — including some who became vice presidents — discovered they possessed capabilities far beyond what they thought they had when admitted.

**The Investment Perspective**: All these things show that with thought and effort, even a company already growing much faster than average can develop the exceptional talent it needs internally, maintaining the company's outstanding performance — rather than relying on the outside hiring approach common in many rapidly growing companies, which often brings in a few high-profile people, causing internal friction and usually proving unsuccessful.

### Three Non-Negotiable Elements for Conservative Investment

Regardless of how policies differ among companies, three elements must be present for a company's stock to be held as a conservative long-term investment tool:

**Element 1: The Company Must Recognize It Operates in a World of Accelerating Change**

All thinking and planning must be directed toward challenging what the company is currently doing — not occasionally, but repeatedly. Every way of doing things taken for granted must be examined and re-examined to confirm the methods truly are the best. As the environment changes, the company should accept some risks in updating methods — no matter how comforting current methods are, they cannot be preserved forever just because they worked in the past or tradition has made them sacred.

**A company that doesn't constantly challenge itself and remains stubbornly stuck in old ways has only one path: downhill.**

**Dow Chemical's Organizational Innovation**: Dow divided management into five different geographic units (US Dow, European Dow, Canadian Dow, etc.). They believed only this way could local problems be handled in ways best suited to local conditions, eliminating the bureaucratic inefficiency that often accompanies growing company size. European Dow's president noted: "As a result, today we're challenged by Dow companies worldwide. They're not our direct competitors, yet they constantly improve, pushing us to be number one."

**The Key Investment Insight**: The most important feature of this change wasn't the change itself, but that Dow's management initiated reform action while its operating efficiency was still far below many other multinational companies — who were still operating quite successfully in traditional ways. In other words, Dow's changes and improvements came from pursuing innovative ideas to make what already worked even better — not from being forced to act by an operational crisis.

**Dow's Pollution Control Innovation**: Dow was far ahead of most companies in recognizing not only that spending money to avoid pollution was necessary, but that major results couldn't be achieved merely through senior management exhortation. The company needed sustained cooperation from middle-level managers. The most effective approach: activate the profit motive of people most directly involved. The company encouraged them to find ways to convert pollutants into saleable products. The result: several firsts in pollution control, praise from environmental groups typically hostile to business, and community acceptance rather than hostility — all at very low total cost, sometimes even with operating profit.

**Element 2: The Company Must Continuously Strive to Make Every Level of Employee Feel It's a Good Place to Work — And This Must Be Real, Not Propaganda**

Most of us spend many hours each week doing what others require for wages, when we'd rather spend that time on leisure. Most people can accept this reality. If management can make employees generally feel (not just a few senior people) that every reasonable thing has been done to create a good work environment and care for their interests, the rewards in increased productivity and lower costs will far exceed the costs of such policies.

**The First Step**: Ensure (not just pay lip service, but genuinely confirm) that every employee receives reasonable respect and care.

Fisher mentions a newspaper report about a union official claiming that one of America's largest companies forced production line workers to eat lunch with oily hands because there weren't enough washrooms and most people didn't have enough time to wash before meals. Whether or not this accusation was factual, if true, this company's stock is not suitable for careful investors to hold.

**Texas Instruments' People Effectiveness Program**:
In TI's early days, senior management believed that if they could establish a system letting all employees participate in management decisions to improve performance, everyone would benefit. In the 1950s, semiconductor production was still mostly manual assembly, and employees had many opportunities to suggest performance improvements. The company held meetings and even formal courses teaching production workers how to propose improvements individually or collectively. Through profit-sharing plans, bonuses, and rewards, participants received both material assistance and felt they were part of the bigger picture.

As operations became more mechanized, some types of individual contribution opportunities decreased because machines controlled what was previously done. Some supervisors in the organization began to feel that lower-level employees could no longer participate in management decisions. Senior management held the opposite view: employee participation would play an even greater role than before — but now through group or team efforts, with employees forming groups to study what could be done and setting their own performance targets.

Because employees felt they (1) truly participated in decisions rather than just taking orders, and (2) received material rewards and recognition for achievements, the program was extraordinarily successful. Countless examples showed that employee teams set their own targets much higher than management would have suggested. Sometimes the targets seemed impossible, or competition between teams created antagonism. Workers would make suggestions and automatically vote on matters never heard of before (for that era) — like reducing coffee break times or shortening lunch breaks — to solve problems. Slow or lazy employees received peer group pressure far more powerful than traditional management discipline.

The results worked across all cultures — US, France, Japan, Singapore, Curaçao (majority Black workforce). Across all countries, because employee teams could report directly to senior management and knew their reports would be taken seriously, with achievements recognized and encouraged, morale was greatly boosted.

**The People Effectiveness Index**: President Mark Shepherd Jr. explained in his 1974 annual shareholder speech that the company designed a people effectiveness index, calculated by dividing net sales by total employees. Despite the company's largest product line being semiconductors — where unit prices continuously decline in today's inflationary world — and despite US wages rising 7% annually, Italy and Japan 20%, the index actually rose from about 2.25% in 1969 to 2.59 by end of 1973. The goal: 3.18 before 1980. If achieved, this company would be a very profitable place to work.

**Element 3: Management Must Lead by Example, Observing the Disciplines Required for Company Growth**

In this rapidly changing world, companies cannot be like stagnant water. They either improve or deteriorate — there's no middle ground. Truly worth-investing growth stocks must not only harvest but also avoid losses.

Very few management teams don't claim their company is growing. However, just because management says it's growth-oriented doesn't mean it actually is. Many companies seem to have an irresistible drive to produce the maximum possible profit at the end of every accounting period — counting every penny they can into earnings.

**Truly growth-oriented companies never do this.** What's on their mind should be earning sufficient current profits to fund the costs needed for business expansion. After adjusting for needed additional funds, companies worth long-term investment, when faced with excellent opportunities to develop new products, processes, establish new product lines, or other great opportunities, make it their priority to suppress immediately obtaining maximum profits — hoping that one dollar spent today will return several dollars in the future.

Such actions include hiring and training new people needed for company growth, and foregoing maximum profit from customer orders — instead, delivering urgently when customers need it, establishing long-term customer loyalty.

**The Test for Conservative Investors**: A good way to verify these actions is to see whether management truly fights for the company's long-term profits, not just surface appearance. No matter how famous a company is, if it only pays lip service to these policies, investors who put money into its stock won't be happy. Companies that try to emulate but give up halfway are the same. For example, a company spending huge research funds but in the wrong direction may achieve nothing.

## Key Concepts

- **Vivid spirit (朝气蓬勃)**: Edward Heller's term for the drive, originality, and determination behind every successful company. Essential for startups but insufficient for conservative investment — must evolve into management teams.
- **Management team vs. one-person control**: Conservative investment requires a CEO committed to long-term growth, surrounded by capable lieutenants with real delegated authority. Not one person controlling everything.
- **Internal promotion**: High-investment-value companies promote from within. External senior hires signal management failure — a company that must hire from outside admits it hasn't developed its own talent.
- **The salary structure test**: Proxy statements reveal whether a company has a real team or one-man control. If the #1 person's pay vastly exceeds #2 and #3, that's a warning sign.
- **Three requirements**: (1) Embrace change — constantly challenge current methods; (2) Make the company a good place to work — genuine respect, not propaganda; (3) Growth discipline — invest for the future, don't milk short-term profits.
- **Motorola's Executive Institute**: Off-site training for high-potential managers. ~400 employees trained by 1974. Many discovered capabilities far beyond initial expectations. Proved that even fast-growing companies can develop talent internally.
- **Texas Instruments' People Effectiveness**: Team-based goal setting, profit sharing, employee participation. Net sales per employee rose despite rising wages. Worked across all cultures (US, France, Japan, Singapore, Curaçao).
- **Dow Chemical's organizational innovation**: Five geographic management units (US Dow, European Dow, Canadian Dow, etc.) to eliminate bureaucracy and encourage local adaptation. Changed while still successful — not in crisis.
- **Dow's pollution control innovation**: Converted pollutants into saleable products by activating middle managers' profit motive. Low cost, sometimes profitable, earned environmental praise and community acceptance.

## Mental Models

- **Use the "salary structure" test**: Look at proxy statements. If executive #1's compensation vastly exceeds #2 and #3, this indicates one-person control — a red flag for conservative investment. A smoothly graded structure indicates a real team.
- **Apply the "internal promotion" test**: Has the company promoted its senior executives from within? If a large public company must hire from outside, that's a very bad sign — current management has failed to develop internal talent.
- **Use the "embrace change" test**: Does the company continuously challenge its own methods? Or does it rest on past success? Dow Chemical reorganized into geographic units while still operating efficiently — proactive change, not crisis-driven.
- **Apply the "good workplace" test**: Do employees at all levels feel respected and heard? Not just lip service — actual open-door policies, profit sharing, genuine participation in decisions. If workers can't wash their hands before lunch, this isn't a conservative investment.
- **Use the "growth discipline" test**: Does management invest for the future (hiring, training, new products) or maximize every quarter's earnings? Truly growth-oriented companies suppress short-term profit maximization for long-term gains.

## Anti-patterns

- **One-person control**: A company dominated by a single brilliant entrepreneur may succeed initially, but lacks the depth for conservative investment. As the company grows, one person cannot handle everything.
- **External senior hiring**: If a large company must bring in senior executives from outside, that signals deep problems. The company has failed to develop internal talent. Exception: occasionally bringing in outsiders to inject fresh perspectives and challenge entrenched routines.
- **Milk-the-quarter mentality**: Management focused on maximizing short-term earnings at the expense of long-term investment. Companies that sacrifice R&D, training, or customer service to boost current profits are destroying future value.
- **Propaganda vs. genuine respect**: Companies that claim to care about employees but don't practice it. If workers can't wash their hands before lunch, that's not a conservative investment — no matter how impressive the financial results.
- **Copying programs without the culture**: Texas Instruments' people effectiveness program works because of decades of genuine commitment to employee participation. A company that copies the mechanics without the underlying culture will fail.
- **Changing only in crisis**: Companies that only reform when facing disaster are reacting, not leading. Dow Chemical changed while still successful — that's proactive management.

## Worked Example

**Motorola's Executive Institute (1967-1974)**:

Motorola recognized that rapid growth would require significant expansion of senior management personnel. Rather than hiring from outside, they established an Executive Institute in Oracle, Arizona — an off-site facility away from daily distractions of offices and factories.

**Goals**:
1. Train the company's rising stars to handle responsibilities beyond their current roles, preparing them for future promotion
2. Give senior management better evidence about whether these people were truly suitable for greater responsibilities

**The Skepticism**: When created, some managers questioned the cost, believing the entire company had only about 100 people with sufficient talent to justify such special training.

**The Results**: They were completely wrong. The Institute ran 5-6 classes per year, 14 people per class. By mid-1974, approximately 400 employees had been trained. Many — including some who became vice presidents — discovered capabilities far beyond what they (or their bosses) initially thought they possessed.

**The Investment Lesson**: This demonstrates that with proper thought and effort, even a company growing much faster than average can develop exceptional talent internally, maintaining outstanding performance without relying on the external hiring approach that often causes internal friction and proves unsuccessful.

**Texas Instruments' People Effectiveness Index**:

TI's program started in the 1950s when semiconductor production was mostly manual assembly. Employees had many opportunities to suggest performance improvements. The company held meetings and courses teaching workers how to propose improvements individually or collectively. Through profit-sharing, bonuses, and rewards, participants received both material benefits and felt part of the bigger picture.

As operations mechanized, individual contribution opportunities decreased in some areas. But senior management believed employee participation would be even more important — through team efforts rather than individual suggestions. Teams set their own performance goals, often far higher than management would have suggested. Peer pressure from teams proved more powerful than traditional management discipline. Workers voluntarily suggested reducing coffee break times or shortening lunch breaks to meet team goals.

**The Numbers**: Net sales per employee (the "people effectiveness index") rose from about 2.25% in 1969 to 2.59 by end of 1973. This was remarkable given US wages rising 7% annually, Italy and Japan 20%. The goal: 3.18 by 1980.

**The Geographic Reach**: The program worked across all cultures — US, France, Japan, Singapore, Curaçao (majority Black workforce). In all countries, employee teams could report directly to senior management and knew their reports would be taken seriously, with achievements recognized and encouraged.

**The Investment Lesson**: When employees feel they (1) truly participate in decisions and (2) receive material rewards and recognition, the results are extraordinary. Productivity rises, costs fall, and the company becomes more competitive — even in inflationary environments with rising wages.

**Dow Chemical's Geographic Reorganization**:

Dow divided management into five different geographic units (US Dow, European Dow, Canadian Dow, etc.). The belief: only this way could local problems be handled in ways best suited to local conditions, eliminating bureaucratic inefficiency that often accompanies growing company size.

European Dow's president noted: "As a result, today we're challenged by Dow companies worldwide. They're not our direct competitors, yet they constantly improve, pushing us to be number one."

**The Key Insight**: The most important feature wasn't the change itself, but that Dow initiated reform while its operating efficiency was still far below many other multinationals — who were still operating quite successfully in traditional ways. Dow's changes came from pursuing innovative ideas to make what already worked even better — not from being forced to act by crisis.

**The Investment Lesson**: Companies that change proactively — while still successful — demonstrate the management quality that conservative investment requires. Companies that only change when forced by crisis are reacting, not leading.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The second element is about people quality**: The first element (production, marketing, R&D, finance) is the result; the second element is the cause — the people who produce those results.
2. **Vivid spirit must become institutional**: Individual brilliance gets companies started, but lasting conservative investment requires a management team, not one-person control.
3. **Internal promotion is a critical signal**: Companies with high investment value promote from within. External senior hires signal management development failure.
4. **Three non-negotiable requirements**: (1) Embrace change — continuously challenge current methods; (2) Make the company a good place to work — genuine respect for all employees; (3) Growth discipline — invest for the future, don't maximize short-term profits.
5. **The salary structure test reveals reality**: Proxy statements show whether a company has a real management team or one-person control. A large gap between #1 and #2 compensation is a red flag.
6. **Motorola and Texas Instruments prove the principle**: Both companies developed systematic programs to identify, train, and empower talent from within — with extraordinary results across cultures and countries.
7. **Dow Chemical demonstrates proactive change**: Reorganized into geographic units while still operating efficiently — not waiting for crisis. This is the management quality conservative investment requires.
8. **The test for conservative investment**: Does the company have the people quality to sustain excellence? Not just current results, but the ability to continue producing those results through future challenges and changing conditions.
