# Chapter 7: 股利杂音 (Dividend Noise)

## Core Idea
Fisher dismantles the obsession with dividends, arguing that investors who focus on dividend yield are missing the point. For growth companies, retaining earnings to fund expansion is far more valuable than paying them out as dividends. The best dividend policy is one that maximizes long-term growth, not current income. And consistency matters more than the level — a stable policy attracts the right kind of shareholders.

## Frameworks Introduced

### When Retained Earnings Benefit Shareholders

**Scenario 1: Management accumulates excess cash beyond current and future needs.**
Some executives hoard cash for a false sense of security. They don't realize that their "safety" comes at the expense of shareholder wealth. Shareholders should have the right to deploy that capital as they see fit.

**Scenario 2: Management lacks the ability to earn a good return on retained earnings.**
Inefficient management may use retained earnings to expand unprofitable operations rather than improving them. They build a larger but less efficient empire, and compensate themselves with higher salaries for "doing more." Result: shareholders get little or no benefit from retained earnings.

**Scenario 3: Retained earnings are used for "defensive" assets that don't increase business value.**
Example: Retail stores installing expensive air conditioning systems. Once every competitor does the same, no one gains market share — but stores that don't install A/C lose customers on hot days. These assets don't increase sales, but they're necessary to maintain them. Accounting standards don't distinguish between these "defensive" assets and productive assets, so shareholders feel shortchanged.

**Scenario 4: Accounting deficiencies distort the true value of retained earnings.**
In an inflationary world, standard accounting treats currency value as constant — like using two-dimensional geometry in a three-dimensional world. Depreciation based on historical cost is insufficient to replace assets at current prices. Companies must retain additional earnings just to maintain their existing asset base, not to grow.

**The Growth Company Advantage**: Growth companies are less affected by this problem because they're constantly acquiring new assets at current prices, so their depreciation is closer to replacement cost. The gap between historical cost and current value is smaller.

### The Tax and Transaction Cost Argument

**The "Sacrifice Small Shareholders" Myth**:
Critics argue that retaining earnings favors large shareholders (who face higher tax rates) at the expense of small shareholders (who face lower tax rates). This is backwards.

**The Reality**:
Most shareholders — regardless of size — pay at least 20% tax on dividends, plus brokerage commissions. Small purchases face higher percentage costs due to odd-lot rates and minimum commissions. After taxes and fees, less than 80% of dividend income is available for reinvestment.

**The Math**:
If a company retains earnings instead of paying dividends, shareholders get 100% of that capital working for them — tax-free and commission-free. If the company pays dividends, shareholders get ~80% (after taxes and fees) to reinvest on their own.

**The Risk Factor**:
If you've chosen an excellent company (one that meets the 15 points), letting its management reinvest retained earnings is lower-risk than taking dividends and trying to find another equally excellent company on your own. The better the company, the more important this factor becomes.

### The Consistency Principle

**The Restaurant Analogy**:
A restaurant can succeed with a high-price policy or a low-price policy. It can serve fancy French cuisine or cheap Chinese food. Each approach attracts a customer base that expects that style.

But if the restaurant switches constantly — expensive one day, cheap the next, foreign cuisine the day after — it will never build a loyal customer base. Customers don't know what to expect.

**The Dividend Policy Parallel**:
A company's dividend policy is like its restaurant policy. It should be consistent. Some companies pay no dividends for years, then distribute 25-40% of earnings once depreciation provides sufficient cash flow. Others maintain a stable payout ratio.

The key is consistency. Shareholders want predictability so they can plan their finances. A company that changes its dividend policy frequently attracts a unstable shareholder base — people who buy and sell based on dividend expectations, not long-term business value.

**The Smart Dividend Policy**:
- Base the policy on what maximizes long-term growth.
- Communicate the policy clearly to shareholders.
- Stick to it — even if it means temporarily disappointing shareholders.
- Only increase dividends when you're confident the new level can be maintained.
- Only decrease dividends in the most extreme emergencies.

**Example: Rohm & Haas**:
First publicly traded in 1949 at $41.25 per share, with a $1.00 cash dividend plus stock dividends. Many investors considered it unsuitable for conservative investment because the yield was so low.

But the company consistently followed its policy: reinvest most earnings, pay small cash dividends, issue regular stock dividends. By 1955, the stock price had exceeded $400 — a 10× increase. Original shareholders received 4% stock dividends annually, 3% in 1956. The capital gains far exceeded what they would have received from a high-yield stock.

**The Paradox**: Those who worry least about dividends end up receiving the highest dividend income in the long run. Growth companies that retain earnings and compound their value eventually generate far more wealth — and far higher dividends — than high-yield stocks that don't grow.

## Key Concepts

- **Retained earnings vs. dividends**: The question isn't "should we pay dividends?" but "can management earn a better return on retained earnings than shareholders can earn on their own?"
- **The tax and transaction cost drag**: Dividends face immediate taxation and brokerage fees, leaving less than 80% available for reinvestment. Retained earnings work for shareholders tax-free.
- **The consistency principle**: A stable dividend policy attracts long-term shareholders. Frequent changes attract short-term traders who care more about dividends than business value.
- **The growth company advantage**: Growth companies are less affected by inflation-related accounting distortions because they're constantly acquiring new assets at current prices.
- **The "sacrifice small shareholders" myth**: This is backwards. Retaining earnings benefits all shareholders by avoiding taxes and fees, and by letting excellent management deploy capital more effectively than individual shareholders can.

## Mental Models

- **Think of retained earnings as "tax-free, commission-free reinvestment"**: When a company retains earnings, shareholders get 100% of that capital working for them. When a company pays dividends, shareholders get ~80% (after taxes and fees) to reinvest on their own.
- **Use the restaurant analogy to evaluate dividend policy consistency**: A company that changes its dividend policy frequently is like a restaurant that changes its cuisine daily — it will never build a loyal customer base.
- **Apply the "excellent management test"**: If you've chosen a company that meets the 15 points, letting its management reinvest retained earnings is lower-risk than taking dividends and trying to find another equally excellent company on your own.

## Anti-patterns

- **Focusing on dividend yield instead of total return**: High-dividend stocks often have limited growth potential. Over 10+ years, growth stocks with low yields generate far higher total returns — including dividend income.
- **Believing that high dividends are a "safety factor"**: Research shows that high-dividend stocks underperform more often than low-dividend stocks. High dividends often signal that management lacks profitable reinvestment opportunities.
- **Demanding that companies pay dividends when they have better uses for the capital**: If management can earn a 20% return on retained earnings, paying dividends forces shareholders to find investments that match that return on their own — unlikely.
- **Judging management by whether they "do something" for shareholders via dividends**: Retaining earnings to build new factories, launch new product lines, or install cost-saving equipment creates far more value than paying dividends — if management is excellent.
- **Changing dividend policy frequently**: This attracts short-term traders and repels long-term investors. Consistency builds a stable shareholder base that cares about business value, not dividend timing.

## Worked Example

**The XYZ Company Dividend Debate**:
Imagine XYZ Company, a well-managed firm with steady earnings growth. Four years ago, it paid out 50% of earnings as dividends. Earnings have grown substantially, and now the same dividend payout requires only 25% of earnings.

Some directors argue: "We should increase the dividend to reward shareholders."
Others counter: "We've never had so many opportunities to invest retained earnings. We should maintain the current payout ratio to maximize growth."

Then a director raises the "sacrifice small shareholders" argument: "Large shareholders face higher tax rates, so they prefer lower dividends. Small shareholders need the income, so they want higher dividends. By not increasing the dividend, we're favoring the rich."

**Fisher's Rebuttal**:
This argument is backwards. Most shareholders — regardless of size — pay at least 20% tax on dividends, plus brokerage fees. After taxes and fees, less than 80% of dividend income is available for reinvestment.

If XYZ retains earnings instead of paying dividends:
- Shareholders get 100% of that capital working for them (tax-free, commission-free).
- Excellent management deploys the capital in high-return projects.
- Shareholders avoid the risk of trying to find equally excellent investments on their own.

If XYZ pays dividends instead:
- Shareholders get ~80% (after taxes and fees) to reinvest on their own.
- They face the difficult task of finding another company as good as XYZ.
- They pay taxes immediately, reducing their compounding potential.

**The Paradox**: The shareholders who worry least about dividends end up receiving the highest dividend income in the long run. By letting excellent management reinvest retained earnings, they maximize the company's growth — and eventually, their dividend income (as a percentage of original investment) far exceeds what they would have received from a high-yield stock.

**Rohm & Haas (1949-1956)**:
First publicly traded in 1949 at $41.25 per share, with a $1.00 cash dividend (yield ~2.4%) plus stock dividends. Many investors rejected it as unsuitable for conservative investment because the yield was so low.

But the company consistently followed its policy: reinvest most earnings, pay small cash dividends, issue regular stock dividends. By 1955, the stock price had exceeded $400 — a 10× increase. Original shareholders received 4% stock dividends annually, 3% in 1956.

**The Result**: Shareholders who ignored the low yield and held the stock achieved extraordinary wealth. Those who demanded high dividends and sold the stock missed out on one of the best growth stories of the era.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Retained earnings are "tax-free, commission-free reinvestment"** — when a company retains earnings, shareholders get 100% of that capital working for them, vs. ~80% if paid as dividends (after taxes and fees).
2. **Focus on total return, not dividend yield** — high-dividend stocks often have limited growth potential. Over 10+ years, growth stocks with low yields generate far higher total returns.
3. **Consistency matters more than the dividend level** — a stable dividend policy attracts long-term shareholders. Frequent changes attract short-term traders.
4. **The "sacrifice small shareholders" myth is backwards** — retaining earnings benefits all shareholders by avoiding taxes and fees, and by letting excellent management deploy capital more effectively.
5. **The paradox of dividends**: Those who worry least about dividends end up receiving the highest dividend income in the long run — because growth companies that retain earnings compound their value and eventually generate far higher dividends than high-yield stocks.
6. **Judge management by its ability to reinvest retained earnings, not by its dividend payout** — if management can earn a 20% return on retained earnings, paying dividends forces shareholders to find investments that match that return on their own.
