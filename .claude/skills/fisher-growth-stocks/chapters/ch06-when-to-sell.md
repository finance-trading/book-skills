# Chapter 6: 何时卖出 — 以及何时不要卖出 (When to Sell — And When Not To)

## Core Idea
Fisher's counterintuitive thesis: if you bought correctly, the time to sell almost never arrives. There are only three valid reasons to sell a growth stock, and most investors sell for all the wrong reasons. The real art is knowing when to hold — forever, if the company remains excellent.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Three Valid Reasons to Sell

**Reason 1: You made a mistake in your original analysis.**
If subsequent events reveal that your original assessment was wrong — that the company doesn't actually meet the 15 points as well as you thought — sell immediately. Don't wait for the market to correct your error.

**Reason 2: The company's fundamentals have deteriorated permanently.**
Two main causes:
- **Management deterioration**: Key executives become complacent after success. New hires don't match the standards of the original team. The policies that made the company great are abandoned or poorly executed.
- **Market exhaustion**: After years of exceptional growth, the company's market potential is exhausted. It can now only grow at the rate of the overall economy, not faster.

**The Test**: Ask yourself: "At the next business cycle peak, will this company's earnings per share (adjusted for dividends and stock splits, but not new equity issuances) be at least as much higher than today's level as they were from the last known cycle peak to today?" If yes, hold. If no, consider selling.

**Reason 3: You find a significantly better opportunity (very rare).**
If you discover a company with clearly superior growth prospects than your current holding, switching may be justified — but only if you're highly confident in your judgment. The capital gains tax makes switching expensive, so the new opportunity must be substantially better, not just marginally better.

**Example**: A company growing at 12% annually vs. one growing at 20% annually — the difference justifies the tax cost of switching. But a switch from 15% to 17% does not.

### When NOT to Sell

**Don't sell because you fear a general market decline.**
Economic forecasting is alchemy (as Fisher argued in Chapter 5). You cannot reliably predict when a bear market will arrive. If you sell now to avoid a hypothetical decline, you'll likely never buy back at a lower price. Meanwhile, you miss the compounding gains of an excellent company.

**Don't sell because the stock has "gone up too much."**
This is perhaps the most absurd reason investors sell. What does "too high" even mean? You cannot accurately predict what a company's earnings will be in 2-5 years, so how can you judge whether the current price is "too high" relative to future earnings?

**The College Classmate Analogy**:
Imagine on graduation day, each classmate offers you a deal: pay them 10× their first year's salary, and they'll give you 25% of all future earnings. You pick three classmates you believe will earn the most.

Ten years later, one classmate is on track to become CEO of a major company, earning a very high salary. Someone offers to buy your "contract" with him for 6× the original price. Would you sell? Of course not — his future earning power is far greater now than when you made the deal.

Yet this is exactly what investors do when they sell a successful growth stock because it has "gone up too much." The company's future growth potential is greater, not lesser, because of its success.

**The Key Difference**: A classmate has a limited lifespan. A company can live forever — it can hire and train excellent managers, preserving its vitality across generations. DuPont is in its second century. Dow Chemical thrives decades after its brilliant founder died. Companies have no fixed lifespan like humans do.

**Don't sell just because the stock has appreciated significantly.**
The "let's take our profits" mentality is foolish. If the company remains excellent, its future growth will far exceed what you've already gained. Selling locks in a small gain and forfeits a much larger one.

## Key Concepts

- **The "hold forever" principle**: If you bought correctly, you should almost never sell. The ideal holding period is indefinite.
- **Management deterioration vs. market exhaustion**: Two distinct reasons a company may no longer qualify as a growth stock. Distinguish between them — one is urgent (sell immediately), the other is gradual (sell when you find a better opportunity).
- **The earnings growth test**: A practical way to assess whether a company still has growth potential. Compare projected future earnings growth to past earnings growth.
- **Capital gains tax as a barrier to switching**: The tax cost of selling makes switching expensive. Only switch if the new opportunity is substantially better, not marginally better.
- **The absurdity of "selling high"**: You cannot reliably judge whether a growth stock is "overpriced" because you cannot accurately predict future earnings. The market may be right, or it may be wrong — you have no way to know with precision.

## Mental Models

- **Think of buying a stock as "hiring" a management team to work for you**: You wouldn't fire an excellent employee just because they've done a good job. Why sell an excellent company just because it's performed well?
- **Use the college classmate analogy to test your selling logic**: If you wouldn't sell the "contract" on your successful classmate, why sell the stock of a successful company?
- **Apply the earnings growth test rigorously**: Before selling, ask: "Will this company's earnings per share at the next cycle peak be significantly higher than today?" If yes, hold.

## Anti-patterns

- **Selling because you fear a market decline**: You cannot predict when a bear market will arrive. If you sell now, you'll likely never buy back at a lower price. Meanwhile, you miss years of compounding gains.
- **Selling because the stock has "gone up too much"**: This is absurd. A company's future growth potential is not diminished by its past success. If anything, it's enhanced.
- **Selling to "take profits" or "lock in gains"**: This locks in a small gain and forfeits a much larger one. If the company remains excellent, its future growth will far exceed what you've already gained.
- **Selling to switch to a marginally better opportunity**: The capital gains tax makes switching expensive. Only switch if the new opportunity is substantially better (e.g., 20% annual growth vs. 12%), not marginally better (17% vs. 15%).
- **Assuming you can time the market**: If you sell now because you fear a decline, when will you buy back? You must correctly predict not just that a decline will occur, but when it will end. This is nearly impossible.

## Worked Example

**American Cyanamid (1954-1959)**:
Fisher describes how some investment funds bought American Cyanamid in 1954 at an average price of $4.50 per share (after a 2-for-1 stock split in 1957). The company had struggled with its Fortier, Louisiana plant, which faced unexpected problems and delayed profitability. The market punished the stock.

But patient investors who understood the 15 points recognized:
- New management was excellent (Points 7, 8, 14)
- The Lederle pharmaceutical division was outstanding (Points 2, 3)
- Cost-cutting was improving the industrial and agricultural chemical divisions (Point 5)
- The problems were temporary, not fundamental

By 1959, the stock price had risen to ~$60, earnings had grown 70%, and the market value had increased 163%.

**But here's the lesson**: Those investment funds sold in 1959 at ~$49, locking in a 110% gain. Fisher criticizes this decision — not because it was wrong to sell (the gain was real), but because the reasons for selling were questionable:
1. They switched to another company with seemingly better prospects (Reason 3 — but the evidence wasn't yet clear that the switch was correct).
2. They worried that American Cyanamid's chemical division wouldn't improve profit margins as expected (a legitimate concern, but Lederle's pharmaceutical division was thriving with new antibiotics and a promising oral polio vaccine).

**The lesson**: Even when you sell at a profit, ask yourself: "Was this the right decision, or did I sell too early because I lacked patience?" In this case, Fisher suggests the funds may have sold prematurely — the company's future looked bright, and the stock likely had much more upside.

**Motorola (the "lucky and able" company)**:
Fisher describes how Motorola evolved from a two-way radio company into a diversified electronics leader. When the TV market saturated in the 1950s, Motorola didn't stagnate — it leveraged its electronics expertise into semiconductors, high-fidelity audio, industrial electronics, and military communications. By the early 1960s, these new product lines exceeded TV revenue.

The stock went from $45.50 to $122 during this period — a demonstration of how the 15-point framework identifies companies that can create new revenue curves when old ones plateau.

**The lesson**: Don't sell just because a company's original product line has matured. If management is excellent (Point 14), it will find new growth opportunities.

## Key Takeaways

1. **If you bought correctly, the time to sell almost never arrives** — hold for the long term, ideally forever.
2. **There are only three valid reasons to sell**: (1) you made a mistake in your original analysis, (2) the company's fundamentals have deteriorated permanently, or (3) you find a significantly better opportunity.
3. **Don't sell because you fear a market decline** — you cannot reliably predict when a bear market will arrive, and you'll likely never buy back at a lower price.
4. **Don't sell because the stock has "gone up too much"** — this is absurd. A company's future growth potential is not diminished by its past success.
5. **Use the earnings growth test**: Before selling, ask whether the company's earnings per share at the next cycle peak will be significantly higher than today. If yes, hold.
6. **Capital gains tax makes switching expensive** — only switch if the new opportunity is substantially better, not marginally better.
7. **The "hold forever" principle is the ultimate goal** — if you can identify truly excellent companies and hold them indefinitely, you will achieve extraordinary returns.
