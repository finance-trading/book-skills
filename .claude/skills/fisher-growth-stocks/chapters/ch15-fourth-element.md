# Part 2 Chapter 4: 第四个要素 — 保守型投资的价格 (Fourth Element: The Price of Conservative Investing)

## Core Idea
The fourth element involves P/E ratios and the concept of "appraisal" (评价) — how the financial community perceives a company's value. Stock prices move not because of what actually happens at a company, but because of what the financial community believes is happening. Understanding appraisal gaps — where perception diverges from fundamentals — is the key to identifying truly conservative investments versus dangerous overvaluations.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The "Appraisal" (评价) Concept

**The Fundamental Principle**: Any individual stock, relative to the overall market, moves significantly in price whenever the financial community's appraisal of that stock changes.

**The Key Insight**: Appraisal is subjective. It may or may not relate to what's actually happening in the real world. What matters is what the person making the appraisal believes is happening, regardless of how far their judgment diverges from facts.

**The Principle Restated**: No individual stock rises or falls because of what actually happens at that company, or will happen. It rises or falls because of the financial community's current consensus view about what is happening and will happen — regardless of how far that consensus is from what truly is or will be happening.

**The G Company Example**:
Two years ago, Company G was considered quite ordinary. EPS was $1, P/E was 10x, price was $10. Over two years, while most competitors declined, G company — thanks to a series of excellent new products and improving margins on old ones — reported EPS of $1.40 last year, $1.82 this year, with further growth expected. The contrast with competitors was stark, and the actions G took internally had clearly been underway for much longer than two years.

However, the financial community was slow to recognize that G company met the first three elements. The delayed appraisal caused the P/E ratio to rise to 22x. Compared to other stocks with similar characteristics and growth prospects, 22x didn't seem too high. 22 × $1.82 = $40, so the stock price rose 300% in two years.

**The Dual Effect**: The G company example illustrates how earnings growth and P/E expansion work together to drive extraordinary returns. As the financial community correctly discovers from fundamentals that investment value is far higher than the old appraisal reflected, the P/E ratio increase often becomes more important than the actual earnings increase in driving stock price gains.

### The Four-Tier Risk Stratification

**Tier 1 — Lowest Risk (最适吅聪明人投资)**:
- Three elements are met to a very high degree
- Current financial community appraisal places value BELOW what fundamentals warrant
- P/E ratio is undervalued relative to true worth
- **Action**: Ideal buying opportunity

**Tier 2 — Next Lowest Risk (通常也适吅聪明人投资)**:
- Three elements are met to a fairly high degree
- Appraisal roughly in line with fundamentals
- P/E ratio approximately matches true worth
- **Rationale**: If these companies truly possess these qualities, they will continue growing, and the stock will appreciate over time

**Tier 3 — Next Lowest Risk (适吅保守型投资人持有，但不适吅新资金首次购买)**:
- Three elements are equally good
- These qualities have become well-known facts in the financial community
- Appraisal or P/E ratio is HIGHER than fundamentals warrant
- **Action**: Hold these stocks, but don't buy new money
- **Reasoning**: If fundamentals are truly strong, earnings will eventually rise enough to justify — or even surpass — current prices. Meanwhile, truly attractive companies meeting the first three elements are rare. Value stocks are hard to find. The risk of making a mistake and buying a stock that appears to meet all three elements but actually doesn't is far higher than the temporary risk of holding a fundamentally sound but currently overvalued stock — because true value will eventually catch up to current price.
- **Warning**: Investors who sell these stocks hoping to buy back at more appropriate times rarely succeed. The drop they wait for usually never materializes. Years later, these fundamentally strong stocks rise to even higher prices, and they've missed all the subsequent gains — and may end up buying essentially inferior stocks instead.

**Tier 4 — Higher Risk**:
- Three elements are ordinary or relatively low quality
- Financial community's appraisal is below or roughly in line with these unattractive fundamentals
- **Action**: These may be good speculative vehicles, but not suitable for intelligent conservative investors
- **Rationale**: Today's rapidly changing world is full of dangers; adverse developments can seriously affect these stocks

**Tier 5 — Highest Risk (最危险)**:
- Financial community's current appraisal or impression is FAR HIGHER than current circumstances can support
- **Action**: Buying these stocks can lead to severe losses and drive investors to dump holdings, shaking the foundations of the investment industry
- **Examples**: Memory Technology high of $173.25, Ampex high of $49, Levitz Furniture high of $60, Mohawk Data Sciences high of $81, Litton Industries high of $101, Kalvar high of $176

### Why Appraisal Diverges from Fundamentals

**The Time Gap**: The gap between financial community appraisal and the real situation affecting prices can persist for several years. But eventually reality breaks through — sometimes in months, sometimes much later.

**The Overvaluation Cycle**: When unrealistic expectations push a stock's price too high, eventually more and more holders grow weary of waiting. Their selling immediately overwhelms the few new buyers still confident in the old appraisal. The stock price crashes. Sometimes the new appraisal that follows is quite grounded. But the emotional pressure of falling prices causes a reassessment that often overemphasizes negative factors, making the financial community's new appraisal even more pessimistic than reality warrants. This depressed view may persist for a while.

**The Undervaluation Cycle**: When this happens, what occurs resembles the overvaluation scenario — just reversed. It may take months or years for a more favorable impression to replace the current one. But as pleasing earnings figures keep climbing, eventually it will happen. The fortunate stockholders — investors who didn't sell when the stock started rising — then reap the benefits: maximum reward relative to stock market risk. Under the combined effect of steadily rising EPS and simultaneously surging P/E ratios, the stock price will surge dramatically.

### Definitions and Clarifications

**"Significant Price Movement"**: Fisher uses this term (not just "price movement") to exclude:
- Small fluctuations caused by clumsy brokers dumping large blocks of shares
- Temporary price spikes from institutional buyers accumulating positions
- These small movements don't represent genuine changes in the financial community's overall appraisal

**"Financial Community"**: Includes everyone with the ability and interest to buy or sell a particular stock at a given price. Each potential buyer and seller's importance depends on the strength of their buying or selling power.

## Key Concepts

- **Appraisal (评价)**: The financial community's subjective perception of a company's value. This drives stock prices more than actual fundamentals in the short-to-medium term.
- **P/E ratio as appraisal measure**: The P/E ratio reflects the financial community's current appraisal. High P/E = high appraisal; low P/E = low appraisal.
- **The dual effect**: When earnings growth and P/E expansion work together, stock prices can surge dramatically. The P/E expansion often contributes more to price gains than the earnings growth itself.
- **The G company principle**: A company that meets the first three elements but is currently under-appraised represents the lowest-risk, highest-reward investment opportunity.
- **The overvaluation trap**: Stocks where appraisal far exceeds fundamentals are the most dangerous. Buying them leads to severe losses when reality eventually breaks through.
- **The hold-don't-sell principle**: For fundamentally strong but currently overvalued stocks, hold but don't buy new money. Don't sell hoping to buy back lower — you rarely succeed.
- **The time gap**: Appraisal can diverge from fundamentals for years, but reality eventually wins. Patience is essential.

## Mental Models

- **Use the "appraisal gap" test**: Ask: "Is the financial community's current appraisal of this stock higher or lower than fundamentals warrant?" If appraisal is below fundamentals, it's a buying opportunity. If appraisal far exceeds fundamentals, it's dangerous.
- **Apply the "four-tier" framework**: Classify any stock you're considering into one of the four tiers based on (1) how well it meets the first three elements, and (2) whether its appraisal is below, in line with, or above fundamentals.
- **Use the "dual effect" lens**: When evaluating potential returns, consider both earnings growth AND potential P/E expansion. The biggest gains come when both work in your favor.
- **Apply the "hold-don't-sell" rule**: If you own a fundamentally strong stock that's temporarily overvalued, hold it. Don't sell hoping to buy back lower — you'll likely miss the subsequent gains.

## Anti-patterns

- **Selling fundamentally strong stocks because P/E looks high**: If the company truly meets the first three elements, earnings will eventually catch up to justify the price. Selling and waiting to buy back rarely works.
- **Buying stocks where appraisal far exceeds fundamentals**: These are the most dangerous investments. When reality breaks through, losses are severe. The examples (Memory Technology, Ampex, Levitz, Mohawk Data, Litton, Kalvar) all crashed.
- **Confusing small price fluctuations with appraisal changes**: Broker block trades or institutional accumulation cause temporary price movements, not genuine changes in the financial community's overall appraisal. Don't mistake noise for signal.
- **Expecting appraisal to align with fundamentals immediately**: The gap can persist for years. Patience is essential. But reality eventually wins — the question is whether you can hold long enough.
- **Focusing on earnings alone without considering appraisal**: The biggest gains come from the dual effect — earnings growth AND P/E expansion. A company with modest earnings growth but dramatically improving appraisal can outperform a company with strong earnings growth but declining appraisal.

## Worked Example

**The G Company Story (P/E Expansion and the Dual Effect)**:

Two years ago, Company G was considered quite ordinary. EPS was $1, P/E was 10x, stock price was $10.

Over the next two years:
- While most competitors declined, G company — thanks to excellent new products and improving margins — reported EPS of $1.40 last year, $1.82 this year
- The financial community was slow to recognize that G met the first three elements
- Eventually, the delayed appraisal caused the P/E ratio to rise to 22x
- 22 × $1.82 = $40 stock price
- Result: 300% gain in two years

**Breaking Down the Gain**:
- Earnings growth: $1 → $1.82 = 82% increase
- P/E expansion: 10x → 22x = 120% increase
- Combined effect: Stock price rose from $10 to $40 = 300% increase

**The Lesson**: The P/E expansion (120%) contributed more to the price gain than the earnings growth (82%). This illustrates the power of the dual effect — when the financial community's appraisal catches up to fundamentals, the resulting P/E expansion can drive extraordinary returns.

**The Four-Tier Classification Applied**:

At the start (P/E 10x): G company was Tier 1 — three elements highly met, appraisal below fundamentals. This was the ideal buying opportunity.

At the end (P/E 22x): G company moved to Tier 3 — three elements still excellent, but appraisal now above fundamentals. The correct action: hold, but don't buy new money.

**The Overvaluation Examples**:

Fisher lists stocks where appraisal far exceeded fundamentals:
- Memory Technology: high of $173.25
- Ampex: high of $49
- Levitz Furniture: high of $60
- Mohawk Data Sciences: high of $81
- Litton Industries: high of $101
- Kalvar: high of $176

These represent Tier 5 — the most dangerous category. When reality eventually broke through, these stocks crashed, causing severe losses for investors who bought at the peak.

**The Lesson**: Compare the financial community's current appraisal with actual fundamentals. If appraisal far exceeds what circumstances can support, the stock is extremely dangerous.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Stock prices move because of appraisal changes, not actual events**: The financial community's perception drives prices more than fundamentals in the short-to-medium term. Understanding appraisal is essential.
2. **Appraisal is subjective**: It may or may not relate to reality. What matters is what the financial community believes, regardless of how far that diverges from facts.
3. **The dual effect drives extraordinary returns**: When earnings growth and P/E expansion work together, stock prices surge. The P/E expansion often contributes more than the earnings growth itself.
4. **Four-tier risk stratification**: Classify stocks by (1) how well they meet the first three elements, and (2) whether appraisal is below, in line with, or above fundamentals. Lowest risk = excellent fundamentals + undervalued appraisal.
5. **Hold fundamentally strong but overvalued stocks**: If the company truly meets the first three elements, earnings will eventually catch up. Selling and waiting to buy back rarely works.
6. **Avoid stocks where appraisal far exceeds fundamentals**: These are the most dangerous investments. When reality breaks through, losses are severe.
7. **The time gap can be long**: Appraisal can diverge from fundamentals for years. Patience is essential. But reality eventually wins.
8. **Small price fluctuations aren't appraisal changes**: Don't mistake broker block trades or institutional accumulation for genuine changes in the financial community's overall appraisal.
