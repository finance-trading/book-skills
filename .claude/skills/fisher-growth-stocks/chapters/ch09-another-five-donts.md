# Chapter 9: 投资人"另五不"原则 (Investor's Another Five Don'ts)

## Core Idea
Fisher outlines five more investor mistakes: over-diversification, fear of buying during wars, emphasizing irrelevant historical statistics, ignoring timing factors, and following the crowd. These errors stem from psychological biases, false precision, and misunderstanding about what actually drives stock prices.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Don't #6: Don't Over-Diversify

**The Problem**:
Diversification is widely praised, but few consider the downside of over-diversification. Putting eggs in too many baskets means:
- Many eggs won't be in good baskets.
- You can't watch all the baskets closely enough.

Among investors with portfolios worth $250K–$500K, a shockingly high proportion own 25+ different stocks. The problem isn't the number — it's that only a few of those stocks are ones the investor truly understands.

**The Machine Gun Analogy**:
A soldier stabilizes a rifle with two supports, but five or six are more stable. Ten supports aren't significantly more stable than five. The key is finding the optimal number — but there's a crucial difference from stocks: with rifles, the number of supports needed doesn't depend on the type of rifle. With stocks, the characteristics of the stocks themselves determine how much diversification is needed.

**Factors Affecting Needed Diversification**:
- **Internal diversification**: Some companies (like large chemical manufacturers) produce products with completely different characteristics, serving different industries and customers. A single stock may provide more diversification than you think.
- **Management depth**: A company with deep, capable management is less risky than one dependent on one person.
- **Cyclical vs. non-cyclical**: Stocks in highly cyclical industries require more diversification than stocks in stable industries.

**Category A: Large, Established Growth Stocks**
- Examples: Dow Chemical, DuPont, IBM
- Minimum: At least 5 such stocks
- Allocation: No more than 20% of original investment in each
- Note: Don't rebalance if one grows faster — if after 10 years it represents 40% of your portfolio because it grew faster, that's fine
- Industry overlap: Five companies should have at most minimal product line overlap

**Category B: Mid-Size Growth Companies**
- Characteristics: Good management teams, annual revenue $15M–$100M, solid industry position
- Minimum: At least 2 such companies per Category A company
- Allocation: 8–10% each (10 companies if only investing in Category B)

**Examples**:
- **P.R. Mallory & Co.**: Revenue grew 4× over 10 years to $78M (1955). Stock value increased ~5× from 1946–1956.
- **Beryllium Corporation**: Revenue increased ~6× to $16M (1957). P/E ratio often reached 20×.
- **Ampex Corporation (1953)**: Category C → Category B transition. Stock rose 700% in 4 years, reaching $107 (35× increase in 6 years). A $10,000 investment in 1953 would be worth over $350,000.

**Category C: High-Risk, High-Potential Small Companies**
- Never invest money you can't afford to lose
- First-time investment in each company should not exceed 5% of total capital

**The C-to-B Transition**:
High-risk small companies often evolve into mid-size growth companies if they succeed. Their stock prices rise, market position grows, and management depth increases. At this point, they can represent a larger percentage of the portfolio without violating diversification principles.

**The Over-Diversification Trap**:
A long securities list usually indicates someone who lacks confidence in their own judgment, not a smart investor. If you hold too many stocks to track management developments at all companies, you'll do worse than if you held too few.

**The Rule**: Diversify appropriately to avoid occasional errors wiping you out. But beyond that, only buy the best stocks you can find. More is not necessarily better.

### Don't #7: Don't Be Afraid to Buy Stocks Under the Shadow of War

**The Problem**:
Every time international tension arises and war threatens, stocks drop sharply. Investors sell, fearing property destruction, high taxes, and government intervention.

**The Historical Evidence**:
Throughout the 20th century, with only one exception, every time a large-scale war broke out, the U.S. stock market dropped sharply. After all actual combat ended, most stock prices were far higher than before the war.

**What Investors Miss**:
They forget that stock prices are quoted in money. Modern war always causes government spending to exceed tax revenue, causing the money supply to increase dramatically. Each dollar becomes worth less — classic inflation. Stocks are a hedge against inflation; cash and bonds are not.

**The Rule**: If you've decided to buy certain stocks but war fears cause prices to drop, don't be afraid — buy. But buy slowly at first (when it's only the threat of war), and accelerate once war actually occurs.

**The Caveat**: Whether stocks become more valuable in real terms during war depends on the specific war and specific stocks. If the U.S. is defeated, both currency and stocks become worthless — no investment strategy helps then. But for wars the U.S. wins or stalemates, stocks in nominal terms always rise.

### Don't #8: Don't Forget Your Gilbert and Sullivan (Don't Emphasize Irrelevant Statistics)

*"Flowers that bloom in the spring — what has that got to do with the case?"*

**The Problem**:
Many investors obsess over past stock prices and earnings records — data that has little relevance to future prospects.

**Why Past Prices Are Irrelevant**:
A stock's price is a composite estimate of all investors' views of the company's future. If the company's fundamentals change dramatically (new management, new products, new markets), the past price has no relationship to the present value.

Four years ago, a company may have had weak management and no new products. Today, it may have excellent management and a pipeline of new products. Its fundamental value relative to other stocks may be 4× what it was four years ago. Today's price may be far below its true value — regardless of what the price was four years ago.

**Why Past Earnings Are Irrelevant**:
Observing past earnings without understanding the context is like an engine disconnected from the machine it powers — useless. Knowing that a company's earnings were 4× higher or 1/4 of today's tells you nothing about whether to buy or sell. What matters is what will happen in the *next* five years, not the past five.

**The Texas Instruments Example (1956-1959)**:
In 1956, Texas Instruments was available at $14 — 20× estimated 1956 earnings of $0.70. The historical earnings record (1952-1955: $0.39, $0.40, $0.48, $0.50) looked uninspiring. Past statistical analysis said: "Don't buy at this price."

But the 15-point analysis revealed:
- The company was a leader in semiconductors (transistors)
- Geophysics and military electronics businesses were growing
- Management was excellent at R&D and commercialization
- The past earnings were artificially low due to tax benefits from acquired loss carryforwards

Within 12 months, earnings grew to $1.10 (34% growth), and the stock price rose 100%. By 1958, earnings were $1.84. By 1959, over $3.50. The stock price rose from $14 to over $96 — a 7× increase.

**The Lesson**: Past earnings and price ranges are useful as supplementary tools (for understanding cyclicality and historical P/E ratios), but they are not the primary factor in deciding whether to buy a stock. What drives stock prices is the future, not the past.

### Don't #9: Don't Forget Timing Factors When Buying Excellent Growth Stocks

**The Problem**:
You've found an excellent company using the 15 points. About a year from now, profitability will improve significantly, but the financial community doesn't yet fully understand this. The stock should be worth $75 in five years. But currently, it trades at $32 — 60% above the "normal" $20 price.

Do you buy at $32, or wait for it to drop to $20?

**The Wrong Approach**: Watch the price and buy only if it drops to $20. This is the "buy at a specific price" approach.

**The Better Approach**: Buy at a specific *time*, not a specific *price*. Study the company's past successful operating plans and identify when they typically reach critical milestones (like a pilot plant starting up). Buy just before those milestones — even if the price is $32.

**Why This Works**:
- The company's future value is about to increase dramatically (from $20 to $75).
- If you buy at $32 and the stock temporarily drops to $20, you're still buying near the bottom — because the bottom will be much higher than $20 once the new value is recognized.
- If you buy at $32 and the stock eventually reaches $75, you've made 134%. If you waited for $20 and it never dropped, you've missed 275%.
- The key is: at a specific *date* (when milestones are about to be reached), the stock is likely near its lowest point *relative to its new, higher value*.

**The Rule**: When buying any stock, try to buy near the lowest price — but understand that "lowest" means lowest relative to *future* value, not lowest relative to *past* prices. Sometimes the best time to buy is at a specific date, not a specific price.

### Don't #10: Don't Follow the Crowd

**The Problem**:
Stock prices are influenced not just by real events (earnings, management changes, new products) but by psychological factors — changes in how the financial community *interprets* the same facts. The stock market has fashions and fads, like fashion.

**The 1948 Dow Chemical Example**:
In 1948, Dow Chemical was about to report record earnings. An experienced investment expert (soon to become president of the New York Society of Security Analysts) dismissed the earnings as a historical curiosity — a temporary post-war boom that wouldn't last. He ignored the new products Dow was developing.

**What Actually Happened**: Dow's earnings never fell from that "abnormally high" peak. The stock price continued to rise many times over. The expert's reasoning was based on a collective illusion — the widespread belief that a severe post-war depression was inevitable (as had happened after the Civil War and WWI).

**The 1948-1949 Collective Illusion**:
During 1947-1949, almost the entire financial community was immersed in a collective illusion: a severe depression and stock market crash were inevitable. This was like the crew of the Santa Maria fearing they'd fall off the edge of the earth. When the expected depression didn't materialize (1949 had only a mild recession), the psychological factor reversed, and many stocks doubled in price — not because fundamentals changed, but because the market's *interpretation* of the same facts changed.

**Industry Fashions**:
The financial community's preferences shift between industries:
- **Defense stocks**: Once considered unattractive (one customer = government, low profit margins, high risk). Later, re-evaluated as recession-proof (defense spending would remain high for years). Same facts, different interpretation.
- **Pharmaceuticals vs. Chemicals (1950 vs. 1958)**: In 1950, both had similarly high P/E ratios. When one pharma product had problems, the entire industry was re-evaluated downward. In 1958, the opposite happened — pharma was seen as recession-resistant, chemicals as over-expanded. Same facts, different interpretation.
- **Machinery manufacturers**: Once considered highly cyclical. A new theory emerged that long-term capital planning had eliminated the cycle. The P/E ratios rose. Then the 1957 recession shattered that theory.

**The Rule**: Think independently. When the financial community's view of an industry or company is far more negative than the facts warrant, not following the crowd can yield extraordinary rewards. Conversely, when the crowd is overly enthusiastic, be especially careful.

**The Practical Approach**:
1. Analyze the facts objectively.
2. Determine what the financial community currently believes about the industry/company.
3. If the community's view is far more negative than the facts warrant → buy (don't follow the crowd).
4. If the community's view is far more positive than the facts warrant → avoid or sell.
5. Remember: the financial community is usually slow to recognize fundamental changes — unless a famous person or sensational event forces attention.

## Key Concepts

- **Over-diversification**: Putting eggs in too many baskets means you can't watch them all. Own 5-10 stocks you truly understand, not 25+ stocks you barely know.
- **Category A/B/C stocks**: Large established growth (A), mid-size growth (B), high-risk small (C). Different allocation rules for each.
- **War and inflation**: Modern war always causes inflation. Stocks are a hedge; cash and bonds are not. Buy during war fears.
- **Gilbert and Sullivan principle**: Past prices and earnings have little relevance to future prospects. Focus on what will happen next, not what happened before.
- **Timing vs. price**: Buy at a specific *time* (when milestones are about to be reached), not at a specific *price*. The future value is what matters.
- **Crowd psychology**: Stock prices are driven not just by facts but by how the financial community *interprets* facts. When the crowd is wrong, independent thinking yields extraordinary rewards.

## Mental Models

- **Use the "machine gun" analogy for diversification**: Five or six supports stabilize a rifle, but ten aren't significantly better. Similarly, 5-10 excellent stocks provide sufficient diversification.
- **Apply the "C-to-B transition" test**: High-risk small companies can evolve into mid-size growth companies. When this happens, they deserve a larger portfolio allocation.
- **Use the "Gilbert and Sullivan" filter**: Ask: "Does this past data have any relevance to the future?" If the company's fundamentals have changed, the answer is no.
- **Apply the "specific date vs. specific price" framework**: When you know a company's value is about to increase, buy before the milestone — even at a "high" price. The price will look cheap in retrospect.
- **Use the "crowd interpretation" test**: Ask: "Is the financial community's view of this stock far more negative (or positive) than the facts warrant?" If yes, think independently.

## Anti-patterns

- **Over-diversifying into mediocrity**: Buying 25+ stocks just to "diversify" means you own many stocks you don't understand. Quality beats quantity.
- **Selling stocks during war fears**: War always causes inflation. Stocks are a hedge. Don't sell during war fears — buy.
- **Obsessing over past price history**: Past prices have no relationship to future value if fundamentals have changed. Focus on the future, not the past.
- **Waiting for a "good price" instead of a "good time"**: If a company's value is about to increase dramatically, the current "high" price will look cheap in retrospect.
- **Following the crowd**: The financial community is often wrong — especially at turning points. Think independently, analyze facts objectively, and act contrary to the crowd when the crowd is far from the truth.

## Worked Example

**The Texas Instruments Story (1956-1959)**:
In 1956, Texas Instruments was available at $14 — 20× estimated earnings. Past earnings (1952-1955: $0.39-$0.50) looked flat. Broker reports cited historical statistics and concluded the stock was overvalued. Internal selling by executives (for diversification and estate tax reasons) added to the pessimism.

But the 15-point analysis revealed a different story:
- Semiconductors (transistors) were a high-growth business
- Geophysics and military electronics were growing rapidly
- Management was excellent at R&D and commercialization
- Past earnings were artificially low due to tax benefits

Within 12 months, earnings grew 34% to $1.10, and the stock doubled. By 1958, earnings were $1.84. By 1959, over $3.50. The stock price rose from $14 to over $96 — a 7× increase.

**The Lesson**: Past earnings and price ranges are supplementary tools, not the primary factor. What drives stock prices is the future, not the past. Those who relied on historical statistics missed one of the greatest growth stocks of the era.

**The Dow Chemical Collective Illusion (1948-1949)**:
In 1948, the financial community believed a severe post-war depression was inevitable. Dow Chemical was about to report record earnings, but the market dismissed them as temporary. The stock was undervalued.

When the expected depression didn't materialize (1949 had only a mild recession), the collective illusion shattered. The psychological factor reversed, and stocks doubled — not because fundamentals changed, but because the market's *interpretation* changed.

**The Lesson**: When the crowd is immersed in a collective illusion, independent thinking yields extraordinary rewards. The key is to analyze facts objectively and recognize when the crowd's interpretation diverges from reality.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Don't over-diversify** — own 5-10 stocks you truly understand, organized into Categories A (large growth), B (mid-size growth), and C (high-risk small). Quality beats quantity.
2. **Don't fear buying during wars** — war always causes inflation, and stocks are a hedge. Buy slowly during threats, accelerate during actual war.
3. **Don't emphasize irrelevant statistics** — past prices and earnings have little relevance to future prospects. Focus on what will happen next, not what happened before. The Texas Instruments example proves this.
4. **Don't forget timing factors** — buy at a specific *time* (when milestones are about to be reached), not at a specific *price*. The future value is what matters.
5. **Don't follow the crowd** — the financial community is often wrong at turning points. Think independently, analyze facts objectively, and act contrary to the crowd when the crowd is far from the truth.
6. **The C-to-B transition** — high-risk small companies can evolve into mid-size growth companies. When this happens, they deserve a larger portfolio allocation.
