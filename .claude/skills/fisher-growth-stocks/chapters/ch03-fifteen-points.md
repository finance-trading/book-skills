# Chapter 3: 找到好股票的15个要点 (15 Points to Finding a Great Stock)

## Core Idea
Fisher's legendary 15-point checklist for identifying exceptional growth stocks — a framework that goes far beyond financial ratios to evaluate management quality, R&D effectiveness, sales strength, labor relations, and integrity. A company need not score perfectly on all 15, but should excel on most.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The 15 Points (15个要点)

**Business & Market Potential:**
- **Point 1 — Market potential**: Does the company have products/services with sufficient market potential for a sizable sales increase for at least several years? Look for multi-year revenue curves, not one-time profits. Distinguish between "lucky and able" (Alcoa) and "able and therefore lucky" (DuPont) — both types can deliver, but management quality must be sustained.

- **Point 2 — R&D for new growth**: Is management determined to continue developing products/processes to increase total sales further? The key is management's *attitude* toward growth after current products saturate — not just current R&D spending.

- **Point 3 — R&D effectiveness relative to size**: How effective are R&D efforts relative to company size? Don't be fooled by raw R&D spending numbers — accounting treatments vary wildly. What matters is: output per R&D dollar. Use scuttlebutt with researchers at the company, competitors, universities, and government to assess real productivity.

**Operational Excellence:**
- **Point 4 — Superior sales organization**: Does the company have an above-average sales organization? Sales efficiency is hard to quantify with ratios but is easy to assess through scuttlebutt — ask customers and competitors.

- **Point 5 (listed as 6 in text) — Profit margin maintenance**: What is the company doing to maintain or improve profit margins? Companies that simply raise prices will eventually be undercut by competitors. The best companies use creative cost-reduction: new equipment, process improvements, better logistics, detailed cost accounting.

- **Point 6 (listed as 7) — Labor and personnel relations**: Are labor and personnel relations good? Look beyond strikes — watch employee turnover rates, applicant volumes, wage levels relative to local norms, and how management treats rank-and-file workers. Good labor relations ≠ no strikes.

- **Point 7 (listed as 8) — Executive relations**: Are executive relations good? Watch for: promotions based on merit (not family connections), competitive executive compensation, willingness to hire outsiders only when internal candidates are unavailable, and a collaborative rather than factional atmosphere.

- **Point 8 (listed as 9) — Management depth**: Does the company have depth to its management? Critical when a company reaches ~$15-40M in revenue. Key test: Is real authority delegated? Do top executives avoid micromanaging? Is there a culture of welcoming suggestions from below?

- **Point 9 (listed as 10) — Cost analysis and accounting controls**: How good are the company's cost analysis and accounting controls? Without accurate cost breakdowns by product and operation, management cannot price correctly, promote effectively, or identify profit-draining activities.

**Competitive Advantage:**
- **Point 10 (listed as 11) — Unique competitive edge**: Does the company have something not found in competitors that gives investors important clues about relative outstandingness? This varies by industry — retail (real estate skill), manufacturing (insurance cost ratios, patent position). But patents alone are rarely sufficient — sustained engineering leadership matters more.

- **Point 11 (listed as 12) — Short-range vs. long-range outlook**: Does the company have a short-range or long-range outlook on profits? Companies that squeeze suppliers/customers for maximum short-term profit will lose long-term advantages. Scuttlebutt reveals which companies help customers/suppliers in tough times — those build enduring relationships.

**Financial Integrity:**
- **Point 12 (listed as 13) — Stock dilution from financing**: Will the company need to issue substantial new stock in the foreseeable future, such that existing holders' interests will be significantly diluted? A company strong on the other 14 points can borrow to its debt ceiling. If that's insufficient, future equity issuance will be at much higher prices, so dilution is minimal. The real danger: a company with mediocre prospects that needs constant equity financing.

**Management Quality & Integrity:**
- **Point 13 (listed as 14) — Management candor**: Does management talk freely to investors about its affairs when things are going well but "clam up" when problems or disappointments occur? Even the best-run companies face unexpected setbacks and R&D failures. Management that hides bad news either lacks solutions, is panicking, or doesn't feel accountable to shareholders.

- **Point 14 (listed as 15) — Management integrity**: Does the company's management have unquestionable integrity? Methods of self-dealing: excessive salaries, selling property to the company at inflated prices, using affiliated brokers for unnecessary fees, abusing stock option programs. A single doubt about integrity should disqualify any company — regardless of how it scores on other points.

## Key Concepts

- **"Lucky and able" vs. "able and therefore lucky"** (幸运所以能干 vs 能干所以幸运): Two types of long-term growth companies. Both require sustained management excellence, but the second type creates its own opportunities through superior execution.
- **Scuttlebutt/闲聊法**: The core research method — gathering intelligence from customers, competitors, suppliers, ex-employees, and industry experts, not just management.
- **Revenue curve/营业额曲线**: The long-term trajectory of a company's sales, measured over many years, not year-to-year.
- **Profit margin maintenance vs. price increases**: Creative cost reduction (new equipment, logistics, cost accounting) beats simply raising prices, which competitors will eventually undercut.
- **Management depth**: The ability to delegate authority and develop talent below the top — critical when companies scale beyond a certain size.
- **Long-range outlook**: Willingness to sacrifice short-term profits for long-term relationships with customers, suppliers, and employees.
- **Management candor**: Transparency about problems, not just successes — a key indicator of integrity.

## Mental Models

- **Use the 15 points as a checklist, not a formula**: Score each point qualitatively. A company failing on just a few points but strong on most can still be excellent. But failure on integrity (Point 14/15) is disqualifying — no amount of excellence elsewhere compensates.
- **Think of scuttlebutt as an art, not a science**: The best questions arise spontaneously from previous answers. Prepare a framework, but let the conversation lead you.
- **Evaluate revenue trends over multi-year periods**: Single-year comparisons are distorted by business cycles and one-time events. Look for sustained multi-year growth curves.

## Anti-patterns

- **Relying solely on financial ratios**: P/E ratios, balance sheet analysis, and statistical screens miss the qualitative factors (management quality, R&D effectiveness, sales strength) that drive long-term returns. The "scholar in a study" image of the investor is dangerous.
- **Using R&D spending as a proxy for R&D effectiveness**: Raw spending numbers are misleading — accounting treatments vary, and what matters is output per dollar, not input.
- **Assuming good labor relations = no strikes**: A company with no strikes may simply have intimidated workers. Look at turnover rates, applicant volumes, and how management treats employees day-to-day.
- **Overvaluing patents as a competitive moat**: Patents expire and can often be designed around. Sustained engineering leadership and customer relationships are far more durable.
- **Management that only shares good news**: Companies that "clam up" during tough times are hiding problems — either they lack solutions, are panicking, or don't respect shareholders.
- **Short-term profit maximization at the expense of relationships**: Squeezing suppliers or customers for maximum short-term profit destroys the goodwill that sustains long-term growth.

## Worked Example

**Motorola as a "lucky and able" company**: Fisher describes how Motorola started as a two-way radio company for police cars and taxis. When the TV market saturated in the 1950s, the company didn't stagnate — it leveraged its electronics expertise into semiconductors (transistors), high-fidelity audio, industrial electronics, and military communications. By the early 1960s, these new product lines exceeded TV revenue. Fisher's initial analysis (using the 15 points) identified Motorola's management as capable of navigating technological change. The stock went from $45.50 to $122 during the period he describes — a demonstration of how the 15-point framework identifies companies that can create new revenue curves when old ones plateau.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The 15 points cover business, operations, competition, finance, and management integrity** — a comprehensive framework for growth stock evaluation.
2. **Scuttlebutt is the key research method** for most of the 15 points — ratios and financial statements alone are insufficient.
3. **Integrity is non-negotiable** — any doubt about management's honesty toward shareholders should disqualify a company, regardless of other strengths.
4. **Long-term revenue growth potential** (Point 1) and **management's determination to create new growth** (Point 2) are the foundation — without these, no amount of operational excellence matters.
5. **Management depth and delegation** (Point 8) become critical as companies scale — micromanaging founders cannot sustain growth indefinitely.
6. **Profit margin maintenance through creative cost reduction** beats price increases — the latter are temporary, the former are durable.
7. **Transparency in bad times** (Point 13) is a stronger signal of integrity than eloquence in good times.
