# Part 3 Chapter 1: 哲学的起源 (Philosophy Origins)

## Core Idea
Investment philosophy doesn't develop in a day — it is forged through decades of experience, mistakes, and direct observation of companies. Fisher's core philosophy: invest in a very small number of companies with exceptional management, above-industry growth rates, and relatively small risk. The journey from a child's curiosity about stocks to a mature investment philosophy spans childhood wonder, Stanford education, a humbling first job, the 1929 crash, and the Depression.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Fisher & Co.'s Investment Purpose
**The Mission Statement**: Invest in a very small number of companies — having management of the highest quality, with the best long-term growth prospects, at the lowest possible risk.

**Three Management Requirements**:
1. **Willingness to sacrifice short-term profits for long-term gains** — Management must be willing to invest in new products, technologies, and capabilities even when it depresses current quarter earnings
2. **Excellence in daily operations** — The company must be run efficiently every day, not just in grand strategic moments
3. **Ability to acknowledge and correct mistakes** — No management is perfect; what matters is whether they can admit errors and fix them quickly

### The Boris Emmett Lesson: Sales Capability
At Stanford (1927-28), Boris Emmett's course visited Bay Area factories. The key lesson from observing two adjacent factories — John Bean (pump company) and Anderson-Barngrover (canning equipment) — both global leaders in their niches:

- **Sales capability is essential**: A company must be able to assess customer needs and desires, then translate those into products
- **Master all marketing concepts**: Not just making good products, but understanding and serving the complete customer relationship
- **Never visit a company that won't discuss management openly**: Transparency with outsiders is a proxy for internal management quality

### The First Lesson: Written Records Are Insufficient
At the San Francisco bank (first job as "statistician"), Fisher discovered:
- Securities analysis was superficial — analysts just copied from Moody's and Standard Statistics
- He wrote a report on radio stocks that correctly identified problems before they appeared in financial statements
- **First lesson**: Written financial records are insufficient for investment analysis. You must get information from people directly involved with the company — customers, competitors, suppliers, employees

### The 1929 Crash: The Low P/E Trap
After the crash, Fisher bought three "cheap" stocks at low P/E ratios:
- A locomotive company
- A billboard company
- A taxi company

All were devastated. The lesson:
- **Low P/E itself is not a bargain** — it may be a warning signal that the business is declining
- **True value = price relative to FUTURE earnings**, not current earnings
- A stock trading at 5x earnings that will halve next year is more expensive than a stock at 20x earnings that will double

### Starting in the Depression
January 1931 — Fisher started his own investment business at the very bottom of the Depression:
- Office with no windows, $25/month rent
- Net profit in 1932: $2.99/month
- This humble beginning forged the discipline and independence that shaped his entire career

## Key Concepts

- **Fisher & Co.'s purpose**: Invest in very few companies with exceptional management, above-industry growth, and relatively small risk — not diversification but concentration in the best ideas
- **Management's three tests**: Willingness to sacrifice short-term for long-term; excellence in daily operations; ability to acknowledge and correct mistakes
- **Sales capability**: The company must understand customer needs deeply, not just make good products. Master all marketing concepts
- **The information hierarchy**: People who work at the company > written financial records. Scuttlebutt (direct intelligence) beats published data
- **Low P/E trap**: Low P/E is not automatically cheap. It may signal declining fundamentals. True value depends on future earnings, not current ones
- **The Depression lesson**: Starting a business at the absolute bottom ($2.99/month) teaches that patience and discipline matter more than timing

## Mental Models

- **Use the "three management tests"**: When evaluating any company, ask: (1) Does management sacrifice short-term profits for long-term gains? (2) Is daily operations excellence maintained? (3) Do they acknowledge and correct mistakes openly?
- **Apply the "information source" test**: Have you talked to people directly involved with the company — customers, competitors, suppliers, former employees? If your analysis rests solely on published financial statements, it is incomplete
- **Use the "future earnings" lens**: Don't ask "is this cheap relative to current earnings?" Ask "is this cheap relative to what this company will earn in 3-5 years?"
- **Apply the "grandmother test" for curiosity**: Fisher's lifelong interest began with his grandmother's conversation about stocks. What sparks your genuine curiosity about a business? Follow that thread

## Anti-patterns

- **Relying solely on financial statements**: Moody's, annual reports, and statistics are starting points, not conclusions. The real information lives in conversations with people who know the business
- **Buying low P/E stocks automatically**: A low P/E may signal declining fundamentals, not a bargain. Always ask: will earnings grow or shrink?
- **Copying other analysts' work**: Superficial analysis that repackages publicly available data adds no value. Independent, ground-level research is what creates an edge
- **Waiting for perfect conditions to start**: Fisher started his firm at the Depression's absolute bottom with $2.99/month income. Waiting for ideal circumstances means never starting
- **Confusing current earnings with value**: The market prices stocks based on future expectations. A company with $1 EPS declining to $0.50 is worth less than a company with $1 EPS growing to $3, regardless of current P/E

## Worked Example

**The Radio Stocks Report**:
While working as a "statistician" at the San Francisco bank, Fisher noticed the booming radio stock market of 1928-29. Instead of copying data from Moody's like other analysts, he investigated directly:
- He talked to people in the radio industry
- He visited companies and assessed their actual operations
- He wrote a report identifying fundamental problems in the radio stock sector
- His analysis proved correct when radio stocks subsequently crashed

**The Lesson**: The written records (Moody's, Standard Statistics) showed radio companies as profitable and growing. But direct investigation revealed problems that financial statements hadn't yet captured. This became Fisher's foundational insight: **the best investment information comes from people directly involved with the company, not from published data**. This principle — the basis of what would become the "scuttlebutt" method — was born from this first professional experience.

**The 1929 "Cheap Stock" Mistake**:
After the crash, Fisher bought three stocks because they seemed cheap:
- Locomotive company: low P/E, but the industry was declining as railroads shifted to diesel
- Billboard company: low P/E, but outdoor advertising was losing share to radio
- Taxi company: low P/E, but the business model was under pressure

All three were devastated. The low P/E wasn't a bargain — it was a warning. Fisher learned: **price relative to current earnings is meaningless if earnings are about to decline. Value must be assessed relative to future earnings potential**.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Investment philosophy takes decades to develop**: Fisher's philosophy wasn't formed in a classroom or from a book — it evolved through 40+ years of direct experience, mistakes, and observation
2. **Management quality is paramount**: The three tests — sacrifice short-term for long-term, daily excellence, acknowledge mistakes — are the foundation of company selection
3. **Direct intelligence beats published data**: The scuttlebutt method's roots lie in Fisher's first job discovery that financial statements lag reality
4. **Low P/E is not a value strategy**: True value depends on future earnings trajectory, not current earnings levels. Cheap stocks are often cheap for good reason
5. **Sales capability is essential**: A company must not just make good products but deeply understand and serve customer needs
6. **The Depression forged discipline**: Starting at the absolute bottom with almost no income taught that patience and independence matter more than market timing
7. **Transparency as a quality signal**: If a company won't discuss its management openly with outsiders, that's a red flag about internal culture
