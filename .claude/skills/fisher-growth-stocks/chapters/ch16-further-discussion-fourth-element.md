# Part 2 Chapter 5: 再论第四个要素 — 行业评价 (Further Discussion of the Fourth Element: Industry Appraisal)

## Core Idea
The financial community's appraisal of any individual stock is not monolithic — it's actually a composite of three different appraisals: (1) the overall attractiveness of common stocks as an investment class, (2) the appraisal of the company's specific industry, and (3) the appraisal of the company itself. Understanding how industry appraisal works — and how it can change dramatically even when fundamentals remain constant — is essential for conservative investors.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Three Layers of Appraisal

**The Composite Nature of Appraisal**: The final appraisal of any stock is woven from three distinct appraisals:
1. **Overall market appraisal**: The financial community's current view of the general attractiveness of common stocks as an investment
2. **Industry appraisal**: The financial community's view of the company's specific industry
3. **Company appraisal**: The financial community's view of the company itself

**The Key Insight**: Each layer can change independently, and all three interact to determine the final P/E ratio. A company with excellent fundamentals can still see its stock price decline if industry appraisal turns negative, even if company-specific appraisal remains positive.

### Industry Appraisal: The Chemical Industry Example (1930s-1970s)

**The High-Appraisal Period (1930s-1960)**:
From the depths of the Great Depression until the mid-1950s to early 1960s, the largest American chemical companies enjoyed very high P/E ratios compared to most other stocks.

The financial community's view of the chemical industry could be seen in a cartoon depicting the industry as an endless conveyor belt: on one side, scientists were creating new compounds in test tubes; through mysterious and hard-to-replicate factories, these materials emerged on the other side as wonderful new products — nylon, DDT, synthetic rubber, quick-drying paint, and countless other new materials. For lucky manufacturers, these seemed to be an ever-increasing source of wealth.

**The Low-Appraisal Period (1960s-1972)**:
By the 1960s, this impression changed. In investors' eyes, the chemical industry became very similar to steel, cement, and paper industries — selling commodities based on technical specifications, where Company A's product was essentially identical to Company B's. Capital-intensive industries typically face enormous pressure to run at high equipment utilization rates to amortize large fixed investments. This often leads to fierce price competition and thin profit margins.

After this impression change, during the ten years ending in 1972, major chemical companies' P/E ratios relative to the overall stock market were far lower than before. Chemical stock P/E ratios, while still significantly higher than many industries, began looking more and more like steel, paper, and cement.

**The Fundamental Reality**:
What was exciting about all these changes was that, except for one important example, the industry's fundamental background in the 1960s was virtually identical to — or not different at all from — the preceding 30 years.

Yes, in the latter five years of the 1960s, certain products experienced severe overcapacity (like most synthetic textile products). Some leading chemical companies' earnings were temporarily hit hard, particularly DuPont. But the industry's fundamental characteristics didn't change enough to justify its altered status in the financial community's mind.

Chemical production had always been capital-intensive. Most products were sold according to technical specifications, so Company A could rarely raise prices above Company B's. On the other hand, thanks to a flood of greatly improved new products — insecticides, packaging materials, textiles, pharmaceuticals, and countless others — the industry's market continued expanding in the 1960s and 1970s. Opportunities seemed endless, because intelligent humans could rearrange molecules to create products that don't exist in nature, with special properties that better meet human needs, or are cheaper than previously used natural materials.

**The Unchanging Opportunity**:
Finally, whether during the period when chemical stocks were most highly regarded, or during the more recent period of lower status, another factor remained largely unchanged. Older and larger-volume chemical products are "first-step" processing of basic materials derived from coal or ammonia compounds as molecular sources. Inevitably, these first-step products must be sold to specifications, and prices must be very competitive. But alert companies always have the opportunity to process these first-step products into far more complex and much higher-priced products. These products are, at least for a time, their own unique products, exempt from fierce competition. By the time these products must again be price-competitive, alert companies can still continuously find newer products to add to their high-margin product lines.

**The Crucial Point**: In other words, when chemical stocks were the financial community's favorites, all favorable factors still existed when chemical stocks later fell from favor. But the 1960s emphasized unfavorable factors that people previously ignored. What changed was the emphasis, not the facts.

### The 1973 Reappraisal

**The New Reality**: Around mid-1973, chemical stocks regained the financial community's favor. A new view of the industry began to predominate. For the first time in modern history, major industrialized nations experienced (on a large scale, except during wars) the pain of economic resource scarcity, with manufacturing capacity growing only slowly. Therefore, fierce price competition might not reoccur for several years.

This impression opened an entirely new situation for chemical stock investors. The investors' question now: Can the underlying facts truly support the new impression? If yes, from the new situation's perspective, has the chemical stocks' rise relative to the overall market gone too far, or is there still room?

### Computer Peripheral Stocks (1969-1972)

**The High-Appraisal Period (1969)**:
Recent financial history provides examples of P/E ratio changes far exceeding the chemical industry's, because the financial community's appraisal of the industry's background facts changed dramatically — yet the industry itself was virtually unchanged from before.

In 1969, computer peripheral stocks were highly favored by the market. These companies produced various special equipment that could be attached to central processing units or mainframe computers to enhance users' benefits from the central unit. High-speed printers, expanded memory, disk pack devices were among the main products. With them, there was no need for keypunch operators to input data into computers.

The general impression at the time was that these companies' futures were limitless. Although central computers themselves were largely developed, and the market was dominated by a few powerful, firmly entrenched companies, small independent companies could still sell these peripheral products cheaper than the big companies. But small companies' products were usually leased rather than sold outright, and large mainframe manufacturers were determined to enter the "piggyback" product market on their equipment.

**The Low-Appraisal Period (1972)**:
Today investors have a new view, discovering that small companies are under financial pressure.

**The Key Question**: Did fundamentals change, or did the appraisal of fundamentals change?

**The Answer**: An extreme example of appraisal change is the financial community's vastly different perception of franchise businesses and franchise stocks in 1969 versus 1972. Similarly, for computer peripheral stocks, all the industry's problems already existed when people bought these stocks at very high P/E ratios. It's just that the general impression at the time was that companies with outstanding current performance would continue growing without interruption, so the problems were ignored.

### The A.C. Nielsen Example (1958)

**The Challenge of New Industries**:
Holding such an impression of an industry, the investor's question remains unchanged: Is the currently predominant appraisal more favorable, more unfavorable, or roughly the same compared to basic economic facts? Sometimes this question is very difficult even for the most sophisticated investors.

December 1958 provides such an example. At that time, the traditionally conservative investment bank Smith, Barney & Co. took an action that seems unremarkable today but was different then: publicly offering A.C. Nielsen Company's stock.

This company had no factories, no tangible products, therefore no inventory. It was engaged in the "service industry," charging fees to provide market research information to clients.

**The Traditional View**:
True, in 1958, banks and insurance companies had long been highly regarded and seen by the market as excellent conservative investment objects. But these industries are hard to compare with each other. A bank or insurance company's book value is cash, liquid investments, or receivables, so investors buying bank or insurance stocks seemed to have solid value to rely on. But this newly introduced service company to the financial community had no such things.

**The Fundamental Reality**:
However, careful study of Nielsen's situation revealed its fundamentals were surprisingly good. Management was honest and capable, the company's competitive position was strong and unique, and further growth was expected for many years to come.

**The Market's Reaction**:
However, before evidence showed how the financial community would first react to such an industry, it still seemed wise to hold off on buying. Would it take several years for the market to make a practical appraisal of such a company's investment value, dispelling doubts that might arise from lacking familiar value metrics?

For a company like Nielsen, many years of high P/E ratios indicated the financial community gave it very high appraisal. But some people, deciding to accept the acknowledged fundamentals and buy these stocks, did so with trepidation — like jumping off a cliff to see if the air below could support us.

**The Lesson**:
Such behavior seems absurd and laughable today, but at the time, the concept of service companies was still novel, different from the concepts we had become accustomed to. In fact, within a few years, the pendulum swung to the other side. As Nielsen's earnings grew and grew again, a new concept arose on Wall Street. Many companies were indiscriminately lumped together, forming one of the financial community's impressions, viewed as extremely attractive service stocks. These companies were all engaged in services, not producing goods, but their economic fundamentals were worlds apart. Some companies' P/E ratios began rising above appropriate levels. As always, fundamentals would eventually dominate everything. The false impression formed by grouping fundamentally different companies into one category slowly faded.

**The Crucial Point**: When conservative investors are about to buy a stock, they must understand the nature of the financial community's current appraisal of the company's industry. They must tirelessly research and investigate whether this appraisal is far more favorable or unfavorable than fundamental facts warrant. Only by correctly judging this point can they be quite certain about one of the three variables that determine the long-term trend of the industry's stock market prices.

## Key Concepts

- **Three layers of appraisal**: Overall market + industry + company. Each can change independently. All three interact to determine final P/E ratios.
- **Industry appraisal can change dramatically while fundamentals remain constant**: The chemical industry example shows how the financial community's emphasis can shift from favorable to unfavorable factors — even when the underlying facts haven't changed.
- **The emphasis vs. facts distinction**: What changes is often not the facts themselves, but which facts the financial community emphasizes. This creates opportunities for investors who recognize the divergence.
- **The service company challenge**: New types of businesses (like service companies in 1958) lack familiar value metrics (tangible assets, book value). The financial community's initial reaction may be skepticism, even when fundamentals are strong.
- **The pendulum effect**: Industry appraisal can swing from extreme favor to extreme disfavor. The A.C. Nielsen example shows how service stocks went from skepticism to indiscriminate enthusiasm to sober reassessment.
- **The investor's task**: Understand whether current industry appraisal is more favorable, more unfavorable, or roughly the same compared to basic economic facts. This is essential for judging long-term stock price trends.

## Mental Models

- **Use the "three layers" framework**: When evaluating any stock, decompose its P/E ratio into three components: overall market sentiment, industry appraisal, and company-specific appraisal. Each can change independently.
- **Apply the "emphasis vs. facts" test**: Ask: "Has the industry's fundamental situation actually changed, or has the financial community simply started emphasizing different aspects?" If facts haven't changed but emphasis has, the appraisal may be due for correction.
- **Use the "pendulum" lens**: Industry appraisal tends to swing between extremes. When an industry is highly favored, be skeptical. When it's deeply disfavored, look for opportunities — especially if fundamentals remain strong.
- **Apply the "new industry" caution**: When a new type of business enters the market (service companies, tech platforms, etc.), the financial community may struggle to appraise it initially. Don't rush in — wait for the market to develop appropriate valuation metrics.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming industry appraisal reflects fundamentals**: The financial community's view of an industry can be wildly out of sync with reality. Chemical stocks were highly favored in the 1950s, then deeply disfavored in the 1960s-70s — even though fundamentals remained largely unchanged.
- **Following industry fashions**: When the financial community enthusiastically embraces an industry (computer peripherals in 1969, franchise stocks in 1969, service stocks in the 1960s), the resulting high P/E ratios often prove unsustainable. The problems were already present; they were just ignored.
- **Rejecting new business models out of hand**: A.C. Nielsen had no tangible assets, no inventory, no factories — traditional value metrics didn't apply. But its fundamentals were excellent. Investors who rejected it because it didn't fit familiar categories missed extraordinary gains.
- **Assuming industry problems are new**: Computer peripheral stocks crashed in the 1970s, but the problems (leasing vs. selling, large manufacturer competition) existed in 1969 when P/E ratios were sky-high. The appraisal changed; the problems were always there.
- **Ignoring the three-layer interaction**: A company with excellent fundamentals can still see its stock decline if industry appraisal turns negative. You must understand all three layers to judge long-term prospects.

## Worked Example

**The Chemical Industry's Appraisal Cycle (1930s-1970s)**:

**Phase 1 — High Appraisal (1930s-1960)**:
The chemical industry enjoyed very high P/E ratios. The financial community viewed it through a cartoon lens: scientists creating new compounds in test tubes, mysterious factories producing wonderful new products (nylon, DDT, synthetic rubber, quick-drying paint), endless sources of wealth for lucky manufacturers.

**Phase 2 — Low Appraisal (1960s-1972)**:
The impression changed dramatically. Chemical industry now looked like steel, cement, paper — selling commodities to technical specifications, fierce price competition, thin margins, capital-intensive with high fixed costs requiring high utilization rates.

During the ten years ending in 1972, major chemical companies' P/E ratios relative to the overall market were far lower than before.

**The Fundamental Reality Check**:
Except for one important example (temporary overcapacity in synthetic textiles in the late 1960s), the industry's fundamental background in the 1960s was virtually identical to the preceding 30 years.

- Chemical production had always been capital-intensive
- Most products had always been sold to technical specifications
- Companies had always been able to create unique, high-margin products from first-step commodity chemicals
- The market had always been expanding thanks to new products (insecticides, packaging, textiles, pharmaceuticals)
- Opportunities had always seemed endless because humans could rearrange molecules to create products that don't exist in nature

**The Crucial Insight**: When chemical stocks were the financial community's favorites, all favorable factors still existed when they later fell from favor. What changed was the emphasis, not the facts.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Appraisal is three-layered**: Overall market sentiment + industry appraisal + company-specific appraisal. Each can change independently. All three interact to determine final P/E ratios.
2. **Industry appraisal can change dramatically while fundamentals remain constant**: The chemical industry example shows how emphasis can shift from favorable to unfavorable factors — even when underlying facts haven't changed.
3. **What changes is often emphasis, not facts**: Chemical stocks were highly favored, then deeply disfavored — but the favorable factors still existed when they fell from favor. The financial community simply started emphasizing different aspects.
4. **New business models face appraisal challenges**: A.C. Nielsen had no tangible assets, so traditional value metrics didn't apply. But fundamentals were excellent. Investors who rejected it because it didn't fit familiar categories missed extraordinary gains.
5. **The pendulum effect**: Industry appraisal swings between extremes. Computer peripheral stocks were wildly favored in 1969, then crashed in the 1970s — but the problems existed all along; they were just ignored.
6. **The investor's task**: Understand whether current industry appraisal is more favorable, more unfavorable, or roughly the same compared to basic economic facts. Only by correctly judging this can you be certain about one of the three variables determining long-term stock price trends.
7. **Fundamentals eventually dominate**: The false impression formed by grouping fundamentally different companies into one category eventually fades. Service companies are not all the same — their economic fundamentals can be worlds apart.
