# Part 3 Chapter 2: 从经验中学习 (Learning from Experience)

## Core Idea
Theory becomes wisdom only through painful experience. Fisher's investment philosophy was tested — and proved — through real investments: the spectacular success of Food Machinery (contrarian buy after crash), the validation of Texas Instruments and Motorola (financial community wrong, Fisher right), and the cautionary lessons of trading mistakes and nickels-and-dimes thinking. The central principle: "Others turn left, you turn right" — contrarian investing grounded in deep fundamental analysis.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The "Others Turn Left, You Turn Right" Principle
**The Contrarian Principle**: When the financial community's appraisal of a company is far below what fundamentals warrant — often because of temporary problems or market-wide fear — that is the ideal buying opportunity. The crowd's pessimism creates the price gap that generates extraordinary returns.

**Requirements for contrarian investing**:
1. Deep fundamental analysis must confirm the company's true value
2. Conviction must be strong enough to hold through continued short-term pain
3. Time horizon must be long enough for fundamentals to reassert themselves

### The Food Machinery Deep Analysis Framework
When Food Machinery crashed from IPO price $21.50 to $4-5, Fisher conducted exhaustive analysis across multiple dimensions:

**Product Line Analysis**:
- Three product lines, all global leaders in their respective niches
- Scale advantages that competitors couldn't match
- Marketing lock-in: canning equipment installed base created recurring revenue

**Creative Engineering Assessment**:
- Pear peeler, peach pitter, orange coloring machine — products that solved real customer problems
- Engineering was creative, not imitative

**Personnel Quality Assessment**:
- **John Crummey**: High efficiency combined with absolute integrity — the ideal management combination
- **Paul Davies**: Financial strength and discipline — created the fiscal infrastructure for growth

**The Investment Result**: Food Machinery bought at $4-5 (after crash) eventually rose dramatically. The contrarian bet, grounded in fundamental analysis, paid off spectacularly.

### The Three-Year Rule
**Don't evaluate investment performance in less than three years.** The first year's results are mostly luck — good or bad. Only after three years can you begin to distinguish skill from randomness.

**Application to Texas Instruments (1955)**:
- Financial community saw TI as speculative, overpriced
- Fisher bought because fundamentals were exceptional
- TI eventually became one of the greatest growth stocks of the era

**Application to Motorola (1955)**:
- Financial community saw Motorola as "just a TV maker"
- Fisher recognized its communications electronics potential
- Underperformed for over a year after purchase
- One client called it "that turkey you bought for me"
- A New York bank evaluated Motorola for an insurance company and refused to rate it — "not worth their time"
- Yet Motorola eventually outperformed every other stock in that portfolio

### The Rogers Corporation Exception
Rogers Corporation tested the three-year rule. The company had invested heavily in a single new product that was struggling. Fisher held through poor results because of confidence in president Norman Greenman's honesty and competence. Eventually:
- The company abandoned the failing product
- Other products bloomed
- The investment succeeded, but the hold was longer than three years

**The Exception Principle**: When you have deep conviction in management's integrity and honesty, you can extend beyond the three-year rule — but only if the fundamental thesis remains intact.

### The Trading Mistake: Small Gains, Large Opportunities Lost
**California Packing (Del Monte) Trading**:
Fisher made three successful round-trip trades in California Packing stock — buying low, selling high, each time making a profit. But:
- Each trade produced "small potatoes" gains
- Had he held the stock long-term instead, the gains would have dwarfed the trading profits
- **The lesson**: Trading successful companies for short-term gains is far less profitable than buying great companies and holding them

### The Nickels-and-Dimes Lesson
A client insisted on buying stock at exactly $33⅜ — not $33½, not $33⅝, exactly $33⅜. The stock rose 50%+ and never came back to that price.

**The Lesson**: Obsessing over small price differences causes you to miss the big picture. A stock that's fundamentally worth $50 is a bargain at $33⅜ AND at $34. The few cents you save by waiting are nothing compared to the dollars you miss by not owning it.

## Key Concepts

- **"Others turn left, you turn right" (别人往左走，你往右走)**: Contrarian investing based on fundamental analysis, not blind opposition to the crowd. Buy when the financial community undervalues quality
- **Three-year rule (三年法则)**: Don't evaluate investment performance in less than 3 years. First-year results are mostly luck. Patience is essential
- **Deep analysis framework**: Product lines (global leadership?), scale advantages, marketing lock-in, creative engineering, personnel quality (efficiency + integrity)
- **The contrarian holding period**: Being right about a contrarian position takes time. The crowd's pessimism can persist longer than your patience — but eventually fundamentals win
- **Trading vs. investing**: Short-term trading of great companies produces small gains. Long-term holding produces extraordinary gains. The temptation to trade is the enemy of compounding
- **Nickels-and-dimes thinking**: Obsessing over small price differences while missing the fundamental value gap. A great company at a slightly-higher price is still a bargain

## Mental Models

- **Use the "contrarian conviction" test**: When the financial community hates a stock, ask: has my fundamental analysis found something the crowd missed? If yes, the crowd's pessimism is your opportunity. If no, the crowd may be right
- **Apply the "three-year" discipline**: Commit to holding any investment for at least three years before evaluating it. If you can't hold for three years, you shouldn't buy it
- **Use the "personnel quality" framework**: When analyzing management, assess both efficiency AND integrity. One without the other is insufficient. John Crummey had both — that's rare and valuable
- **Apply the "trading temptation" check**: Before selling a stock for a quick gain, ask: "If I sell this, will I be able to buy it back at a reasonable price? Would I even want to?" If the answer is no, don't sell
- **Use the "nickels-and-dimes" test**: Are you obsessing over price while ignoring value? If a company is worth $50 and the market offers it at $33 or $34, the exact price doesn't matter

## Anti-patterns

- **Selling great companies for quick trading gains**: California Packing showed the pattern — three successful trades, each profitable, but all dwarfed by what holding would have produced. The trading "profits" were actually opportunity costs
- **Evaluating investments too quickly**: The one-year mark is when luck separates from skill. Motorola underperformed for over a year. Investors who evaluated at 12 months would have sold their best performer
- **Obsessing over exact purchase prices**: The client who demanded $33⅜ exactly missed a 50%+ gain. Perfect prices are a myth; good-enough prices on great companies produce extraordinary returns
- **Trusting the financial community's consensus**: The New York bank that refused to evaluate Motorola — and the insurance company that relied on it — missed the stock that would outperform everything else in their portfolios. Consensus opinion is often wrong about the best companies
- **Confusing honesty with competence or vice versa**: Rogers Corporation's Norman Greenman had both — honesty and competence. But Fisher learned that some managers have one without the other. You need both
- **Giving up on contrarian positions too early**: Being early is indistinguishable from being wrong in the short term. The three-year rule exists precisely because contrarian positions need time to play out

## Worked Example

**The Food Machinery Story (Contrarian Analysis in Action)**:

Food Machinery was formed by merging three companies:
1. John Bean (pumps) — global leader
2. Anderson-Barngrover (canning equipment) — global leader
3. Sprague-Sells (another canning line)

**The IPO**: Stock debuted at $21.50. Stock manipulators were active — they pumped the price initially, then dumped their shares.

**The Crash**: Stock fell to $4-5. The financial community wrote it off. Panic selling.

**Fisher's Analysis** (conducted during the crash):
- **Product lines**: Three distinct businesses, each a global leader in its niche
- **Scale advantages**: Competitors couldn't match their manufacturing efficiency
- **Marketing lock-in**: Once a food processor installed Food Machinery's canning equipment, switching costs were enormous. The installed base created recurring revenue from replacement parts and service
- **Creative engineering**: The pear peeler (mechanical pear peeling), peach pitter (mechanical peach pitting), and orange coloring machine solved real, expensive problems for food processors. These weren't me-too products
- **Personnel**: John Crummey (operations) combined high efficiency with absolute integrity. Paul Davies (finance) provided fiscal discipline and strength

**The Decision**: Fisher bought heavily during the crash, when everyone else was selling.

**The Result**: Food Machinery eventually became one of the most successful growth stocks of the era. The contrarian analysis — grounded in fundamentals, not sentiment — produced extraordinary returns.

**The Motorola Story (Patience with Contrarian Positions)**:

In 1955, Fisher bought Texas Instruments and Motorola:
- **TI**: Financial community saw it as speculative. Fisher recognized the semiconductor revolution
- **Motorola**: Financial community saw it as a TV maker. Fisher recognized its communications electronics potential

Motorola's path:
- Underperformed for over a year after purchase
- One client complained to Fisher: "that turkey you bought for me"
- An insurance company asked a New York bank to evaluate Motorola
- The bank refused: "not worth our time"
- Yet Motorola eventually outperformed every other stock in that insurance company's portfolio

**The Lesson**: Being right about a great company doesn't mean being right immediately. The financial community can be wrong for a year, two years, or longer. But eventually, fundamentals assert themselves — and when they do, the gains are extraordinary.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Contrarian investing works when grounded in fundamentals**: "Others turn left, you turn right" is not blind stubbornness — it requires exhaustive analysis to confirm the crowd is wrong
2. **The three-year rule prevents premature judgment**: First-year results are luck. Only after three years can you evaluate whether your thesis was correct
3. **Deep analysis covers five dimensions**: product lines, scale, marketing, engineering creativity, and personnel quality (efficiency + integrity)
4. **Trading great companies is the enemy of compounding**: Three successful trades of California Packing produced less than one long-term hold would have
5. **The financial community is often wrong about the best companies**: Motorola was dismissed by a major New York bank — yet became the best performer. Consensus ≠ correctness
6. **Nickels-and-dimes thinking destroys returns**: Obsessing over exact purchase prices causes you to miss great opportunities entirely
7. **Conviction in management integrity can extend the hold period**: Rogers Corporation showed that when you trust management's honesty and competence, you can hold through longer periods of poor results
