# Part 2 Chapter 6: 三论第四个要素 — 公司评价比行业评价更重要 (Third Discussion of the Fourth Element: Company Appraisal Matters More Than Industry Appraisal)

## Core Idea
When it comes to factors affecting P/E ratios, the financial community's appraisal of a company's intrinsic qualities is more important than its appraisal of the company's industry. Investors who can determine the true facts about a specific company — far better or far worse than the financial community's current impression — are very likely to know when that stock is significantly undervalued or overvalued. The chapter illustrates this with the two-stock comparison principle, the Motorola TV-sale example, and warnings about anchoring to historical price ranges.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Company Appraisal vs. Industry Appraisal

**The Fundamental Principle**: The financial community's appraisal of a company itself is more important than its appraisal of the company's industry when it comes to determining P/E ratios.

**The Implication**: The closer the financial community's appraisal of a specific stock is to the ideal investment qualities discussed in the first three elements, the higher its P/E ratio. If the appraisal falls below these standards, the P/E ratio tends to decline accordingly.

**The Opportunity**: Investors who can use their intelligence to determine the true facts about a company worthy of investment — far superior to or far inferior to the financial community's current impression — will very likely know when that stock is significantly undervalued or overvalued.

### The Two-Stock Comparison Trap

**The Simple Math Problem**: When comparing two stocks' relative attractiveness, investors often try to use overly simple mathematical methods and confuse themselves.

**The Setup**: Suppose they compare two companies that, after careful study, appear to both have future annual growth rates of about 10%. If one's price is 10x earnings and the other is 20x, the 10x stock looks cheaper.

**Why This Is Wrong**: Perhaps, but not necessarily. There are many reasons:
- The apparently cheaper company may use substantial leverage (must pay interest and preferred dividends before earnings go to common stockholders). The risk that the anticipated growth rate cannot be realized may be much higher.
- Purely from a business operations perspective, since both stocks' growth rates are merely most-likely estimates, if unexpected events occur, the impact on one stock's estimate may be far greater than on the other's.

**The Extended Example**:
Two stocks both likely to have earnings double in four years, both currently at 20x earnings. Meanwhile, other non-growth but fundamentally sound companies in the same market trade at 10x.

After four years, assume overall market P/E unchanged, fundamentally sound non-growth companies still at 10x.

**Stock A**: Four years later, future growth prospects are roughly the same as four years ago. Financial community's appraisal: this stock's earnings should double again in the next four years. This means past four years' earnings doubled, but the price will still be 20x that new earnings number — in other words, the price also doubled during that period.

**Stock B**: Four years later, earnings also doubled as expected. But now the financial community's appraisal is: future four years' earnings will be flat, though fundamentals remain sound. This means, although the four-year earnings doubling expectation has been realized, Stock B's holders will be very disappointed. Because the financial community's impression of Stock B is "no growth for the next four years," it now expects P/E of only 10x. So despite earnings having doubled, the stock price is the same as before.

**The Fundamental Investment Principle**: The higher the probability of continued earnings growth, the higher the P/E ratio investors can afford to bear.

### The "No Response" Phenomenon

**Applying the Principle with Care**: When applying this principle, investors must never forget that actual P/E ratio changes are not caused by what actually happens, but by what the financial community currently believes will happen.

**During Periods of Generally Optimistic Market Sentiment**: A stock may have extremely high P/E because the financial community accurately predicts many years of very high growth rates. But we must wait many years for that growth to fully materialize. P/E accurately reflecting high growth rates may have a period of "no response" (没有反应) — especially if the company experiences temporary setbacks in operations; even the best companies experience this.

**During Periods of Generally Pessimistic Market Sentiment**: This "no response" situation for some excellent investment objects can be quite severe. When this happens, patient investors who can distinguish the gap between current market impression and factual truth can find excellent long-term common stock investment opportunities with relatively low risk and very considerable profits.

### The Motorola TV Sale Example (March 13, 1974)

**The Setup**: On March 12, 1974, after the New York Stock Exchange closed, Motorola announced it would exit the television business, selling its American TV factories and inventory to Japanese giant Matsushita at near book value.

**The Result**: The previous day (March 12), Motorola closed at $48.75. On March 13, it closed at $60 — up about 25% in one day.

**The Surface Explanation**: Investors universally knew Motorola's TV business had small losses that dragged down other divisions' earnings. This alone would cause the stock to rise somewhat, but not as much as actually occurred.

**The Deeper Reasoning**: The main motive behind the buying came from more complex reasoning. For some time, quite a few investors believed Motorola's money-losing divisions — especially the communications division — made the company one of very few American electronics companies truly worth investing in.

**The Spencer Trask Report**: Spencer Trask and Co. published a report by securities analyst Otis Bradley that discussed Motorola's communications division investment value in great detail. The report used an unusual method — calculating only one division's current and future estimated P/E ratios, not discussing Motorola's overall earnings. The report compared this division's estimated revenue and P/E with Hewlett-Packard and Perkin-Elmer — two companies universally regarded as excellent electronics companies. From this report, one could easily infer (the article didn't explicitly state it) that Motorola's communications division was of extremely high investment quality, and its value alone was worth Motorola's then-current stock price. So at that price, buying Motorola's stock was exactly like buying the entire communications division, with all other divisions thrown in free.

**The Market's Misconception**: Given such views of some of Motorola's high-complexity product lines, what factors prompted massive buying after the TV sale to Matsushita was announced? People who had been buying Motorola stock heavily knew for a long time that many in the financial community dismissed this stock because it left the impression of being a "television manufacturing company." Most people in the financial community, upon hearing "Motorola," immediately thought of televisions, then semiconductors.

**The Standard & Poor's Misclassification**: In Standard & Poor's stock guide, the small space used to list each company's main businesses said Motorola produced "radios and televisions: semiconductors." This view wasn't exactly wrong, but it was misleading because it didn't match Motorola's actual situation and completely ignored the very important communications division — which at that time accounted for about half the entire company.

**The Appraisal Gap**: After the TV sale to Matsushita was announced, some who rushed to buy Motorola stock were无疑 just reacting to positive news expected to drive the stock up. But behind the massive buying was a reason: investors believed the financial community's appraisal of this company had always been far worse than the facts warranted. From historical records, in the television business, Motorola was seen as merely "a struggling chaser," not as good as industry leader Zenith. Since the television business would no longer obscure investors' views of the company's other divisions, a new impression would form, and the P/E ratio would rise much higher.

**The Short-Term Reversal**: Were the people who bought Motorola stock at high prices smart? Not entirely. Over the next few weeks, the stock price gave back the gains from the initial surge. When the market declines, the financial community's impression of a company changes for the worse faster than for the better. When the market rises, the opposite is true. Those who rushed to buy Motorola stock because of the Matsushita news were unfortunate because, over the next few weeks, short-term interest rates rose sharply, creating downward pressure on the overall stock market and worsening the already-pervasive bearish sentiment.

**The Lesson**: Patient investors who recognized the appraisal gap between market impression and reality were eventually vindicated — but the timing required patience through short-term volatility.

### The Danger of Anchoring to Historical Price Ranges

**The Most Subtle and Dangerous Force**: There is another force in the entire investment field that is the most subtle and most dangerous — even the most sophisticated investors must constantly guard against it.

**The Anchoring Effect**: When a stock remains in a certain price range for a long time — for example, low of $38, high of $43 — investors unconsciously begin to treat that price range as "true value." After the financial community takes that price as the stock's "value," and it becomes deeply rooted and taken for granted, if the appraisal changes and the price drops to $24, all kinds of investors will rush in to buy heavily. They conclude this stock is now really very cheap. But if fundamentals are poor, $24 may still be too high.

**The Opposite Scenario**: Conversely, if the stock rises to $30, $60, $70, selling pressure will cascade as people take profits, because many cannot resist the feeling that such prices are "too high." Yielding to such impulses can cause severe losses.

**The Fundamental Test**: To make substantial profits from stock investment, you must hold large positions in stocks that rise several multiples. The only true test of whether a stock is "cheap" or "expensive" is not whether the current price is high or low relative to past prices (no matter how accustomed we are to past prices), but whether this company's fundamentals are significantly higher or lower than the financial community's current appraisal of that stock.

### The Three Appraisal Elements Combined

**The Summary**: Besides investors' appraisals of the overall industry and specific companies, we must consider the third appraisal element. Only when all three elements are fused together can we reasonably judge whether a stock is cheap or expensive at any given point.

**The Third Appraisal — Overall Market Outlook**: The third appraisal targets the overall stock market outlook.

**The 1927-1929 "New Era" Delusion**: To observe how extreme the overall stock market's appraisal impact can be, and how far these views can diverge from facts, we should look back at two of the most extreme appraisals of this century. Today they may seem absurd and laughable, but between 1927 and 1929, the financial community largely believed we were in a "New Era." For years, the vast majority of Americans truly believed our companies' earnings would only rise, never fall. Not only had severe economic depressions become a thing of the past, but even the great engineer and entrepreneur Herbert Hoover was elected president. His outstanding abilities were expected to ensure economic prosperity. In this environment, many people felt it was almost impossible to lose money holding stocks. Many wanted to profit as much as possible from this seemingly safe proposition, so they bought on margin — acquiring far more stock than would otherwise have been possible. Factual truth eventually shattered that appraisal — we all know what happened next. The suffering of the "Great Depression" and the 1929-1932 bear market are hard to erase from people's memories.

**The 1946-1949 Post-War Depression Fear**: From mid-1946 to mid-1949, over three years, investors' appraisal of common stocks as investment objects had the exact opposite outlook from the above scenario, but was equally wrong. At that time, most companies' earnings were very pleasing. But under the prevailing appraisal, stocks' P/E ratios were at multi-year lows. The financial community's reasoning: "These earnings don't amount to anything," "they're just a flash in the pan," "they'll rapidly wither or disappear in the coming economic depression." The financial community remembered that after the Civil War came the 1873 panic, triggering an extremely severe economic depression that lasted until 1879. After World War I, the 1929 stock market crash plus six years of Great Depression was even worse. Since World War II consumed far more human and material resources than World War I, people believed more severe bear markets and more serious depressions were just around the corner. As long as this appraisal persisted, most stocks sold cheaply. After the financial community finally saw clearly, realizing this impression was wrong and no severe depression was imminent, one of the longest stock price rallies in American history unfolded vigorously.

**The 1972-1974 Bear Market**: Under the 1972-1974 bear market's shadow, most stocks' P/E ratios, like the 1946-1949 period, were at records seen only twice this century. But obviously, we must ask: was the financial community's appraisal causing this correct? Was the fear psychology driving P/E ratios to historic lows reasonable? Will the 1946-1949 history repeat itself? The book will attempt to answer these questions later.

### Interest Rates and Overall Stock Market Levels

**The Key Difference**: The factors affecting all stocks' overall price level and the factors affecting one stock's P/E relative to another have a fundamental difference.

**For Individual Stock Relative P/E**: As discussed, the factors at any point affecting one stock's P/E relative to another are simply investors' current impressions of a specific company and that company's industry.

**For Overall Stock Price Levels**: But overall stock price levels are not just a matter of impression. Partly they come from the financial community's current appraisal of common stocks' attractiveness, and partly from some purely financial factors in the real world.

**The Interest Rate Factor**: The real-world factor primarily relates to interest rates. When long or short-term money market interest rates rise — especially when both rise simultaneously — more investment funds flow to these markets, and stock demand therefore declines. People may sell stocks and transfer funds to these markets. Conversely, when interest rates are low, funds flow out of these markets and into the stock market. So rising interest rates usually cause all stock prices to fall; declining interest rates cause stock prices to rise.

**The Savings Rate Factor**: Similarly, if people are willing to save a higher proportion of their income, more funds become investment funds. Compared to investment funds rising slowly, stock market prospects are more bullish. But compared to interest rate levels, this factor's influence is much smaller.

**The New Issue Factor**: New stock issuance volume fluctuations have even less influence. New issues absorb some funds that could otherwise be invested in the stock market. The reason new issuance volume has relatively small impact on overall stock prices is that when other factors make stocks attractive to investors, new issuance always increases to seize favorable opportunities. When common stock prices fall to low points, new issuance volume typically drops sharply. Thus, new issuance fluctuations are mainly influenced by other factors and are themselves less likely to be an independent influencing factor.

### The Fourth Element Summarized

**The Summary Statement**: The fourth element of stock investment can be summarized as follows: The price of any individual stock at any given point is determined by the financial community's appraisal at that time of the company, the company's industry, and to some extent, the stock price level. To determine whether a stock's price at a specific point is attractive, unattractive, or somewhere in between primarily depends on how far the financial community's appraisal has diverged from factual truth. But because overall stock price levels also influence the picture to some extent, we must also accurately estimate the impending changes in some purely financial factors; in this regard, interest rates remain the most important.

## Key Concepts

- **Company appraisal > industry appraisal**: For P/E ratios, the financial community's view of the company itself matters more than its view of the industry.
- **The two-stock comparison trap**: Simple P/E comparisons between growth stocks can be deeply misleading. A 10x stock may be worse than a 20x stock if the 10x stock's growth is less sustainable, more leveraged, or more vulnerable to unexpected events.
- **The fundamental principle**: The higher the probability of continued earnings growth, the higher the P/E investors can afford to bear.
- **"No response" phenomenon**: Even when growth materializes, the stock price may not respond if the financial community's appraisal turns negative. Patient investors who recognize this gap can find excellent opportunities.
- **The Motorola appraisal gap (March 13, 1974)**: Motorola sold its TV business to Matsushita at near book value. Stock rose 25% in one day ($48.75 → $60). The reason: the TV business had obscured investors' view of Motorola's excellent communications division. Once the TV business was gone, a new, more favorable impression could form.
- **Anchoring to historical price ranges**: When a stock trades in a range for years, investors anchor to that range as "true value." When the price breaks out of the range in either direction, emotional reactions dominate. The only true test is whether fundamentals are above or below the financial community's appraisal.
- **Three appraisal elements**: Company + industry + overall market. Only when all three are fused can we judge whether a stock is cheap or expensive.
- **The "New Era" delusion (1927-1929)**: The financial community believed no more depressions were possible. Herbert Hoover was elected president. Stocks could only go up. Reality shattered this appraisal catastrophically.
- **The post-war depression fear (1946-1949)**: The exact opposite delusion. Despite excellent earnings, the financial community believed a severe depression was inevitable (based on post-Civil War and post-WWI patterns). P/E ratios hit multi-year lows. When the depression didn't materialize, one of the longest rallies in American history began.
- **Interest rates as the key real-world factor**: Overall stock price levels are influenced partly by financial community appraisal and partly by real-world financial factors — primarily interest rates. Rising rates → stock prices fall. Falling rates → stock prices rise.

## Mental Models

- **Use the "company > industry" principle**: When evaluating a stock, focus more on the financial community's appraisal of the company itself than on industry-wide sentiment. A great company in a disfavored industry can outperform a mediocre company in a favored industry.
- **Apply the "continued growth probability" test**: When comparing two growth stocks with similar P/E ratios, ask: "Which one has a higher probability of continued growth?" The one with higher probability deserves a higher P/E. The one with lower probability may be overvalued even at a lower P/E.
- **Use the "anchoring" awareness check**: Are you anchored to a stock's historical price range? If a stock traded at $38-$43 for years and is now at $24, is it really "cheap"? Or if it's now at $70, is it really "expensive"? The only true test is whether fundamentals are above or below the financial community's current appraisal.
- **Apply the "three elements fusion" framework**: To judge whether a stock is cheap or expensive, you must consider all three appraisal layers — company, industry, and overall market — simultaneously. Missing any one leads to incomplete analysis.
- **Use the "interest rate" check**: What is the current interest rate environment? Rising rates create headwinds for all stocks. Falling rates create tailwinds. This is a real-world factor that operates independently of company or industry fundamentals.

## Anti-patterns

- **Simple P/E comparisons between growth stocks**: Comparing two stocks' P/E ratios without considering the sustainability of growth, leverage, and vulnerability to unexpected events leads to wrong conclusions. A 10x P/E may be worse than a 20x P/E if the 10x stock's growth is less likely to continue.
- **Anchoring to historical price ranges**: Treating a stock's past trading range as "true value" is a cognitive trap. When the price breaks out of the range, emotional reactions (buying because it's "cheap" or selling because it's "expensive") dominate rational analysis.
- **Ignoring the company-specific appraisal**: Focusing too much on industry trends and not enough on the financial community's specific view of the company leads to incomplete analysis. Company appraisal matters more than industry appraisal.
- **Confusing "no response" with fundamental deterioration**: When a stock's earnings grow but the price doesn't respond, it may be because the financial community's appraisal has turned negative — not because fundamentals are bad. Patient investors who recognize this gap can find excellent opportunities.
- **Ignoring interest rates**: Overall stock price levels are influenced by real-world financial factors, primarily interest rates. Ignoring the interest rate environment when making investment decisions is a serious oversight.
- **Following the crowd at market extremes**: The 1927-1929 "New Era" delusion and the 1946-1949 post-war depression fear show that the financial community can be wildly wrong at market extremes. Independent thinking is essential.

**The Two-Stock Comparison Principle**:

Two companies both expected to grow earnings at 10% annually. One trades at 10x earnings, the other at 20x. The 10x stock looks cheaper — but it may not be.

Reasons:
