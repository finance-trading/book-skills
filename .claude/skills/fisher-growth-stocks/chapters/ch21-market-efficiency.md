# Part 3 Chapter 4: 市场效率真的很高？ (Is the Market Really Efficient?)

## Core Idea
The "efficient market" theory is wrong — and Fisher's 40 years of experience prove it. Stock price dispersion is enormous (best 5 of 140 stocks returned 600%+ in 5 years while worst 5 lost 68%), companies like Raychem demonstrate that the financial community takes years to absorb known facts, and every decade creates extraordinary opportunities for investors who see what the crowd misses. The market is not efficient for diligent, knowledgeable, long-term investors.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Five-Decade Pattern
Every decade from the 1930s to the 1970s followed the same pattern:
1. A period where people believe external forces are overwhelming, making individual stock selection futile
2. Extraordinary investment opportunities emerge despite (or because of) the prevailing fear
3. Knowledgeable investors who buy and hold are richly rewarded
4. Speculative hot stocks of the era prove to be traps for the unwary

**The decades**:
- **1930s**: Depression fear — but enormous opportunities for stock buyers
- **1940s**: War fear — post-war depression expected but didn't materialize
- **1950s**: Recession fear — again, great opportunities
- **1960s**: The one decade that was different — speculative excess, but still opportunities for the disciplined
- **1970s**: Inflation and government intervention fear — same pattern repeating

**The French Proverb**: "The more things change, the more they stay the same." Every era's fears are different, but the pattern of opportunity creation and mass delusion is identical.

### The Stanford Classroom Experiment: Proving Market Inefficiency
Fisher's investment class experiment at Stanford (1961 and 1963):

**Setup**: Students divided into two groups. Group 1 got stocks starting with A; Group 2 with T. Each student received 4 stocks. They looked up closing prices from end of 1956 and compared with October 13, 1961 (nearly 5 years later). Adjusted for stock dividends and splits.

**Results** (140 stocks total):
| Capital Gain/Loss | Number of Stocks | % of Total |
|---|---|---|
| Gain 200% to 1020% | 15 stocks | 11% |
| Gain 100% to 199% | 18 stocks | 13% |
| Gain 50% to 99% | 14 stocks | 10% |
| Gain 25% to 49% | 21 stocks | 15% |
| Gain 1% to 24% | 31 stocks | 22% |
| Unchanged | 3 stocks | 2% |
| Loss 1% to 49% | 32 stocks | 23% |
| Loss 50% to 74% | 6 stocks | 4% |
| **Total** | **140 stocks** | **100%** |

**Key findings**:
- During a period when the Dow Jones rose 41% (from 499 to 703), 38 stocks (27%) had capital losses
- 6 stocks (4%) lost more than 50%
- About 25% of stocks had very good capital gains
- **Top 5 stocks**: $10,000 invested → $70,260 in 4.5 years (602% return)
- **Bottom 5 stocks**: $10,000 invested → $3,180 (68% loss)
- **Wealth gap**: $48,000 difference between smart and foolish investment behavior over ~5 years
- Even selecting from the top 10 (not just top 5): $10,000 → $52,070. Bottom 10: $10,000 → $4,270
- **Replication**: 1.5 years later, same exercise with different stocks, nearly identical dispersion over 5 years

**The Conclusion**: If markets were efficient, such extreme dispersion would be impossible. The fact that top stocks returned 10x while bottom stocks lost 70% in the same period proves that individual stock analysis creates enormous value — and that the market does NOT efficiently price all known information.

### The Raychem Corporation Story: The Definitive Proof
Raychem exemplified why markets are not efficient.

**The Rise (early-mid 1970s)**:
Raychem had four operating principles (from EVP Robert Halperin):
1. Never do anything technically simple (that competitors can easily copy)
2. Never do anything without vertical integration — must handle R&D, manufacturing, and sales
3. Never pursue a project without a large chance of winning proprietary protection (usually patents)
4. Only produce products that can become market leaders, regardless of market size

The financial community recognized these advantages and bid the stock to a high P/E ratio (~25× estimated earnings). By end of June 1973, the stock reached $42+ (split-adjusted).

**The Fall (1976)**:
Raychem suffered a double blow:
1. **Stilan failure**: Their proprietary polymer for aerospace coating — the product the financial community was most excited about — was "scientifically successful but commercially a failure." Competitors introduced cheaper alternatives that were "good enough." Raychem wrote off the massive investment (~$9.3 million charge), EPS fell from $7.95 to $0.08
2. **Organizational restructuring**: Management restructured from product-based to industry-based divisions. This caused 1-2 quarters of reduced earnings as people adjusted to new reporting relationships. Management deliberately proceeded during the worst year to get it over with — showing long-term thinking

**The Result**: Stock fell to ~$14 (split-adjusted) — about ⅓ of the previous high. The financial community lost faith in Raychem's R&D abilities.

**Why This Proves Market Inefficiency**:
- Raychem's management was completely transparent — they held a full-day meeting with all analysts, brokers, and investment advisors, explaining every fact openly
- The facts presented at that meeting predicted exactly the earnings recovery that followed
- **Yet the stock didn't react**. A year or two later, earnings reached new highs, and the stock rose over 1× from the meeting day level
- But in the weeks after the meeting, the stock barely moved
- Most attendees were impressed but couldn't shake the negative impression from 1-2 years earlier
- **Efficient market theory says**: All known information is immediately reflected in prices. Raychem's reality: All information was presented openly, but the market took 1-2 years to absorb it

**The Silver Lining**: The Stilan failure actually freed up resources. The R&D money and — more importantly — the top research scientists were released to work on other projects. Within 1-2 years, a bumper crop of promising new research projects emerged, higher in proportion to the company's size than ever before.

### The Implications for Different Investor Types
Fisher observed that belief in efficient market theory divides into two groups:
1. **Students with little practical experience** — attracted by the academic elegance
2. **Large institutional fund managers** — ironically, the very people whose behavior creates market inefficiency

Individual retail investors largely ignore the theory — and are better off for it.

### The Book's Conclusion: Seven Core Principles
Fisher summarized his investment philosophy in seven points:

1. **Buy companies with systematic plans for significant earnings growth**, and whose intrinsic qualities make it difficult for new entrants to share that growth
2. **Concentrate in companies out of favor** — when the financial community misprices true value, buy decisively
3. **Hold until** (a) the company's character fundamentally changes (e.g., management deterioration), or (b) growth rate can no longer exceed the overall economy. Never sell based on market/economic forecasts or short-term reasons. Watch for companies that outgrow their management capability
4. **De-emphasize dividends** for investors seeking major capital appreciation. High-growth, low-dividend companies offer the best opportunities
5. **Accept some mistakes as unavoidable costs**. The important thing is to recognize errors quickly, understand their causes, and learn to avoid them. Good investment management means tolerating small losses on some stocks while letting the big winners run. Never sell just to realize a profit
6. **Truly outstanding companies are few**. Their stocks are often unavailable at attractive prices. When a favorable price exists, take full advantage. Concentrate capital in the most favorable opportunities
7. **(Implied — the book ends here)**: The entire philosophy rests on the proposition that deep research, patience, and independence can identify companies that the financial community undervalues — and that this undervaluation persists long enough to be exploited

## Key Concepts

- **Efficient market theory (高效率市场理论)**: The academic claim that stock prices immediately and fully reflect all known information, making it impossible to consistently find undervalued stocks without illegal insider information
- **Stock price dispersion**: The empirical observation that stocks' returns vary enormously over any 5-year period — proving that some stocks are dramatically mispriced
- **The Raychem proof**: Even when a company is completely transparent and presents all recovery information openly, the market takes 1-2 years to absorb it. This directly contradicts efficient market theory
- **The five-decade pattern**: Every decade produces the same pattern — mass fear creates opportunities, speculative fever creates traps, knowledgeable investors who hold are rewarded
- **"The more things change, the more they stay the same"**: French proverb that captures the recurring pattern of investor psychology across decades
- **Seven core principles**: Fisher's complete investment philosophy distilled — buy growth, buy contrarian, hold long-term, ignore dividends, accept some mistakes, concentrate in the best ideas

## Mental Models

- **Use the "dispersion test"**: Look at the range of stock returns over any 5-year period. If the best stocks return 10x while the worst lose 70%, the market is clearly NOT efficiently pricing all known information. This dispersion is your opportunity
- **Apply the "Raychem transparency" test**: If a company presents all its recovery information openly and the market still doesn't react for 1-2 years, then you don't need insider information to profit — you just need patience and conviction
- **Use the "five-decade" lens**: Every decade's "unprecedented" crisis has precedents. The specific fear changes (Depression, war, inflation, government intervention) but the pattern of opportunity creation is identical. Act accordingly
- **Apply the "concentrate when rare" rule**: Truly outstanding companies at attractive prices are rare. When they appear, concentrate capital. Don't dilute your position in your best ideas to maintain arbitrary diversification
- **Use the "sell only for fundamental change" discipline**: Sell a stock only when (a) the company's character fundamentally changes, or (b) growth can no longer exceed the economy. Never sell because of market forecasts, economic predictions, or short-term price movements

## Anti-patterns

- **Believing markets are efficient because an academic theory says so**: The empirical evidence — enormous stock dispersion, Raychem's delayed repricing, five decades of recurring opportunities — all contradict efficient market theory. Don't let elegant theory override observed reality
- **Selling great companies to "lock in profits"**: Fisher's seven principles explicitly say: never sell just to realize a profit. Sell only when fundamentals change or growth stalls
- **Diversifying away from your best ideas**: When you find a truly outstanding company at a favorable price, concentrate capital. The best investors don't own 50 stocks — they own their best 3-5 ideas heavily
- **Letting short-term noise drive decisions**: Market forecasts, economic predictions, and daily price movements are irrelevant to long-term investment success. Focus on company fundamentals
- **Accepting mistakes without learning**: Some errors are unavoidable. But failing to diagnose the cause and learn from it turns an unavoidable cost into a repeated pattern
- **Paying attention to the "era delusion"**: Whether it's Depression fear, war fear, or inflation fear, every era's mass psychology creates the same opportunity pattern. Don't be seduced by the narrative of "this time it's different"

## Worked Example

**The Stanford Classroom Experiment (1956-1961)**:

Setup: 140 stocks, split into two alphabetical groups. Each student tracked 4 stocks for nearly 5 years.

Results (when Dow Jones rose only 41%):
- **Best performer category**: 15 stocks gained 200-1020%
- **Worst performer category**: 6 stocks lost 50-74%
- **Dollar impact**: $10,000 in the top 5 stocks → $70,260. $10,000 in the bottom 5 → $3,180
- **The wealth gap**: $67,080 difference between the best and worst investment decisions
- **Even excluding luck**: $10,000 in the top 10 → $52,070. Bottom 10 → $4,270. Gap: $47,800

**What this proves**:
1. Stocks are NOT efficiently priced — enormous dispersion in returns over the same period is incompatible with efficient pricing
2. Individual stock selection creates enormous value — the difference between good and bad selection is life-changing
3. Even the Dow Jones (supposedly the "market") rose only 41% — but individual stocks ranged from +1000% to -74%. The "market" hid enormous dispersion

**Replication**: 1.5 years later, Fisher ran the same experiment with different stocks over a different 5-year period. Nearly identical dispersion. The pattern is structural, not coincidental.

**The Raychem Story (1973-1978)**:

Timeline:
- **1973**: Stock at $42+ (split-adjusted). P/E ~25×. Financial community enthusiastic about Stilan and R&D pipeline
- **1976**: Double blow — Stilan failure ($9.3M charge, EPS from $7.95 to $0.08) and organizational restructuring (temporary earnings depression). Stock falls to ~$14 (⅓ of high)
- **1978**: Management holds full-day meeting with all analysts. Ten top executives present complete, candid information about:
  - Company prospects
  - Problems and their causes
  - Why the earnings dip would be brief
  - New research projects emerging from freed-up resources
- **Meeting date stock price**: ~$23½
- **1-2 years after meeting**: Earnings reach new highs. Stock rises to $48+ (over 1× from meeting day)

**The market inefficiency proof**:
- All information was publicly available at the meeting
- Management was maximally transparent
- The meeting's predictions proved exactly correct
- Yet the stock barely moved in the weeks after the meeting
- Analysts were impressed but couldn't overcome their negative impression from 1-2 years earlier
- It took 1-2 years for the market to absorb what was presented clearly and honestly in a single day

**The Lesson**: If markets were efficient, the stock should have immediately reflected the information presented at the meeting. Instead, it took 1-2 years. This delay — caused by the financial community's psychological bias toward recent negative events — creates the opportunity that makes active investing profitable.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Markets are not efficient**: Enormous stock dispersion (best 5: +600%, worst 5: -68%, same period) proves that prices do not reflect all known information
2. **The Raychem proof is definitive**: Complete transparency, accurate predictions, immediate availability of information — yet the market took 1-2 years to absorb it. Efficient market theory cannot explain this
3. **Every decade creates opportunities**: The 1930s through 1970s all followed the same pattern — mass fear creates buying opportunities, speculative fever creates traps. "The more things change, the more they stay the same"
4. **Individual stock selection creates enormous value**: The wealth gap between good and bad stock selection over 5 years was $48,000 on a $10,000 investment. This is not noise — it's the reward for diligent analysis
5. **Concentrate in the best ideas**: Outstanding companies at attractive prices are rare. When they appear, bet heavily. Diversification is for people who don't know what they're doing
6. **Hold for fundamental reasons only**: Sell when the company's character changes or growth stalls. Never sell for market forecasts, economic predictions, or short-term price movements
7. **The seven principles summarize a lifetime**: Buy growth companies, buy contrarian, hold long-term, ignore dividends, accept some mistakes, concentrate capital, and never sell just to lock in profits
