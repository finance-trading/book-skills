# Chapter 5: 何时买进 (When to Buy)

## Core Idea
Timing matters — even the right stock bought at the wrong time can underperform. Fisher rejects traditional economic forecasting as useless (comparing it to medieval alchemy) and instead identifies a superior timing signal: the development cycle of new products and plants. When a company's new commercial plant hits unexpected problems and the stock price plunges, that's often the best time to buy.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Why Economic Forecasting Fails

Fisher compares economic forecasting to medieval alchemy:
- Experts disagree fundamentally, even when their arguments seem persuasive
- Predictions are often wrong, even when based on sophisticated models
- The probability of correct prediction is too low to base investment decisions on
- Even with computers and complex "input-output" models, accurate forecasting remains elusive

**The verdict**: Until economics advances significantly, treat economic forecasting as alchemy — interesting but unreliable for making investment decisions.

### The Development Cycle Timing Signal

Instead of macroeconomic forecasting, Fisher identifies a company-specific timing signal:

**The Cycle**:
1. **Laboratory development**: Company develops new products/processes in the lab
2. **Pilot plant**: Initial commercialization in a small test facility (takes 6 months to several years, very expensive)
3. **Stock price rises**: News of pilot plant success drives stock price up
4. **First commercial plant**: Company builds full-scale commercial plant
5. **Unexpected problems**: The commercial plant faces "bugs" — unforeseen technical problems, higher-than-expected costs, longer-than-expected startup time
6. **Earnings plunge**: Unexpected expenses reduce earnings per share
7. **Stock price crashes**: Disappointed investors sell, stock price falls to multi-year lows
8. **Problems solved**: Eventually, the plant runs smoothly
9. **Sales push**: Company must spend heavily on marketing to establish the new product
10. **Earnings remain depressed**: Special sales expenses keep earnings low
11. **Stock price hits bottom**: This is the optimal buying opportunity

**Why this works**:
- The market overreacts to short-term problems
- Once the plant is running smoothly and marketing succeeds, earnings explode
- Subsequent plants (2nd, 3rd, 4th) are built faster and cheaper — no more startup costs
- The company scales rapidly, and the stock price appreciates dramatically

### The American Cyanamid Example

In 1954, American Cyanamid's stock was undervalued because:
- The market saw its industrial and agricultural chemical divisions as inefficient (leftover from 1920s merger boom)
- Few noticed that new management was ruthlessly cutting costs, streamlining operations
- The market didn't appreciate the value of the Lederle pharmaceutical division (one of the world's best)

Fisher's analysis (using the 15 points) revealed:
- New management was excellent (Point 7, 8, 14)
- Cost-cutting was improving profit margins (Point 5)
- The Lederle division had outstanding R&D (Point 2, 3)
- The market was mispricing the company

**Result**: The stock appreciated dramatically as the market recognized the improvements.

## Key Concepts

- **Development cycle timing**: Buy when a company's new plant hits unexpected problems and the stock price plunges — not when everything is going smoothly
- **The pilot plant vs. commercial plant distinction**: Pilot plants are like driving 10 mph on a bumpy country road. Commercial plants are like driving 100 mph on the same road — problems multiply exponentially
- **Market overreaction to short-term problems**: The market punishes companies for temporary setbacks, creating buying opportunities for patient investors
- **The "bug" period**: Every new technology or plant faces unexpected problems during startup. This is normal, not a sign of fundamental failure
- **Subsequent plants are cheaper**: Once the first plant's problems are solved, the 2nd, 3rd, 4th plants are built faster and with fewer surprises

## Mental Models

- **Think of development cycles as "valleys of opportunity"**: When a company's stock plunges due to temporary development problems, that's often the best time to buy — not the worst
- **Distinguish between temporary setbacks and permanent failures**: A company struggling with plant startup problems is different from a company whose product is fundamentally flawed
- **Use the 15 points to assess whether the problem is temporary**: If the company scores well on management quality, R&D effectiveness, and financial strength, the problem is likely temporary

## Anti-patterns

- **Buying when everything is going smoothly**: By the time a company's new product is a proven success, the stock price already reflects that. The best buying opportunity was during the "valley of despair"
- **Selling during temporary setbacks**: If a company's stock plunges due to plant startup problems, that's often a buying opportunity — not a reason to sell
- **Relying on economic forecasting**: Macroeconomic predictions are too unreliable to base investment decisions on. Focus on company-specific factors instead
- **Assuming short-term earnings declines signal permanent problems**: A company investing heavily in new product development may see short-term earnings declines, but long-term prospects may be excellent
- **Ignoring the development cycle**: Companies in high-tech or capital-intensive industries go through predictable development cycles. Understanding where a company is in its cycle is crucial for timing

## Worked Example

**The Typical Development Cycle**:

A company develops a revolutionary new product:
1. Lab R&D succeeds → stock price rises modestly
2. Pilot plant works → stock price rises significantly
3. First commercial plant built → stock price peaks
4. Plant startup hits unexpected problems → costs soar, earnings plunge
5. Stock price crashes to multi-year lows
6. Eventually, problems are solved → plant runs smoothly
7. Company launches major sales push → earnings remain depressed due to marketing costs
8. Stock price hits absolute bottom ← **THIS IS THE BUYING OPPORTUNITY**
9. Sales succeed → earnings explode
10. 2nd, 3rd, 4th plants built quickly and cheaply → earnings continue rising
11. Stock price appreciates dramatically over the next 5-10 years

**Why most investors miss this**:
- During steps 4-8, the news is terrible: "Company X's new plant is a disaster!" "Earnings plunge!" "Management made a huge mistake!"
- The market panics and sells
- But patient investors who understand the 15 points recognize that:
  - The company has excellent management (Point 7, 8, 14)
  - The R&D was sound (Point 2, 3)
  - The financial position is strong enough to weather the storm (Point 12)
  - The problems are temporary, not fundamental
- They buy during the "valley of despair" and reap the rewards when the company succeeds

## Key Takeaways

1. **Economic forecasting is useless for investment timing** — treat it as alchemy, not science
2. **The development cycle provides superior timing signals** — buy when a company's new plant hits temporary problems and the stock price plunges
3. **Distinguish between temporary setbacks and permanent failures** — use the 15 points to assess whether the company's fundamentals remain strong
4. **The market overreacts to short-term problems** — this creates buying opportunities for patient investors who understand the business
5. **Subsequent plants are cheaper and faster** — once the first plant's problems are solved, the company scales rapidly
6. **The "valley of despair" is the optimal buying opportunity** — not when everything is going smoothly
