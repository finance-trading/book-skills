# Chapter 4: 要买什么 — 应用所学选取所需 (What to Buy — Apply What You've Learned)

## Core Idea
Once you understand the 15 points, you must decide what *type* of growth stock to buy: large, established growth companies (like Dow Chemical, DuPont, IBM) or smaller, high-potential companies (like early-stage tech firms). Fisher argues that most investors should allocate the majority of their capital to the former, with a smaller portion to the latter — and that growth stocks consistently outperform high-dividend stocks over long periods.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Spectrum of Growth Stocks

**Large, Established Growth Companies (大型成熟成长股)**:
- Examples: Dow Chemical, DuPont, IBM (in Fisher's era)
- Characteristics: Deep roots in the economy, strong financial position, limited further dramatic growth potential but very stable
- Risk profile: Lower risk — even in a downturn, the company's competitive position is unlikely to deteriorate catastrophically
- Returns: Over 10 years (1946-1956), these stocks appreciated ~5x in value, plus growing dividends
- Suitable for: Most investors, especially those with significant capital or family responsibilities

**Small, High-Potential Growth Companies (小型高潜力成长股)**:
- Examples: Ampex Corporation (in 1953), young tech companies
- Characteristics: $1M-$7M annual revenue, promising products, outstanding management and technical talent, entering high-growth fields
- Risk profile: Higher risk — if you make a mistake, the entire investment can be lost
- Returns: Can appreciate 7x or more in 4 years (Ampex example), but success is not guaranteed
- Suitable for: Investors with higher risk tolerance, longer time horizons, and sufficient capital to diversify

### The Allocation Principle

**The 60-100% Rule**: "Most" of your capital (60-100%, depending on circumstances) should go to larger, more conservative growth stocks. The remainder can go to smaller, higher-risk companies.

**Examples by investor type**:
- Wealthy widow with $500K: Can invest 100% in conservative growth stocks
- Wealthy widow with $1M and three children: Should keep 85% in conservative growth stocks, 15% in high-risk small companies (to preserve living standard while still seeking growth for heirs)
- Business executive with $400K and $10K annual savings: Can invest existing $400K in conservative growth stocks, use annual $10K savings for higher-risk small companies

### Growth Stocks vs. High-Dividend Stocks

**The Evidence**: Over 35 years, every major financial institution study comparing growth stocks (low dividend yield, high reinvestment) vs. high-dividend stocks showed the same result: over 5-10 year periods, growth stocks' capital appreciation far exceeded that of high-dividend stocks.

**The Surprising Result**: Even the dividend income from growth stocks, after 5-10 years, exceeded the dividend income from high-dividend stocks bought at the same time — because the growth companies' earnings and dividends grew substantially.

**The Logic**: Growth stocks compound. A stock that doubles in value and also increases its dividend creates a powerful compounding effect. High-dividend stocks may provide immediate income, but they sacrifice long-term wealth creation.

### When to Use Each Type

**Use high-dividend stocks when**:
- You need immediate income to live on (retirees without other income sources)
- You have insufficient capital for growth stocks to generate meaningful future income
- You prioritize current consumption over future wealth

**Use growth stocks when**:
- You have a long time horizon (10+ years)
- You have sufficient capital that you don't need immediate income
- You want to maximize long-term wealth, even at the expense of current income
- You can afford to wait for compounding to work

## Key Concepts

- **Institutional stocks (机构型股票)**: Large, stable growth stocks that insurance companies, pension funds, and professional trustees buy. They provide safety and steady growth.
- **The compounding advantage**: Growth stocks appreciate in value *and* increase their dividends over time. The combined effect far exceeds what high-dividend stocks can deliver.
- **Risk diversification**: Small, high-risk companies can fail completely. Large growth companies are unlikely to fail catastrophically. This asymmetry favors allocating most capital to the safer option.
- **The time horizon test**: If you need money within 5 years, don't invest in stocks at all. If you have 10+ years, growth stocks are superior. If you need income now, high-dividend stocks may be necessary — but understand the trade-off.

## Mental Models

- **Think of growth stocks as "planting trees"**: You sacrifice current fruit (dividends) for future harvest (capital appreciation + larger future dividends). The longer you wait, the greater the harvest.
- **Use the "60-100% rule" as a guideline**: Most of your capital should go to safer, established growth companies. Only use money you can afford to lose on high-risk small companies.
- **Evaluate stocks by total return, not just dividend yield**: A stock with 2% dividend yield that appreciates 20% annually delivers far more than a stock with 8% dividend yield that doesn't appreciate.

## Anti-patterns

- **Chasing high dividend yields**: High dividends often signal limited growth potential. The company may be mature, facing competitive pressures, or lacking reinvestment opportunities. Over 10+ years, growth stocks consistently outperform.
- **Allocating too much to small, high-risk companies**: Small companies can fail. If you put 80% of your capital in speculative small caps and one fails, you've lost a catastrophic amount. Keep speculative positions small.
- **Ignoring the time horizon**: If you need money in 3 years, don't invest in stocks at all. Growth stocks require patience — they may underperform in the short term but outperform over 10+ years.
- **Assuming dividends are "safe" income**: Dividends can be cut. A company facing competitive pressures may reduce or eliminate dividends. Capital appreciation from a growing company is more durable.
- **Over-diversifying into mediocre companies**: It's better to own 5-10 excellent growth stocks than 50 average ones. Dilution reduces your returns and makes monitoring impossible.

## Worked Example

**Dow Chemical, DuPont, and IBM (1946-1956)**:
Fisher's son Kenneth provides real data from a 10-year period. An investor who bought these three stocks in 1946 saw:
- Capital appreciation: Each stock's value increased ~5x over 10 years
- Dividend growth: Dow's dividend yield started at ~2.5%, but by 1956, the yield-on-original-cost was 8-9% (because dividends had increased so much, and the stock had split multiple times)
- Total return: The combined effect of 5x capital appreciation plus growing dividends created extraordinary wealth

Compare this to an investor who bought high-dividend stocks in 1946: they received steady income but missed the explosive capital appreciation. After 10 years, the growth stock investor's portfolio was worth far more — *and* their dividend income (as a percentage of original investment) now exceeded what the high-dividend investor was receiving.

**The lesson**: Patience and compounding beat immediate income. Growth stocks deliver both capital appreciation *and* growing dividends — the best of both worlds, if you can wait.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Most capital should go to large, established growth companies** (60-100% depending on circumstances). Only use money you can afford to lose on small, high-risk companies.
2. **Growth stocks consistently outperform high-dividend stocks over 10+ year periods** — in both capital appreciation and eventual dividend income.
3. **The compounding effect is powerful**: A stock that appreciates 5x and increases its dividend creates exponential wealth. High-dividend stocks cannot match this.
4. **Match your allocation to your time horizon and risk tolerance**: If you need income now, high-dividend stocks may be necessary. If you have 10+ years, growth stocks are superior.
5. **Don't over-diversify**: It's better to own 5-10 excellent companies than 50 average ones. Focus and concentration beat diversification into mediocrity.
6. **Understand the risk asymmetry**: Small companies can go to zero. Large growth companies are unlikely to fail catastrophically. This favors allocating most capital to the safer option.
