## Key Decisions

### Entering Wall Street in 1914

Graham began in a market environment where analysis, quotations, and financial information were less standardized. The experience gave him direct exposure to how securities were described and sold, and later reinforced his insistence on independent examination.

### Responding to 1929-1932

Graham recognized that speculative prices were excessive but still suffered because he used borrowed money. The behavioral lesson is stronger than the market call: being directionally right does not protect an investor who has made the capital structure fragile.

### Creating Graham-Newman

The firm institutionalized a repeatable research and portfolio process rather than relying on one-off personal judgments. This reflects his preference for rules, documentation, and diversification across bargains.

### Buying GEICO in 1948

Graham-Newman bought a major stake in GEICO at a low price. The position became highly successful, but legal ownership limits forced distribution of the shares to clients. The episode shows both the power of a bargain with exceptional economics and the importance of understanding structural constraints.

### Teaching at Columbia and UCLA

Graham repeatedly chose teaching as a major channel of influence. He treated investing as a learnable discipline that could be transmitted through definitions, examples, and practice.

### Retiring from detailed security analysis

By the 1976 interview, Graham reported that detailed analysis had lost much of its interest and that a few simple principles could produce satisfactory results. This was a decision to reduce complexity when the marginal research edge no longer seemed worth the effort.

## Decision Heuristics Observed

1. Do not use leverage when the future is uncertain.
2. Buy only when the facts and price jointly provide protection.
3. Prefer a repeatable group process when individual forecasts are unreliable.
4. Let the portfolio absorb individual errors.
5. Separate a good company from a good purchase.
6. Treat legal, accounting, and capital-structure constraints as part of the investment case.
7. Stop doing work whose cost exceeds its likely improvement in results.
8. Teach the method in a way that another person can execute.

## Results and Feedback

Graham's 1929 experience reinforced his anti-leverage stance. GEICO demonstrated that a statistical or asset-oriented investor could encounter a business with exceptional economics, but Graham did not turn that exceptional case into a universal requirement. His later simplification shows feedback from decades of practice rather than blind loyalty to his earliest techniques.

## Contradictions / Weak Signals

- The public record gives stronger evidence for Graham's principles and teaching than for a complete audited decision-by-decision portfolio history.
- Famous successes can make the method seem more uniform than it was. Graham used multiple strategies: liquidations, arbitrage, control situations, statistical bargains, and groups of securities.
- His later views may reflect age, retirement, changing opportunity sets, or philosophical preference; they should not be read as a universal proof that detailed analysis is never useful.

## Extraction Notes for Skill Builder

When applying Graham's lens, ask what action is actually being taken, what leverage or fragility it introduces, what evidence would falsify the thesis, and whether the process is repeatable across a basket rather than dependent on one heroic forecast.
