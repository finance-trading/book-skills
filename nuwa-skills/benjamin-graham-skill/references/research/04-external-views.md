## Admirers and Intellectual Heirs

Graham's strongest legacy is methodological. Columbia identifies him and David Dodd as pioneers of value investing. Buffett publicly framed a group of independently operating investors as evidence that the Graham-Dodd approach could travel across people, portfolios, and market situations. The point was not that all disciples used identical formulas; it was that they shared a discipline of buying value at a meaningful discount.

Buffett also shows the framework's capacity for adaptation. He began with Graham's bargain orientation and later emphasized durable business quality, economics, and management more heavily. That evolution should be treated as an extension by Buffett, not retroactively assigned to Graham.

## Major Critiques

### Formula dependence

Net-nets, low price-to-book stocks, and mechanical earnings-yield rules can become traps when assets are obsolete, earnings are cyclical, or liabilities are understated. A cheap ratio is a screening signal, not a complete investment thesis.

### Historical market structure

Graham worked in a market with more neglected small companies, less standardized disclosure, and more opportunities to buy below liquidation value. Modern competition and information access can reduce the availability of the easiest bargains.

### Intangible and growth businesses

The framework is less naturally suited to businesses whose value comes from brand, software, network effects, organizational capital, or long-duration reinvestment. Graham's insistence on measurable evidence remains useful, but the evidence must be adapted.

### False precision

Readers can mistake formulas for truth. Intrinsic value depends on assumptions about normalized earnings, asset quality, and future returns. A model with more decimals is not necessarily safer.

### Survivorship and disciples

The success of famous Graham students demonstrates portability but does not remove survivorship bias. A serious application must ask how many practitioners failed, abandoned the method, or lacked the temperament to persist.

## Comparison with Buffett and Munger

| Dimension | Graham | Buffett | Munger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary edge | Price below conservative value | Business quality plus sensible price | Quality, incentives, psychology, and concentrated judgment |
| Typical posture | Defensive, diversified, statistical | Selective, increasingly concentrated | Highly selective and concentrated |
| Core danger | Permanent loss and overpaying | Paying too much or trusting quality blindly | Stupidity, incentives, and psychological error |
| Style | Definitions, rules, arithmetic | Narrative plus economics | Aphorism, inversion, cross-disciplinary models |

## Blind Spots / Failure Modes

- Treating accounting value as economic value without testing asset quality.
- Over-diversifying into mediocre bargains without a portfolio-level thesis.
- Ignoring business durability because the stock is statistically cheap.
- Using “Mr. Market” to excuse not understanding the business.
- Confusing patience with refusal to update facts.
- Applying an old formula to a changed market without checking whether the expected edge still exists.

## Contradictions / Weak Signals

Graham is remembered as a detailed analyst but late interviews point toward simpler group methods. He is remembered as the father of active value investing but also expressed sympathy for indexing and skepticism about the average professional's ability to beat the market after costs. His legacy is therefore better described as “process discipline under uncertainty” than as one fixed stock screen.

## Extraction Notes for Skill Builder

The skill should include an explicit “Graham plus modern correction” boundary: use his process to test price, balance-sheet strength, downside, and investor fit, but do not claim that every modern business can be valued by 1930s liquidation metrics.
