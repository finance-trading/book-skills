# Philip Arthur Fisher: 决策记录与行动逻辑

调研日期：2026-08-24。历史人物的逐笔交易记录不完整；本文件优先记录原著中明确描述的决策规则和可核对的职业节点，避免编造收益率或持仓比例。

来源编号：S01、S06（详见 [`../sources.md`](../sources.md)）。

## 职业与方法形成

- 1927 年左右进入旧金山金融行业，经历 1929 年后市场环境，随后形成重视企业经营事实、管理质量和长期盈利能力的取向。具体年份在不同传记中有差异，时间线文件以“1920 年代末”标注。
- 1931 年创办 Fisher & Co.（该年份常见于机构历史资料，仍属二手，需以公司档案复核）。这解释了其长期、集中、研究驱动的职业形态，但不应被写成已核实的个人自述。
- 以“先筛选、再外部调查、最后见管理层”作为研究顺序；管理层会议不是营销演示，而是对已形成假设的验证。

## 原著中反复出现的决策规则

1. **先确认企业质量，再处理价格和时点**：宏观预测的低可靠性不能替代对未来盈余曲线的研究。
2. **暂时性问题可提供买点**：新工厂、产品转换或销售投入压低短期利润时，先判断问题是否有限、可修复、且长期能力未损坏。
3. **论题失效才卖出**：管理层退化、市场空间耗尽、15 点出现实质性裂缝、诚信问题或有明确更优替代时退出；价格上涨本身不是事实恶化。
4. **股利服从再投资回报**：若企业保留一美元能高回报再投资，低派息可能更合理；没有高回报项目时应返还股东。
5. **适度集中、承认错误**：不要用大量普通公司掩盖研究不足；同时仓位要能承受单一判断错误，不能投入无法承受损失的钱。

## 公开案例的证据边界

原著使用过工业、化工、电子等历史案例来说明工厂爬坡、研发、销售和管理质量，但不同版本的公司名、年份和上下文在网络摘录中常被简化。本 Skill 只使用仓库逐章稿中可复核的“暂时性问题/美国氰胺”等案例逻辑，不给出未经原书页码核验的精确收益数字。

## 事后复盘

Fisher 的哲学文本把投资经验视为实验：保留当时的信息集，区分事实、推理、执行和结果，修改流程而不是仅凭结果改结论。该原则是方法论的一手摘要，但“每次交易都写有完整备忘录”没有足够公开证据，不作事实陈述。
 # Philip Arthur Fisher: decision records

 **Research date:** 2026-08-24. Unless marked otherwise, the evidence below is Fisher's first-person account in *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (1958), especially the autobiographical chapters 12–13. The repository's OCR is a local working copy of the book; the publisher record for the English collected edition is https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Common+Stocks+and+Uncommon+Profits%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9780471445500. “Inference” means a pattern drawn from the event, not a claim Fisher explicitly made.

 ## 1. Start an advisory business during the Depression (1931–1935)

 * **Choice:** After his employer failed during the 1929–30 collapse and the available job was clerical, Fisher used the crisis to start his own investment-advisory practice in January 1931. He kept overhead extremely low; the account in the book reports average monthly net profit of only $2.99 in 1932, rising to roughly $29 in 1933, with a more established profitable practice by 1935.
 * **Direct evidence:** Fisher's autobiographical narrative, ch. 12, local OCR pp. 242–244 (primary; high credibility for his own reasoning, lower for independently audited amounts).
 * **Decision logic:** A dislocated market created unusually open access to business owners and dissatisfied clients; a small, low-cost practice gave him room to apply a different research method. **Inference:** he treated constraints and poor labor-market conditions as an opportunity to build a durable process rather than wait for normal conditions.

 ## 2. Buy Food Machinery Company when the market treated it as speculative (1932–1934)

 * **Choice:** He studied Food Machinery Company after the stock fell to roughly $4–$5 during the 1932–33 trough, visited and evaluated management, and bought for clients and himself. He emphasized product breadth, distribution, installed-base replacement demand, R&D, management depth, and integrity. He urged clients to buy and hold through the 1933–38 recovery.
 * **Direct evidence:** ch. 12, local OCR pp. 246–252 (primary; the company discussion and dates are Fisher's retrospective account).
 * **Decision logic:** Business evidence and management quality outweighed the market's “promotional/speculative” label. Fisher explicitly warns that contrarianism is only useful when the analyst has a method for checking whether the crowd is wrong. **Inference:** this is an early prototype of his later 15-point test and scuttlebutt process.

 ## 3. Replace short-term trading with long-term ownership (post-World War II)

 * **Choice:** In the 1930s he made three profitable short-term trades in California Packing Company. In retrospect he judged the trades a poor use of time and risk compared with holding superior businesses for years. After World War II he resolved to devote his effort to long-term investing.
 * **Direct evidence:** ch. 13, local OCR pp. 257–258 (primary; self-critique).
 * **Decision logic:** Repeated short-term wins did not compensate for opportunity cost, timing risk, and the likelihood that a fourth trade would fail. **Inference:** Fisher's “long term” is a deliberate response to process economics and error rates, not a claim that every holding should be permanent.

 ## 4. Adopt a three-year evaluation rule and hold through noise

 * **Choice:** Fisher told clients to give a new purchase up to three years to produce substantial results. One or two years of market underperformance was not, by itself, a sell signal; he would sell earlier only if his original facts changed or he concluded the three-year outcome would not be good.
 * **Direct evidence:** ch. 13, local OCR pp. 252–255 (primary; explicit rule and rationale).
 * **Decision logic:** Separate business thesis from short-term price confirmation and force a review horizon long enough for research, product, and earnings investments to show up.

 ## 5. Buy Texas Instruments and Motorola against consensus (second half of 1955)

 * **Choice:** Fisher bought unusually large positions in Texas Instruments and Motorola in late 1955, when most professional investors considered them speculative electronics names. He was impressed by the people and saw semiconductor/communications potential before the market consensus did.
 * **Direct evidence:** ch. 13, local OCR pp. 253–254 (primary; first-person account). He says Motorola's communications people and long-run industry potential were the key observations; Daniel Noble's later contribution was an unforeseen positive, not the original thesis.
 * **Decision logic:** Identify a capable management team and a large technological runway before current earnings and category labels make the opportunity obvious. **Inference:** this is a high-conviction example of “quality plus future opportunity,” not a license to buy any fashionable technology stock.

 ## 6. Refuse to sell Motorola after an initially poor result (1955–late 1950s)

 * **Choice:** Texas Instruments rose promptly, while Motorola traded below Fisher's cost for about a year and drew sharp criticism from a major client. He held because his research conclusion had not changed. An insurance company partner later told him Motorola had outperformed every other holding in its portfolio before Wall Street accepted the communications opportunity.
 * **Direct evidence:** ch. 13, local OCR pp. 254–255 (primary; retrospective and not independently verified here).
 * **Decision logic:** Apply the three-year rule and re-check facts, rather than outsource the decision to price action, client pressure, or an external portfolio review.

 ## 7. Make an explicit exception for Rogers Corporation (around the 1970s)

 * **Choice:** Rogers had weak earnings and underperformed at the three-year mark, but Fisher did not sell. He relied on the chief executive Norman Greenman's unusual honesty and on evidence that a large, misallocated R&D program was being abandoned so other promising products could receive resources. Fisher increased his and clients' positions while the share price was depressed.
 * **Direct evidence:** ch. 13, local OCR pp. 255–257 (primary; names and sequence are Fisher's account).
 * **Decision logic:** The original thesis was revised, not blindly defended: management integrity plus a finite operational problem outweighed near-term earnings. **Inference:** a rule is a default; a documented thesis change can justify an exception.

 ## 8. Correct his own limit-price anchoring

 * **Choice:** Fisher describes repeatedly refusing to pay a small amount above a self-imposed limit price even when he believed a stock could multiply over several years. After seeing a client miss Food Machinery because of a rigid limit order, he consciously corrected this habit.
 * **Direct evidence:** ch. 13, local OCR pp. 258–260 (primary; autobiographical self-critique).
 * **Decision logic:** When expected business value dominates a minor entry-price difference, avoid false precision; still respect position size and liquidity constraints. **Inference:** this is a behavioral lesson about execution, not a blanket endorsement of paying any price.

 ## Cross-case pattern

 Across these decisions, Fisher repeatedly (1) looked for durable growth plus capable, candid management, (2) gathered field evidence before acting, (3) accepted temporary price or earnings pain when the operating thesis remained intact, and (4) changed his mind when facts or management quality deteriorated. The accounts are valuable first-person evidence, but they are retrospective and selective; they do not constitute a complete, independently audited performance record.
