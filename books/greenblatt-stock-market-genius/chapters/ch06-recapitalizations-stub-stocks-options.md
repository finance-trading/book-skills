# Chapter 6: “Baby Needs New Shoes” Meets “Other People’s Money”—Recapitalizations and Options

## Core Idea
Recapitalizations, stub stocks, LEAPS, warrants, and options can magnify the effects of corporate change—but leverage magnifies errors too. Analyze the underlying business and transaction first; use derivative pricing gaps only when you understand the payoff, timing, and event-driven change better than a purely historical volatility model captures.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Recapitalization analysis**: Model the company before and after cash, debt, preferred securities, or shares are exchanged or distributed. Recalculate per-share equity value, debt service, and downside resilience.
- **Stub-stock valuation**: When a company distributes cash or securities but leaves a highly leveraged residual equity, subtract the distributed value and debt from total value to estimate what the remaining stub implies about the operating business.
- **Separate option leverage from business value**: A warrant or option can have substantial time value despite little intrinsic value. Determine exercise terms, expiration, dilution, and the security's value under multiple share prices.
- **Look for event-blind option assumptions**: Standard models often infer volatility from historical prices. A pending spinoff, restructuring, or merger can cause a discontinuous move unrelated to ordinary volatility; analyze event probability and timing independently.
- **Match instrument to thesis**: Prefer common stock when the valuation case does not require leverage; use options only if the event window, strike, and expiry align with the expected catalyst and risk tolerance.

## Key Concepts
- **Recapitalization**: Change in capital structure, often involving repurchases, dividends, debt, or preferred securities.
- **Stub stock**: Residual equity in a company after value is distributed or separated, often with substantial leverage.
- **LEAPS**: Long-dated listed options, providing exposure over a longer period than near-term contracts.
- **Warrant**: Company-issued right to buy shares at a specified price, often with dilution upon exercise.
- **Intrinsic value**: Amount by which an option is in the money, distinct from its remaining time value.
- **Event volatility**: Potential price movement caused by a discrete corporate event rather than ordinary historical fluctuations.

## Mental Models
A recap is a balance-sheet transformation, not free value: cash paid out is matched by changed leverage and risk. An option is a wasting, nonlinear claim on an underlying business; correctly predicting direction is insufficient if the move comes after expiry or is too small to overcome premium. Treat historical volatility as an input, not a substitute for event analysis.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using leverage without understanding balance-sheet fragility**: High debt can turn a small operating shock into permanent equity loss.
- **Buying options because they are cheap**: Low premium can reflect low probability, short time, or an unfavorable strike.
- **Confusing theoretical prices with certainty**: Models depend on assumptions and may omit corporate-event discontinuities.
- **Ignoring dilution and exercise mechanics**: Warrants and options can change the share count and ownership economics.

## Worked Example
A company with shares at $36 distributes a bond worth $30 per share. The simplified ex-distribution share value might be near $6, not $36, before considering tax, leverage, and market effects. An investor who evaluates only the pre-recap quote mistakes returned value for continuing equity. Rebuild the post-transaction balance sheet and estimate the residual business value before deciding whether the stub is attractive.

## Key Takeaways
1. Revalue the business after every capital-structure change.
2. Separate distributed value from residual equity and its debt burden.
3. Model option payoff, expiration, and event timing—not just direction.
4. Treat event-driven mispricing as a research hypothesis requiring independent valuation.
