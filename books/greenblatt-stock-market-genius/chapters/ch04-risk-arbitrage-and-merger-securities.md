# Chapter 4: Don’t Try This at Home—Risk Arbitrage and Merger Securities

## Core Idea
An announced merger spread is compensation for deal-break risk and waiting time, not a risk-free return. The analyst must estimate closing probability, loss if the deal breaks, consideration structure, and time to completion; a small spread can hide a large asymmetric loss.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Underwrite the spread**: For a cash deal, compare the target price with the offer, estimate annualized return if it closes, and model the target's likely value if it fails. Then judge whether expected return compensates for the downside and time.
- **Identify the consideration**: Cash deals expose the investor chiefly to completion and timing. Stock-for-stock deals add exchange-ratio and acquirer-price exposure; assess the hedge and terms rather than treating the announced value as fixed.
- **Map deal failure modes**: Regulatory opposition, financing, diligence findings, business deterioration, shareholder votes, and contractual conditions can prevent closing. Weigh deal-specific evidence; don't infer certainty from public announcement.
- **Use merger securities carefully**: Bonds, preferreds, warrants, or contingent instruments can have different outcomes from common stock in a transaction. Read the agreement and model each security's payoff under close, delay, and break scenarios.

## Key Concepts
- **Risk arbitrage**: Buying securities after an announced merger in expectation of earning the spread if it closes.
- **Deal spread**: Difference between the target's current price and merger consideration.
- **Break risk**: Chance the transaction fails, potentially returning the target toward its unaffected price.
- **Timing risk**: Capital remains committed until resolution; longer duration reduces annualized return and increases uncertainty.
- **Merger consideration**: Cash, stock, or other securities promised to target holders.
- **Unaffected price**: Target's market price before deal speculation or announcement, a starting point—not a guaranteed floor.

## Mental Models
View the spread as a contingent claim: the small gain on close must be weighed against the much larger possible loss on failure. A quoted dollar spread is not comparable across deals until translated into annualized return and downside exposure. Deal terms are a probability tree, not a single promised price.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling arbitrage risk-free**: A failed deal can erase years of small spreads.
- **Annualizing without downside analysis**: A high annualized figure may simply price a high probability of failure.
- **Treating stock consideration as cash**: Acquirer-price movements can change what holders actually receive.
- **Ignoring concentration and correlation**: Several deals may fail together when financing or regulation tightens.

## Worked Example
A target trades at $38 after a $40 cash offer; before announcement it traded at $25. Closing in six months yields $2, about 5.3% on capital (roughly 10.5% annualized before costs). If the deal breaks and the target returns to $25, the loss is $13, more than six times the potential gain. The key question is therefore not “Is there a $2 spread?” but “What evidence justifies the break probability and expected holding period?”

## Key Takeaways
1. Estimate both close value and break value before entering.
2. Translate spread into annualized return only after modeling delay.
3. Understand whether consideration is cash, stock, or contingent securities.
4. Read merger conditions and update the thesis as new evidence appears.
