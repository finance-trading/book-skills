# Chapter 5: Blood in the Streets (Hopefully, Not Yours)—Bankruptcy and Restructuring

## Core Idea
Bankruptcy creates both mispricing and severe risk. The most promising cases are often viable businesses burdened by excessive debt, where restructuring changes claims and ownership; distinguish business value from the securities' recovery value and understand where each claim sits in the capital structure.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Separate business failure from financial failure**: Ask whether operations are fundamentally impaired or whether an otherwise viable company cannot service its capital structure. Financial distress can create opportunity only if the business has durable value.
- **Read the capital structure from senior to junior**: Identify secured debt, unsecured debt, preferreds, and equity; estimate enterprise value and allocate it by legal priority. Do not assume old common stock survives reorganization.
- **Analyze the plan, not the label**: Review the proposed restructuring, exchange ratios, new securities, court process, and dilution. Compare likely recovery under alternatives and include uncertainty in timing.
- **Wait for evidence**: A restructuring may be announced after initial pessimism has already pushed prices down. Look for a genuine asset/value mismatch and a catalyst, not a dramatic headline alone.
- **Treat distressed securities as specialized**: Liquidity, legal complexity, and uncertain valuations demand deeper work; pass when documents or recovery mechanics are beyond your competence.

## Key Concepts
- **Chapter 11**: U.S. bankruptcy process allowing a company to reorganize under court supervision.
- **Enterprise value**: Value available to all capital providers before allocating it among debt and equity claims.
- **Absolute priority**: General principle that senior claims are paid before junior claims receive value, subject to legal outcomes and negotiated plans.
- **Recovery value**: Estimated value a specific claim receives under liquidation or reorganization.
- **Restructuring**: Reorganization of liabilities, assets, or ownership to address financial distress.
- **Distressed debt**: Debt trading at a substantial discount because payment or recovery is uncertain.

## Mental Models
A distressed company is a waterfall: estimate the total value at the top, then see which claims the value reaches. A good business can still be a bad security if the purchase price exceeds plausible recovery. Conversely, debt overhang can make a viable business's equity appear worthless until claims are renegotiated.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying common stock because the company is famous or its business seems sound**: Existing equity may be wiped out by senior claims.
- **Equating bankruptcy with cheapness**: Mispricing and land mines coexist.
- **Ignoring legal priority and dilution**: Recovery depends on the actual claim and confirmed plan, not headline enterprise value.
- **Trying to forecast every restructuring**: The author favors recognizing an attractive situation when it appears, not prospecting blindly across complex cases.

## Worked Example
Suppose a company has estimated reorganized enterprise value of $600 million, $250 million of secured claims, and $500 million of unsecured claims. Secured lenders may absorb the first $250 million; the remaining $350 million would leave unsecured creditors with an estimated 70% recovery before costs, disputes, and plan-specific adjustments. Existing equity receives nothing in this simplified waterfall. The estimate is a starting model, not a prediction: validate asset values, priority, administrative claims, and the plan.

## Key Takeaways
1. Determine whether distress is operational or primarily financial.
2. Estimate enterprise value and allocate it by claim seniority.
3. Read court filings and plan terms; old equity may be canceled.
4. Demand a clear valuation edge and stay within your expertise.
