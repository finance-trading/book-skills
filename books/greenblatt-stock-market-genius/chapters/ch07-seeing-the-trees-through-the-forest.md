# Chapter 7: Seeing the Trees Through the Forest

## Core Idea
Special-situation investing is a learned skill, not a guaranteed outcome. Build a small portfolio of situations you understand, find ideas through routine reading, verify them with company filings, and value them using cash generation rather than headline earnings.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Portfolio construction by instrument risk**:
  - A diversified group of spinoffs can be appropriate; a portfolio composed only of LEAPS is not.
  - Avoid concentrating in one industry unless you have genuine expertise.
  - Use modest borrowing only with substantial experience; margin can force sales at the wrong time.
  - A beginner can limit special situations to a small share of assets and increase it with experience.
  - At roughly one attractive idea every two or three months, a respectable portfolio can develop over about a year.
  - Keep LEAPS exposure generally below 10–15% because of leverage.
  - A time-constrained investor may pair a diversified statistical-value portfolio with special situations representing about 20–30%.
- **Idea-finding process**: Read the Wall Street Journal and selected business publications for spinoffs, bankruptcies, mergers, and unusual consideration. Use newsletters and fund prospectuses only as leads, then do independent research. Quality matters more than idea volume.
- **Primary-document map**:
  - **10-K / 10-Q**: business, results, and financial statements.
  - **Proxy (Schedule 14A)**: ownership, options, and compensation.
  - **8-K**: material events.
  - **Registration statements / S-4**: new securities, mergers, exchanges, recaps, and restructurings.
  - **Form 10**: spinoff information.
  - **13D / 13G**: large-holder stakes and intentions.
  - **14D-1, 13E-3, 13E-4**: tender offers, going-private transactions, and issuer self-tenders.
- **Free-cash-flow test**: Start with net income, add depreciation and amortization, and subtract capital expenditures. Compare the result with earnings over several years. Distinguish maintenance spending from growth spending and do not penalize justified expansion merely because it depresses current free cash flow.

## Key Concepts
- **Free cash flow**: Cash available after estimated capital investment, useful for debt reduction, dividends, repurchases, acquisitions, or new opportunities.
- **Depreciation / amortization**: Noncash accounting allocations; compare them with actual replacement and intangible-asset economics.
- **Maintenance vs growth capital expenditure**: Spending required to sustain operations versus spending to expand a viable business.
- **Statistical value portfolio**: A diversified group selected by measures such as low price relative to book value and cash flow, used as a complement rather than a replacement for research.
- **Margin of safety**: A discount large enough to absorb analytical and business uncertainty.

## Mental Models
Treat publications and investor portfolios as prospecting maps, not instructions. Most ideas should fail the understanding, downside, or upside tests. If a transaction moves too quickly for research, it is probably outside the individual investor's process. Accounting earnings and cash can diverge for legitimate or misleading reasons; the multi-year pattern matters.

## Anti-patterns
- **Substituting a newsletter or famous investor's holding for research**: It is only a starting point.
- **Building a whole portfolio from options**: Leverage can dominate portfolio risk even if each idea looks attractive.
- **Using one year's free cash flow mechanically**: Growth investment, inflation, competition, and depreciation schedules can distort it.
- **Chasing situations that require immediate action**: Insufficient time to understand the situation removes the intended edge.

## Worked Example
A company reports $20 of net income, $6 of depreciation, and $3 of amortization, then spends $5 on capital equipment. Greenblatt's basic free-cash-flow calculation is $20 + $6 + $3 − $5 = $24. If this relationship persists, valuation may reasonably use free cash flow rather than earnings. If capital spending persistently exceeds depreciation because facilities must be replaced or competitive renovations are mandatory, the lower cash figure may be more conservative. If the excess spending is demonstrably growth investment in a successful business, it need not imply weak earnings power.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start small, diversify by situation and industry, and limit options and margin.
2. Read broadly, but investigate only a few understandable opportunities.
3. Use company filings as primary evidence and secondary sources for context.
4. Compare earnings with multi-year free cash flow and identify why they differ.
