# Chapter 3: Chips Off the Old Stock—Spinoffs, Partial Spinoffs, and Rights Offerings

## Core Idea
Spinoffs can create mispricing because shareholders receive a business they never chose, while institutional mandates, forced selling, limited coverage, or temporary complexity suppress demand. The event is only a promising hunting ground: value the parent and child independently and check whether incentives and economics support the thesis.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Look for forced or indifferent sellers**: A distribution creates supply from investors who bought the parent, not the new security. Use this as a catalyst for research, not as proof of cheapness.
- **The spinoff checklist**: Look for (1) a forced or indifferent shareholder base, (2) insider incentives and meaningful ownership in the new company, and (3) a newly revealed or created investment opportunity. The more factors present, the stronger the case.
- **Study both sides of the split**: A complex or loss-making division can hide value in the parent; the spinoff may also have attractive standalone economics. Compare each separately and consider the combined implied valuation.
- **Read the distribution documents**: Form 10, proxy materials, and transaction disclosures explain capital structure, allocation, management incentives, and the intended separation. Test management's projections rather than accepting them.
- **Rights offerings**: Determine subscription price, rights ratio, transferability, deadlines, dilution, and fully diluted ownership. An unattractive headline can conceal favorable optionality, but only when the documents support it.

## Key Concepts
- **Spinoff**: A subsidiary or division becomes an independent company and shares are distributed to the parent's shareholders.
- **Partial spinoff**: The parent distributes only part of a subsidiary, retaining a continuing stake.
- **Forced seller**: An investor whose mandate, portfolio or preference leads to selling regardless of the security's intrinsic value.
- **Rights offering**: Existing holders receive rights to buy additional shares, usually at a specified price and ratio.
- **Parent-company discount**: The market may undervalue remaining operations when a complex or troubled division obscures them.
- **Insider alignment**: Meaningful ownership or compensation tied to the new company's success can support—but never guarantee—shareholder-oriented decisions.

## Mental Models
Treat the transaction as a temporary supply-demand shock layered over two businesses that need separate valuation. Compare the standalone value of each entity with the market-implied value before and after distribution. Think of the parent as a second opportunity: separating a troublesome unit may reveal that the continuing business was priced cheaply all along.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying every spinoff**: The event creates a research lead, not a margin of safety.
- **Relying on management projections alone**: Check assumptions against assets, debt, peers, cash flows, and incentives.
- **Ignoring the parent**: The best bargain may be the company left behind.
- **Overlooking transaction details**: Debt allocation, retained ownership, tax treatment, or rights terms can change the economics materially.

## Worked Example
For a hypothetical parent trading at $50, suppose it distributes a subsidiary whose shares initially trade at $12. The parent then trades at $32. The market has effectively priced the parent’s remaining operations at $38 immediately before distribution ($50 less $12), but at $32 afterward. The $6 difference is a prompt to investigate whether the continuing business is misunderstood—not an automatic buy. Compare its normalized cash generation, liabilities, and peers, then assess whether the new subsidiary's holders are likely to sell indiscriminately.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat spinoffs as a repeatable screening category, not a ready-made investment conclusion.
2. Identify why recipients may sell without regard to value.
3. Evaluate the incentives, capital structure, and economics of both parent and child.
4. Read the transaction filings; the details often contain the edge.
5. Search the parent for hidden value as carefully as the new security.
