# Chapter 13: 巨大的危机

## Core Idea
A financial crisis requires facts, communication, and qualified help. Fear is reduced by focusing on practical next steps and support, while pride or secrecy can delay necessary action.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Crisis triage**: Identify the immediate obligation, deadline, consequences, and available sources of legitimate help before making promises.
- **Fear-to-plan shift**: Name the feared outcome, then list actions that can change it and people qualified to assist.
- **Keep learning after success**: Continue recording lessons so confidence does not become complacency.

## Key Concepts
- **Default risk**: Missing contractual payments can trigger penalties or loss of collateral, depending on the agreement.
- **Qualified advice**: Complex debt or housing problems may require a lender, adviser, or legal professional.
- **Support network**: Trusted adults can help evaluate options that a child cannot responsibly manage.

## Mental Models
- Separate “what has happened” from “what might happen.”
- Ask who has authority and expertise before offering money or assurances.
- Use fear as a signal to prepare, not as proof that action is impossible.

## Anti-patterns
- **Catastrophic rumination**: Repeating worst-case stories without checking options.
- **Children solving adult debt**: A minor should not assume responsibility for parents’ legal obligations.
- **Stopping review after early success**: Progress can conceal new risks.

## Worked Example
Parents receive a warning after missing housing payments and imagine losing their home. The protagonist seeks an experienced adult’s help. In real life, the household should read the notice, contact the lender promptly, document finances, and obtain appropriate professional advice rather than rely on an informal rescue.

## Key Takeaways
1. Respond to warnings with facts and deadlines.
2. Use qualified help for serious debt or housing risk.
3. Keep learning so confidence does not turn into complacency.
