## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Make a specific wish actionable
Translate vague wanting into a small number of concrete, meaningful goals. Picture the desired outcome to keep attention on relevant opportunities, then pair the picture with an immediate action and regular savings. Visualization is a motivator and attention cue, not a substitute for resources, planning, or probability.

### Confidence through evidence
Keep a success journal: record real completed actions and what enabled them. Use it to challenge helpless assumptions and identify repeatable strengths. The book's confidence loop is practical: notice capability → attempt a useful action → observe results → strengthen confidence. Do not turn it into denial of constraints or guaranteed-outcome thinking.

### Start from assets you have
Look for a solvable problem among what you know, can do, enjoy, and can access. Offer a service, ask potential customers, deliver reliably, then improve or delegate repeatable work. Charge for value and outcomes where appropriate, not only hours; account for costs, safety, permission, and fair treatment.

### Protect the seed: the “golden goose”
Separate earned money by purpose. In the story's illustrative allocation, 50% builds the long-term capital base (“goose”), 40% funds named goals, and 10% remains discretionary. Percentages are a teaching example, not a universal budget. Start with a sustainable amount and adapt to essentials, debt terms, local conditions, and personal circumstances. Capital creates future options; spending all income keeps work dependent on the next paycheck.

### Make progress small and persistent
Use a fixed, brief daily practice for important long-term aims, even when urgent distractions arise. The book's “72-hour rule” says to take a first concrete step within 72 hours of deciding; interpret it as an anti-procrastination prompt, not a deadline for consequential financial commitments. Build routines around saving, learning, and review.

### Debt requires liquidity and a plan
The narrative warns against revolving credit and consumer borrowing, and challenges aggressive repayments that leave no cash buffer. It proposes saving half of surplus after living costs while directing the other half to consumer debt, alongside a “do I really need this?” pause. This is not a safe rule for every loan: compare actual interest, fees, required minimums, penalties, collateral, and emergency reserves; never miss contractual payments. Seek qualified help for serious debt.

### Investing: understandable, diversified, patient
The club's checklist is: safety, growth potential, and simplicity. The story introduces shares, funds, diversification, volatility, time horizon, and the Rule of 72. Long-term investing still carries market, inflation, currency, fee, and behavioral risks; returns are uncertain and past performance does not establish future results. Keep near-term spending needs and emergency cash out of volatile assets. Do not read the book's historical return assumptions as forecasts or recommendations.

### Courage is action despite fear
Fear often grows when attention fixates on failure. Name the risk, prepare, seek trusted guidance, then take a proportionate next step. Courage is not recklessness: the story itself shows that children entering a dangerous property should call adults/police rather than investigate.
