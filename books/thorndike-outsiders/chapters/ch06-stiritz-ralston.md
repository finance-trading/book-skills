# Chapter 6: A Public LBO — Bill Stiritz and Ralston Purina

## Core Idea
Stiritz treated a stable consumer-products company like an owner with limited capital: sell low-return legacy units, apply prudent leverage to predictable cash flows, and use the proceeds for high-return acquisitions, spinoffs, and repurchases.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The public LBO**: Borrow against predictable cash generation and actively reshape a public company's asset and share base while retaining public ownership.
  - When to use: Cash flows are durable, assets can be sold at reasonable prices, and the debt load remains serviceable under a conservative case.
  - How: Remove low-return businesses, preserve core operations, set debt limits, compare each new use of cash with own-share repurchases, and reduce debt when appropriate.
  - Failure mode: Leverage without reliable cash flow or an asset-sale plan makes a capital allocation strategy fragile.
- **Repurchase return as acquisition benchmark**: Stiritz passed on a deal unless its expected return meaningfully exceeded what buying Ralston shares could earn.
- **Spinoff as ultimate decentralization**: Separate a business when independent capital decisions, management incentives, and transparency will be better than inside a conglomerate.

## Key Concepts
- **Debt capacity**: Borrowing that a business can support through downturns, not the largest amount a lender offers.
- **Spinoff**: Distribution of a subsidiary's shares to the parent company's owners; it changes who makes capital decisions without necessarily selling the asset for cash.
- **Asset pruning**: Selling or closing units whose marginal returns lag alternatives.
- **Internal rate of return (IRR)**: A time-sensitive measure for comparing investment and buyback outcomes.
- **Public LBO**: Thorndike's label for deploying leverage and portfolio reshaping in a listed company, distinct from a private-equity buyout.

## Mental Models
- Ask **what the asset is worth to the next owner** and what its proceeds can earn elsewhere; ancestral status is irrelevant to the calculation.
- Treat predictable cash flow as an asset but stress its durability before increasing debt.

## Anti-patterns
- **Protecting the legacy portfolio**: Holding a low-return feed or other noncore unit because of corporate history sacrifices reinvestment capacity.
- **Acquisition by habit**: A deal whose return falls below the buyback alternative does not become attractive because it grows reported sales.
- **Debt as a free amplifier**: More leverage magnifies both gains and mistakes.

## Worked Example
Stiritz divested less attractive businesses as Ralston shifted toward branded products, then used cash flow and debt for selective acquisitions, notably Energizer and Continental, and repurchases. The book says he eventually repurchased about 60% of Ralston's shares, with a historical long-term IRR of about 13% on those purchases. He also spun off businesses so their managers and investors could see their own returns more clearly. The book reports pretax margins moving from 9% to 15% and ROE from 15% to 37% as the mix changed. Its 19-year shareholder-return comparison is 20.0% annually for Ralston, 17.7% for peers, and 14.7% for the S&P 500. None of these outcomes is a safe default for a current leveraged company.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compare every acquisition with the return available from own shares.
2. Sell low-return legacy assets when another owner values them more highly.
3. Use spinoffs when separate governance can improve allocation and accountability.
4. Size debt against cash-flow downside, not historical average conditions alone.
