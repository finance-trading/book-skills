# Chapter 2: An Unconventional Conglomerateur — Henry Singleton and Teledyne

## Core Idea
Singleton treated a company's own shares as an investment competing with every acquisition. He issued expensive shares when they financed attractive businesses, later bought shares back in large blocks when cheap, and changed course when prices changed.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Large, opportunistic tender offers**: Repurchase meaningful blocks of stock during depressed valuations instead of mechanically buying a small amount every quarter.
  - When to use: Intrinsic value exceeds the tender price by a defensible margin and the balance sheet can withstand the financing.
  - How: Compare the return on own shares with external investments; choose a meaningful size; use cash or debt only within a conservative stress case; stop when the price no longer offers a good return.
  - Failure mode: Price support, dilution-offset buybacks, or debt-funded buying at an inflated price can destroy per-share value.
- **Issue high, repurchase low**: Treat the equity currency symmetrically. Singleton's average P/E on shares issued to acquire businesses exceeded 25; the average multiple on his later repurchases was below eight, according to the book.
- **Extreme decentralization**: Break a conglomerate into accountable units led by operators; keep corporate overhead and integration ambitions limited.

## Key Concepts
- **Tender offer**: A time-limited offer to buy a substantial number of shares at stated terms.
- **Equity currency**: Shares issued as consideration; their valuation affects the real cost of an acquisition.
- **Per-share value**: Value attributable to each remaining share, which can grow even when the company itself does not expand.
- **Free cash flow**: Cash available after operating and investment needs; Singleton emphasized it over reported earnings.
- **Opportunity cost**: The return forgone when capital goes into one option instead of its best alternative.

## Mental Models
- Use the own-stock return as a **hurdle for external deals**; if the company is materially undervalued, an acquisition must beat buying its shares.
- Think of investor communication and organizational design as part of the allocation system: shareholders with a long horizon allow lumpy, unpopular decisions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Steady buybacks regardless of valuation**: A fixed calendar ignores the economics that make repurchases attractive.
- **Conglomerate synergy theater**: Centralizing unrelated businesses for promised synergies can displace accountable managers and raise cost.
- **Dividend dogma**: A regular payout can be less attractive than cheap shares or high-return projects; it becomes appropriate when better uses disappear.

## Worked Example
Teledyne made eight major repurchase offers during Singleton's tenure. In one 1980 tender, more than 20% of shares were tendered; he accepted all and financed the purchase with fixed-rate debt after judging Teledyne's own stock attractive relative to other uses of capital. The book reports more than $2.5 billion spent on buybacks and a 42% compound annual return across the tenders, a historical calculation that should not be generalized to ordinary buybacks. By 1987, when both acquisition prices and Teledyne shares were high, Singleton declared Teledyne's first dividend in 26 years as a public company. The change of tool followed the change in opportunity set. The book reports 20.4% annual shareholder returns from 1963–1990, versus 8.0% for the S&P 500 and 11.6% for conglomerate peers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make repurchases an investment decision with price, expected return, and funding limits.
2. Compare stock issuance and buybacks through the same valuation lens.
3. Change the payout mix when prices and opportunities change; consistency of method matters more than consistency of action.
4. Keep operating accountability close to the business and allocation authority near the capital owner.
