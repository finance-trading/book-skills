# Decision Cheatsheet — The Outsiders

## Capital Allocation Gate

| If you see... | Decide by... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A proposed “strategic” acquisition | Conservative after-tax return versus hurdle **and** own-share return | Narrative and size do not establish owner gains |
| A low stock price | Value per share, balance-sheet stress, and alternative returns | A buyback is an investment only below defensible value |
| Excess cash with no qualifying project | Cash reserve need, debt reduction, then dividend | Retaining cash without a use invites poor deals |
| Structurally weaker core demand | Forward unit returns versus sale/closure proceeds | Shrinking may release more value than defending scale |
| Large local operating footprint | Local manager accountability plus CEO-led capital choices | Delegated operations and centralized allocation fit different information |

## Ten Questions Before Committing Capital

Adapted from the book's Epilogue checklist; use with current financing and tax advice.

1. Is the CEO personally accountable for the allocation decision?
2. What is the minimum acceptable return, and how does it compare with cost of capital and alternatives?
3. What do conservative return-and-risk estimates show for **all** internal and external options?
4. What return would buying the company's own shares offer at today's price? Does the acquisition clearly beat it?
5. What changes after tax, including losses, interest, and the form of consideration?
6. What cash reserve and debt level remain safe in a downside case?
7. Could local managers make operating decisions faster with less central staff?
8. Will retained earnings plausibly earn more than the hurdle over time?
9. If no project qualifies, would a dividend serve owners, given its tax and reversal costs?
10. At an unusually high sale price, should an asset or the company be sold? Should a failing unit close?

## Worked Choice from the Epilogue

| Bakery expansion | Upfront cost | Estimated annual profit | Simple profit/cost return | Decision issue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Add adjacent space and an oven | $100,000 | $20,000 | 20% | Meets the baker's illustrative 20% hurdle |
| Open another store and buy an oven | $200,000 | $50,000–$75,000 | 25%–37.5% | Higher estimated return, but more demand uncertainty and financing need |

The author does **not** select the second store automatically. Test forecast confidence, whether the extra return compensates for risk, the cost of an additional $100,000 in capital, and possible diversification and future option value. These are illustrative book numbers and simple annual profit/cost ratios, not a full discounted-cash-flow calculation or universal 20% hurdle.

## Fast Smells

- Revenue grows while value **per share** falls: inspect acquisition prices and share issuance.
- Buybacks continue at high valuations: recalculate expected repurchase return.
- Detailed deal decks omit a clear hurdle or conservative case: demand a one-page return bridge.
- Headquarters expands faster than operating units: ask what central spending earns.
- Managers cite peer activity as the thesis: pause and redo the arithmetic.
