# Chapter 9: Business Valuation and Corporate Restructuring

## Core Idea
A business is worth the higher of what its assets would fetch in liquidation and what its future free cash flows are worth as a going concern — and most M&A and restructuring is a fight over who captures the gap between value and control.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fair market value (FMV)** = max(**liquidation value**, **going-concern value**). Going-concern value = present value of future free cash flow; when it falls below liquidation value, value is literally being destroyed by continuing.
  - When to use: as the master valuation frame before any more precise method.
- **Discounted-cash-flow (DCF) valuation**: value the whole firm from its free cash flow = EBIT(1 − t) + depreciation − capital expenditures − increase in working capital, discounted at the weighted-average cost of capital.
  - When to use: for going concerns with forecastable cash flows.
  - How: project FCF, discount at WACC, subtract debt to get equity value.
- **Comparable multiples**: value a firm by applying the P/E, EV/EBIT(DA), or other multiples observed on similar companies.
  - When to use: as a market-relative cross-check on DCF.
- **Minority vs. control value**: control (the power to redirect cash flows and assets) is worth a premium over a passive minority share, so acquisition value ≠ share price.
- **The market for corporate control**: why a buyer pays a premium (synergies, tax shields, improved incentives, discipline of free cash flow), and how to compute the maximum rational price.

## Key Concepts
- **Free cash flow (FCF)** — the cash available to all capital providers after necessary reinvestment; the true engine of value.
- **Liquidation value vs. going-concern value** — break-up proceeds vs. discounted operating cash flow.
- **WACC** — the discount rate reflecting the blend of debt and equity financing.
- **Synergy** — value created only by combining two firms (cost savings, revenue, tax shields).
- **LBO (leveraged buyout)** — acquisition financed heavily with debt, using the target's cash flow to service it.
- **Restructuring motives** — increased tax shields, sharper management incentives, and restoring shareholder control of free cash flow.

## Mental Models
- Anchor every valuation in the **liquidation vs. going-concern test** before choosing a method.
- Think of the value of a firm as the **present value of free cash flow**, and every other method as a shortcut to it.
- Separate **ownership from control** — a minority share is worth less than a controlling stake, which is why premiums exist.

## Anti-patterns
- **Valuing on a single multiple** without reconciling to DCF; multiples embed the market's mood, not just fundamentals.
- **Confusing a market cap with fair value**: price is what informed traders transact at, which differs from intrinsic value when control or synergies change hands.
- **Destroying value by ignoring the going-concern floor**: if liquidation value exceeds going-concern value, continued operation destroys wealth.
- **Paying an acquisition premium for synergies that never materialize**; the buyer's maximum price must be net of integration costs and risk.

## Worked Example
The chapter opens on **Microsoft's $26.2B acquisition of LinkedIn at $196/share** against a pre-bid price of $131, posing the real questions: why would a rational buyer pay 50% over market, and why didn't LinkedIn's own managers capture that value? The answer in the framework: Microsoft paid for **control** and expected **synergies + strategic option value**, not for the standalone cash-flow value that the market price already reflected. Working the FMV logic: standalone value = PV of LinkedIn's expected free cash flow ≈ the $131 market price; the $196 bid = that standalone value *plus* a control premium for synergies. The reusable lesson — **acquisition price = standalone DCF value + PV of synergies**, and the buyer should never exceed that sum — is the chapter's core discipline for the market for corporate control.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with **FMV = max(liquidation, going-concern)**; the gap is value being created or destroyed.
2. Value the going concern as **PV of free cash flow discounted at WACC**.
3. Use **comparable multiples** only as a cross-check, not a substitute for DCF.
4. Distinguish **minority value from control value**; the premium is the price of the synergies you can actually deliver.
5. Never pay more than **standalone value + PV of synergies** when acquiring.
