# Chapter 2: Evaluating Financial Performance

## Core Idea
Return on equity is the master summary of financial performance, and it decomposes into exactly three management levers — profit margin, asset turnover, and financial leverage — so "better performance" always means moving one of those three.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Determinants of ROE (DuPont decomposition)**:
  `ROE = Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage`
  where margin = Net income / Sales, turnover = Sales / Assets, leverage = Assets / Equity.
  - When to use: to diagnose *why* ROE is high or low, or to predict the ROE impact of a proposed change.
  - How: multiply the three ratios and confirm they cancel to ROE; attribute any ROE move to one lever.
- **"Many paths to heaven"**: ten diverse businesses can earn the same ROE via very different mixes of margin, turnover, and leverage.
  - When to use: to avoid judging one "correct" way to make money — a discounter (low margin, high turnover) and a luxury firm (high margin, low turnover) can be equal performers.
- **Control ratios**: turnover computed asset-by-asset (receivables, inventory, fixed assets) to find where cash is stuck.
  - When to use: when aggregate asset turnover hides a specific runaway asset.
- **Is ROE a reliable yardstick?** — ROE has two well-known flaws: the *timing problem* (point-in-time measurement) and the *risk problem* (leverage inflates ROE at the cost of risk).

## Key Concepts
- **Return on equity (ROE)** — net income ÷ shareholders' equity.
- **Profit margin** — net income per dollar of sales; summarizes income-statement performance.
- **Asset turnover** — sales per dollar of assets; summarizes management of the asset side.
- **Financial leverage** — assets per dollar of equity; summarizes management of the financing side.
- **Current ratio / acid test** — short-term liquidity; a company's capacity to pay claims due within a year.
- **Coverage ratio** — earnings available to pay a fixed obligation (e.g., interest) relative to that obligation.
- **Self-liquidating loan** — a loan whose use automatically creates its repayment source (current assets shrink in a downturn, releasing cash).

## Mental Models
- Read each DuPont lever against its statement: **margin = income statement**, **turnover = asset side**, **leverage = liabilities/equity side**.
- Think of current assets as an **accordion** — they expand with sales (absorbing cash) and contract in downturns (releasing cash).
- Use **turnover as a diagnostic**: a bloated receivables or inventory balance shows up as a falling control ratio before it shows up as a loss.

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing ROE with pure leverage**: leverage raises ROE but also the range of possible outcomes — it is a risk decision disguised as a performance number.
- **Ignoring the timing problem**: a year-end ROE snapshot can flatter a firm whose performance deteriorated mid-year.
- **Comparing ROE across different capital structures without adjusting**: a highly levered firm's "superior" ROE may simply be more risk.

## Worked Example
Polaris's 2020 ROE is reconstructed in the text as:
`28.9% = 5.824% × 1.444 × 3.432`
i.e. a profit margin of 5.824%, asset turnover of 1.444, and leverage of 3.432. The point is that the three levers multiply back exactly to the reported ROE, and Polaris's figure is *not* high margin but healthy margin plus substantial leverage. This decomposition is the template: take any firm, compute the three ratios, and you can name precisely which lever is doing the work.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start any performance review from **ROE = margin × turnover × leverage**.
2. Name which of the three levers explains the number before forming an opinion.
3. Ratios are diagnostics: a ratio is only meaningful against a trend, a peer, or a target.
4. Treat leverage separately — it is the one lever that simultaneously raises return *and* risk.
5. Break asset turnover into control ratios to localize a cash problem.
