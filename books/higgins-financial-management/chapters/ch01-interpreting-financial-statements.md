# Chapter 1: Interpreting Financial Statements

## Core Idea
Financial statements are a window onto a company's operations: the cash the business consumes and generates is driven by the working capital cycle and the investment cycle, and profit is not cash flow.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Cash Cycle (Working Capital Cycle)**: cash → inventory → accounts receivable → cash. Every item passing through the business takes a slice of fixed-asset value (depreciation) and financing.
  - When to use: whenever you need to explain *why* profit and cash diverge, or why a growing firm runs short of cash.
  - How: trace cash through the acquisition of assets, production/sale, collection of receivables, and reinvestment.
- **The Investment Cycle**: fixed assets are consumed to produce goods; to maintain capacity and finance growth the firm must reinvest fresh cash in new fixed assets.
  - When to use: to see that a company's productive capacity is a recurring cash demand, not a one-time cost.
- **Profits ≠ Cash Flow**: cash, and the timely conversion of cash into inventory, receivables, and back again, is the lifeblood — profit alone does not tell you whether the firm is solvent.
  - When to use: before concluding a profitable firm is "healthy" or a loss-making firm is "sick."

## Key Concepts
- **Working capital** — current assets minus current liabilities; the net cash tied up in the operating cycle.
- **Depreciation** — the accountant's recognition of fixed-asset value being transferred into inventory/COGS over time (a non-cash expense).
- **Liquidity vs. solvency** — liquidity is the ability to meet near-term obligations; solvency is the ability to meet long-term obligations.
- **Sources and uses of funds** — a fund-flow view: increases in liabilities/equity and decreases in assets are *sources* of cash; increases in assets and decreases in liabilities/equity are *uses*.
- **Going concern / "growing broke"** — a profitable, rapidly growing firm can still fail if it outruns its financing.

## Mental Models
- Think of the balance sheet as **the beginning balance plus the cumulative effect of every transaction** since inception.
- Use the **fund-flow lens** ("a reduction in an asset account is a source of cash") to read any set of statements.
- Think of every asset as a wager that the future cash it throws off will exceed what it cost.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading profit as cash**: a firm can report rising net income while burning cash on receivables and inventory — and miss payroll.
- **Equating the bank balance with success**: cash can rise from borrowing, not from operations.
- **Ignoring the timing of cash conversion**: slow receivables or bloated inventory silently consume cash.

## Worked Example
The book builds **Worldwide Sports (WWS)**, a startup retailer, transaction by transaction through 2021. Beginning cash is $250K; the firm buys fixtures and inventory, makes credit sales of $875K, pays wages, buys more merchandise, records depreciation, pays interest and taxes — ten ledger entries in all — and ends with cash $175K, receivables $25K, inventory $110K, fixed assets $45K, against payables $20K, a $100K loan, and owners' equity $235K.

Two lessons fall out of the ledger: (1) the accounting identity `Assets = Liabilities + Equity` holds after *every* transaction, and (2) owners' equity rose $85K over the year (plus owner salary) — a "decent" first year that is only visible once you reconcile profit to the movement of cash through the cycle. This single worked-through table is the chapter's anchor for converting a raw set of transactions into a balance sheet.

## Key Takeaways
1. Always reconstruct the **cash cycle** (cash → inventory → receivables → cash) before judging financial health.
2. State sources and uses of funds explicitly; increases in assets consume cash, decreases release it.
3. Profit and cash flow are different numbers measured on different bases — reconcile them, don't assume.
4. Rapid growth can make a profitable company *insolvent*; watch the cash it absorbs, not just the profit it books.
5. A balance sheet is the running total of every transaction since inception, so anomalies trace back to a specific entry.
