# Chapter 3: Financial Forecasting

## Core Idea
Project what the future financial statements *will* look like by assuming the ratios that have been stable in the past remain stable — the percent-of-sales method — and the pro-forma balance sheet automatically reveals the financing the firm will need.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Percent-of-sales forecasting**: hold stable relationships (days' sales in receivables, payables period, inventory turnover, cost ratios) constant, then let forecast sales drive every line item.
  - When to use: whenever sales drive the balance sheet — i.e., for an operating (non-financial) firm growing at a steady pace.
  - How: (1) forecast sales; (2) express every balance-sheet/income item as a % or days-sales driver; (3) project pro-forma statements; (4) the plug that balances is *external funding required*.
- **Pro-forma statements & the "plug"**: the projected balance sheet won't balance on its own; the gap is the external financing need.
  - When to use: to answer "how much outside money do we need this year?"
- **Cash-flow forecast**: project cash directly (receipts and disbursements) rather than accrual income, because lenders care about cash, not profit.
  - When to use: to set a borrowing limit or test whether the firm can service a loan.

## Key Concepts
- **External funding required (EFR)** — the residual that makes pro-forma assets equal pro-forma liabilities plus equity.
- **Stable patterns / ratio analysis** — the historical ratios (days' sales, payables period, turnover) you must verify before extrapolating.
- **Days' sales / collection period** — receivables expressed in days of sales; a leading indicator of tightening liquidity.
- **Capital budget** — planned fixed-asset spending, added separately to net fixed assets.
- **Sensitivity analysis** — re-running the forecast under alternative assumptions to see how robust the answer is.

## Mental Models
- Think of forecasting as **extrapolating ratios, not absolute numbers** — the dollar amounts follow the ratios.
- Read a declining cash-days' or rising payables-days' trend as **the firm silently using suppliers to fund growth**.
- Treat the pro-forma "plug" as a *question*, not an answer: it tells you how much financing the plan implies.

## Anti-patterns
- **Extrapolating without checking stability**: ratios drift (margins compress, payables stretch), so blindly projecting the past overstates or understates the need.
- **Forecasting sales that outrun financing**: growth consumes cash — projection must pair the sales line with its financing source.
- **Presenting a single point forecast** as certain; always label the assumptions and stress them.

## Worked Example
The chapter walks through **R&E Supplies**, a plumbing/electrical wholesaler whose president asks the bank for a $500K loan "because it seemed about right." The lending officer instead builds a pro-forma 2022 statement. Ratio analysis of 2018–2021 first reveals the warning signs: cash and securities fell from 22 to 7 days' sales while payables stretched from 39 to 66 days, and COGS + SG&A grew faster than sales — earnings aren't keeping pace. Projecting forward, the pro-forma balance sheet shows **external funding required of $1,380K** (current assets of $7,353K plus net fixed assets of $280K = $7,633K assets, against $6,253K of projected liabilities + equity). The answer isn't the round $500K the owner guessed — it's nearly triple — and it forces the conversation onto how the financing will actually be sourced rather than a gut number.

## Key Takeaways
1. Forecast **ratios first**, then let them generate the dollar amounts.
2. The pro-forma balance-sheet plug *is* the financing need — compute it, don't guess it.
3. Verify which ratios have been stable before projecting them forward.
4. Present a cash-flow forecast alongside an accrual forecast; lenders underwire cash, not profit.
5. Stress the forecast under alternative assumptions before committing to a financing plan.
