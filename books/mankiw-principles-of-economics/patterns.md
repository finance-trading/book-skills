# Applied Patterns

These are procedures extracted from Mankiw's models. Each requires the chapter's assumptions before use.

## Marginal choice (Ch 1)
**When to use**: Choosing one more unit of time, output, spending, or abatement.
**How**: Name the best forgone alternative; calculate the marginal benefit and full marginal cost of the next unit; expand while benefit exceeds cost.
**Trade-offs**: Average benefits and sunk costs can mislead. Unpriced external effects require a social, not private, comparison.

## Comparative-advantage allocation (Ch 3)
**When to use**: Deciding who should produce which of two goods or tasks.
**How**: Calculate each producer's opportunity cost in units of the other good; assign specialization by lower relative cost; choose a trade ratio between the two costs.
**Trade-offs**: Absolute productivity does not determine the assignment. Distributional gains depend on terms of trade.

## Three-step equilibrium shift (Ch 4)
**When to use**: Explaining a market event's direction of price and quantity.
**How**: Classify demand or supply shift; determine direction; compare new and old intersections. Add both curves if both move.
**Trade-offs**: Simultaneous shifts can leave one outcome unknown. Elasticity is needed for magnitudes.

## Midpoint elasticity and revenue (Ch 5)
**When to use**: Estimating proportional quantity response or a price change's revenue direction.
**How**: Divide change in quantity by its two-point average, and change in price by its two-point average; divide the percentages. Compare demand elasticity with one for the local revenue effect.
**Trade-offs**: Market definition and time horizon change the result.

## Tax incidence and welfare ledger (Ch 6–8)
**When to use**: Reviewing a per-unit tax.
**How**: Draw buyer and seller prices separated by the tax; find traded quantity; assign more burden to the less elastic side; compare consumer surplus, producer surplus, and revenue before and after.
**Trade-offs**: Lost beneficial trades are deadweight loss, separate from the revenue transfer.

## Externality correction (Ch 10)
**When to use**: Production or use harms or benefits third parties.
**How**: Estimate marginal external cost or benefit; compare private and social curves; evaluate a corrective price, permit quantity, regulation, or bargaining.
**Trade-offs**: Information, enforcement, and transaction costs may alter the preferred tool.

## Goods classification (Ch 11)
**When to use**: Diagnosing free riding or overuse.
**How**: Ask whether nonpayers can be excluded and whether one use subtracts from another. Public goods need collective-benefit assessment; common resources need depletion controls.
**Trade-offs**: Ownership alone does not settle the classification.

## Firm output and participation (Ch 14–18)
**When to use**: Pricing and production decisions.
**How**: Derive marginal and average costs; for a price taker set `P = MC`, then check `P ≥ AVC` for temporary production and `P ≥ ATC` for long-run survival. For monopoly choose `MR = MC` then read price from demand; for oligopoly model rival reactions.
**Trade-offs**: Sunk costs, entry, differentiation, and strategic defection change the answer.

## Wage-gap audit (Ch 19–21)
**When to use**: Interpreting earnings differences.
**How**: Trace product demand to factor demand; compare skill, job conditions, experience, hours, and institutions; assess remaining gap and data limitations.
**Trade-offs**: Observational controls neither prove nor disprove discrimination completely.

## Consumer budget and substitution (Ch 22)
**When to use**: Predicting demand after a price or wage change.
**How**: Draw the budget line; find the highest affordable indifference curve; separate substitution from purchasing-power effects.
**Trade-offs**: Opposing effects can make net response ambiguous.

## GDP and inflation normalization (Ch 24–25)
**When to use**: Comparing output or money values over time.
**How**: Count domestic final production once; classify `C + I + G + NX`; use real GDP for quantity; convert old dollars with an appropriate price-index ratio.
**Trade-offs**: CPI and GDP deflator cover different baskets and both have measurement limits.

## Growth-policy channel (Ch 26–27)
**When to use**: Assessing a claim about long-run living standards.
**How**: Trace the policy to physical capital, human capital, resources, or knowledge; judge productivity per worker; distinguish a level gain from sustained growth.
**Trade-offs**: Capital accumulation faces diminishing returns; institutions affect transmission.

## Present-value comparison (Ch 28)
**When to use**: Comparing cash flows at different dates.
**How**: Discount each future flow by `(1+r)^N` with a rate matching timing and risk; compare at one date; examine uncertainty and diversification separately.
**Trade-offs**: A small rate change can materially alter long-dated values.

## Open-economy two-market chain (Ch 32–33)
**When to use**: Interpreting trade balance, fiscal shock, or exchange-rate move.
**How**: Write `S = I + NCO` and `NX = NCO`; solve loanable-funds rate first, then net capital outflow, currency-market supply, real exchange rate, and net exports.
**Trade-offs**: Currency response can offset the direct trade effect of a tariff.

## AD–AS shock classification (Ch 34–36)
**When to use**: Analyzing a recession or inflation shock.
**How**: Identify demand or supply shock; shift AD or SRAS; track output, price level, and unemployment; then consider expectations and long-run adjustment.
**Trade-offs**: A supply shock can raise inflation while lowering output, making stabilization harder.

## Data-to-causality gate (Ch 38)
**When to use**: Turning an economic correlation into a policy or business claim.
**How**: Define population and variables; identify randomization or an alternative source of variation; test confounding, reverse causation, uncertainty, and external validity.
**Trade-offs**: Precise prediction is not necessarily causal identification.
