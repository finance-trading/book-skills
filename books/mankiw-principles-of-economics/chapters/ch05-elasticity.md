# Chapter 5: Elasticity and Its Application

## Core Idea
Elasticity measures responsiveness, allowing a supply-and-demand prediction to move beyond direction to a proportional magnitude.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Midpoint elasticity**: Compute percentage change in quantity using the average of initial and final quantities as denominator; do the same for price; divide the two percentages. This gives the same magnitude whether the comparison is run forward or backward.
- **Elasticity determinants**: Demand is more elastic with close substitutes, luxury status, a narrow market definition, and more time to adjust. Supply is more elastic when producers can change quantity more readily and have more time.
- **Revenue test**: For an inelastic demand curve, a price rise raises `P × Q`; for an elastic curve it lowers `P × Q`. Unit elasticity leaves total revenue unchanged for a small change. This is a local relation, not a constant property along an entire straight-line curve.

## Key Concepts
- **Price elasticity of demand**: Absolute percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price.
- **Price elasticity of supply**: Percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percentage change in price.
- **Income elasticity**: Demand response to a change in income.
- **Cross-price elasticity**: Demand response for one good to another good's price.
- **Total revenue**: Price multiplied by quantity sold.

## Mental Models
- Distinguish short-run from long-run response: a temporary lack of alternatives can make a market look inelastic.
- Ask whether price or volume dominates revenue before projecting a seller's receipts.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using raw unit changes**: A ten-unit quantity change means different things at different bases; use percentages.
- **Assuming a cartel can always raise revenue**: Buyers and rival suppliers may adjust over time.

## Worked Example
The chapter uses gasoline to show that demand responds more after consumers have time to change vehicles, commuting, or location. It cites a roughly 2.5% consumption reduction after a year for a 10% price increase, an elasticity near 0.25 over that horizon. Do not carry that figure into a different market or long-run period; identify the relevant substitutes and time window first.

The book also gives a two-point calculation: at point A, price is $4 and quantity is 120; at point B, price is $6 and quantity is 80. Dividing by the initial values gives 50% price growth and a 33% quantity decline from A to B, apparently an elasticity of 0.66. Reverse the direction and the same observations misleadingly produce 33% price decline and 50% quantity growth, or 1.5. The midpoint method instead uses average price `$5` and average quantity `100`: price changes `2/5 = 40%`, quantity changes `40/100 = 40%`, and elasticity is **1 in either direction**. This fixes base dependence, not estimation uncertainty or the possibility that elasticity differs elsewhere on the curve.

## Reference Tables
| Two-point midpoint computation | Formula | Book values |
|---|---|---|
| Price percentage change | `(P₂ − P₁)/[(P₂ + P₁)/2]` | `($6 − $4)/$5 = 40%` |
| Quantity percentage change | `(Q₂ − Q₁)/[(Q₂ + Q₁)/2]` | `(80 − 120)/100 = −40%` |
| Demand elasticity magnitude | `|%ΔQ/%ΔP|` | `1` |

| Demand elasticity | Quantity response to a 1% price increase | Direction of total revenue after a small price increase |
|---|---|---|
| Below 1 | Less than 1% decrease | Rises |
| Equal to 1 | About 1% decrease | Approximately unchanged |
| Above 1 | More than 1% decrease | Falls |

## Key Takeaways
1. Use the midpoint method for comparisons between two observations.
2. Specify market definition and time horizon before using an elasticity.
3. Translate elasticity into revenue and policy effects only under the relevant assumptions.
