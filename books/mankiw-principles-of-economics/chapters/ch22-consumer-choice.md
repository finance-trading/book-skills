# Chapter 22: The Theory of Consumer Choice

## Core Idea
A consumer chooses the most-preferred affordable bundle. Changes in prices and income alter the feasible set, and the resulting quantity response can be separated into substitution and income effects.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Budget–preference tangency**: Draw a budget line from prices and income; draw indifference curves representing equally preferred bundles. The best affordable interior point has marginal rate of substitution equal to the relative price.
- **Price-change decomposition**: A relative-price change induces substitution toward the now cheaper good; altered real purchasing power produces an income effect. For an inferior good the income effect can oppose substitution, and in a rare Giffen case exceed it.
- **Application to work and saving**: A higher wage raises the opportunity cost of leisure but can also make more leisure affordable; a higher interest rate raises the return to saving but changes lifetime income. Do not assert a net direction without considering both effects.

## Key Concepts
- **Budget constraint**: Bundles affordable at given income and prices.
- **Indifference curve**: Bundles yielding equal satisfaction.
- **Marginal rate of substitution**: Rate at which a consumer is willing to exchange one good for another.
- **Substitution effect**: Response to a change in relative prices holding purchasing power conceptually fixed.
- **Income effect**: Response to the change in purchasing power from a price or wage change.

## Mental Models
- The budget slope is the market trade-off; indifference-curve slope is the consumer's own trade-off.
- When two effects work in opposite directions, explain both before predicting a net response.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming all demand curves must slope down by definition**: The model identifies unusual conditions under which a Giffen good may violate that pattern.
- **Assuming a wage rise always increases hours worked**: Income and substitution effects can oppose one another.

## Worked Example
Mankiw's Consuela has $1,000 per month to spend on pizza at $10 each and Pepsi at $2 per liter. Her budget intercepts are 100 pizzas or 500 liters of Pepsi; 50 pizzas plus 250 liters of Pepsi also spends exactly $1,000. The budget slope is five liters of Pepsi per pizza, the market exchange rate. An indifference curve tangent to that line marks her preferred affordable bundle. If pizza becomes cheaper, the line pivots outward and its slope changes; substitution toward pizza and the increase in purchasing power must both be considered before predicting the final quantities.

## Key Takeaways
1. Draw affordability before ranking bundles.
2. Set marginal willingness to trade against the market exchange rate.
3. Decompose price, wage, and interest-rate effects before claiming a net direction.
