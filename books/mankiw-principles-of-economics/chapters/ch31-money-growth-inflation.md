# Chapter 31: Money Growth and Inflation

## Core Idea
The quantity theory explains sustained inflation through money growth relative to real output. In the long run, monetary changes mainly alter nominal values, while inflation itself imposes several real costs.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Quantity equation**: Use `M × V = P × Y` as an accounting relation. Under relatively stable velocity and long-run output driven by real factors, faster money growth produces faster price growth.
- **Classical dichotomy and neutrality**: Separate nominal variables such as dollar prices from real variables such as output and relative prices. Long-run monetary neutrality is an approximation, not a claim about the short run.
- **Inflation-cost audit**: Count shoe-leather costs, menu costs, distorted relative prices, tax interactions, confusion, and arbitrary wealth redistribution from unexpected inflation.

## Key Concepts
- **Velocity**: Rate at which money circulates in transactions.
- **Quantity theory of money**: Link from money supply to the price level under specified assumptions.
- **Monetary neutrality**: Long-run proposition that money changes nominal rather than real variables.
- **Inflation tax**: Purchasing-power loss to money holders when money creation finances government.
- **Fisher effect**: Nominal interest rates tend to rise with expected inflation in the long run.

## Mental Models
- Separate a one-time price-level change from persistent inflation.
- Inflation does not automatically lower every real income; assess whether nominal pay and contracts adjust.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the quantity equation as a causal law without assumptions**: Velocity and output can change.
- **Calling only higher sticker prices the cost of inflation**: Wages may rise too; the costs lie in adjustment and distortions.

## Worked Example
If a government finances spending by creating money rather than taxing directly, holders of existing money lose purchasing power as prices rise. Mankiw calls this the inflation tax. In an extreme case, rapid creation encourages people to shed money quickly, raising transaction effort and destabilizing prices. The example shows who bears the burden even when no conventional tax bill appears.

## Key Takeaways
1. State assumptions about velocity and real output before predicting inflation from money.
2. Distinguish nominal effects from short-run real disturbances.
3. Assess inflation's resource and distributional costs, not just the price index.
