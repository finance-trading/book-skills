# Chapter 14: The Costs of Production

## Core Idea
The firm's supply decisions depend on economic cost, which includes forgone opportunities, and on how marginal cost changes as production expands.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Economic versus accounting profit**: Subtract explicit and implicit opportunity costs from revenue for economic profit; accounting profit subtracts recorded explicit expenses only. Use economic profit when deciding whether resources should remain in the business.
- **Cost-curve construction**: Separate fixed and variable costs; calculate `ATC = TC/Q`, `AVC = VC/Q`, and `MC = ΔTC/ΔQ`. Diminishing marginal product tends to make marginal cost rise as output expands.
- **Short-run versus long-run choice**: In the short run some inputs are fixed; in the long run all can be varied. Compare long-run average cost across plant sizes and identify economies or diseconomies of scale.

## Key Concepts
- **Implicit cost**: Opportunity cost not requiring a direct cash payment.
- **Economic profit**: Revenue minus explicit and implicit costs.
- **Marginal product**: Extra output from one more unit of input.
- **Marginal cost**: Extra total cost of one more output unit.
- **Average total cost**: Total cost divided by quantity produced.

## Mental Models
- A successful firm can report accounting profit while earning too little to compensate its owner's alternative use of capital and time.
- When marginal cost lies below average total cost, expanding output lowers average total cost; when above it, average rises.

## Anti-patterns
- **Ignoring the owner's forgone wage or invested capital**: This overstates the economic return to staying in business.
- **Using average cost for the next-unit decision**: Marginal cost is the relevant incremental measure.

## Worked Example
Mankiw's factory example hires additional workers while plant size is fixed. Early workers may specialize, but eventually extra workers crowd the fixed equipment, so the marginal product of labor falls. Producing each additional unit then requires more labor and raises marginal cost. Plotting total cost against output therefore yields a steeper curve at high output; its slope, not its overall height, answers whether one more unit is costly.

The chapter quantifies the distinction in Caleb's Coffee Shop. Its fixed cost is $3 per hour whether it produces zero or three cups. At two cups, total cost is $3.80, so variable cost is `$3.80 − $3 = $0.80`, average variable cost is `$0.80/2 = $0.40`, and average total cost is `$3.80/2 = $1.90`. At three cups, total cost rises to $4.50. The third cup's marginal cost is `$4.50 − $3.80 = $0.70`, even though the average total cost of three cups is only `$4.50/3 = $1.50`. Average and marginal costs answer different decisions. The book's schedule also shows average total cost reaching about $1.30 at five to six cups before rising, the familiar U-shape. This particular minimum depends on the assumed cost schedule; it is not a universal production scale.

The final column is the incremental cost **from this row to the next cup**. In the book's original table it is printed between output rows; that placement prevents confusing MC with the average cost at an output level.

## Key Takeaways
1. Include all opportunity costs in an entry or continuation decision.
2. Derive average and marginal measures from the same cost schedule.
3. State the time horizon before treating a cost as fixed.
