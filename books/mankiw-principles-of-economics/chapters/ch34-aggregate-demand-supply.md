# Chapter 34: Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

## Core Idea
Short-run output and the overall price level fluctuate when aggregate demand or short-run aggregate supply shifts. The long-run supply benchmark is governed by real productive capacity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **AD–AS diagnosis**: Identify the initial intersection of aggregate demand (AD) and short-run aggregate supply (SRAS); classify a shock as spending-side or cost/productivity-side; shift the relevant curve; compare output, prices, and unemployment with the long-run output benchmark.
- **Why AD slopes downward**: A lower price level raises the real value of money holdings (wealth effect), tends to lower interest rates (interest-rate effect), and can make domestic goods relatively cheaper abroad (exchange-rate effect). These are price-level movements along AD, not shifts caused by policy or confidence.
- **Long-run correction**: Wages and expectations adjust over time, shifting SRAS toward output determined by labor, capital, resources, and technology. A demand contraction can lower output temporarily without permanently changing productive capacity under the model.

## Key Concepts
- **Aggregate demand**: Quantity of domestic goods and services demanded at each price level.
- **Short-run aggregate supply**: Quantity firms produce at each price level while some expectations or prices adjust slowly.
- **Long-run aggregate supply**: Output capacity independent of the price level in the classical benchmark.
- **Stagflation**: Weak output accompanied by rising prices after adverse supply shocks.
- **Recession**: Significant decline in economic activity across the economy.

## Mental Models
- Demand and supply shocks can both lower output but imply different price movements.
- A movement along AD from a price-level change differs from an AD shift from a policy, wealth, or confidence change.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming every recession is deficient spending**: An adverse supply shock may reduce output while raising prices.
- **Treating long-run neutrality as immediate**: Sticky prices or mistaken expectations permit short-run real effects.

## Worked Example
If consumers and firms become pessimistic, planned spending falls and AD shifts left. At existing short-run supply, output and the price level fall and unemployment rises. Over time lower expected prices and wages can shift SRAS right, returning output toward long-run capacity at a lower price level. By contrast, an oil-cost shock shifts SRAS left: output falls while the price level rises, creating a harder stabilization choice.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify the shock before choosing a policy response.
2. Track price level and output jointly, then compare with long-run capacity.
3. State assumptions about wage and expectation adjustment.
