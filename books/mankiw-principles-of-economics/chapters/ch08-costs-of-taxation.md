# Chapter 8: Application — The Costs of Taxation

## Core Idea
A tax transfers some surplus to government and destroys another part by discouraging trades whose buyer value exceeds seller cost. The destroyed part grows with behavioral responsiveness.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Tax-surplus ledger**: Compare consumer surplus, producer surplus, and tax revenue before and after a tax. The part of lost private surplus that does not become revenue is deadweight loss.
- **Elasticity test for deadweight loss**: More elastic supply or demand produces a larger quantity contraction for a given tax, so more beneficial trades disappear.
- **Tax-rate path**: As the tax increases, revenue initially can rise, but the base shrinks. Deadweight loss generally rises more than proportionally; revenue need not rise forever. The Laffer curve shows possibility, not the location of an economy on it.

## Key Concepts
- **Deadweight loss**: Reduction in total surplus beyond government revenue due to a policy distortion.
- **Tax revenue**: Per-unit tax multiplied by quantity traded after tax.
- **Tax base**: Activity or value on which a tax is levied.
- **Elasticity**: Measure of quantity response governing the number of deterred trades.

## Mental Models
- Treat tax revenue as a transfer in the welfare ledger; measure lost trades separately.
- The more alternatives buyers and sellers have, the larger the behavioral distortion from a given wedge.

## Anti-patterns
- **Claiming tax revenue is the whole social cost**: It omits mutually beneficial exchanges that no longer occur.
- **Using the Laffer curve to assert any tax cut pays for itself**: The result depends on the current rate and the response of the tax base.

## Worked Example
The chapter draws a per-unit tax between buyer and seller prices. Before the tax, units up to the competitive quantity trade because marginal buyer value exceeds marginal seller cost. Afterward, the wedge stops some of those trades. On the diagram, the government collects a rectangle equal to tax times the remaining quantity; the triangle between the old and new quantities is deadweight loss. Increasing the wedge widens that lost-trade region.

Mankiw gives a concrete transaction: Malik cleans Mei's house for $100. Mei values a clean house at $120 and Malik's opportunity cost is $80. At the agreed price, each receives `$20` surplus; total gain is `$120 − $80 = $40`. A $50 tax on cleaning eliminates every mutually acceptable price. If Mei pays no more than $120, Malik keeps at most $70, below his $80 cost; if Malik keeps $80, Mei must pay at least $130, above her value. The trade disappears, government receives **zero** tax from this transaction, and the entire $40 possible gain is lost. This example shows why tax revenue is not the complete welfare ledger and why a tax can shrink its own base.

## Reference Tables
| Malik–Mei transaction | Before tax | With $50 tax |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mei's value | $120 | $120 |
| Malik's opportunity cost | $80 | $80 |
| Feasible mutually beneficial price | $80–$120 | None: buyer pays at most $120, seller needs at least $130 |
| Consumer + producer surplus | $40 total at $100 price | $0 realized; $40 deadweight loss |
| Tax revenue from this transaction | $0 | $0 because trade stops |

## Key Takeaways
1. Add consumer surplus, producer surplus, and revenue before assessing welfare.
2. Use elasticities to judge how large the lost-trade area may be.
3. Treat revenue responses to a tax change as empirical, not guaranteed.
