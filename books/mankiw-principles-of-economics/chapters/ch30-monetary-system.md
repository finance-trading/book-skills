# Chapter 30: The Monetary System

## Core Idea
Money reduces the transaction costs of barter. Banks and the central bank affect the supply of money, but the reserve system and public behavior limit simple multiplier formulas.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three functions of money**: Check whether an asset serves as medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value. Liquidity matters even when another asset yields a higher return.
- **Fractional-reserve mechanics**: A bank keeps part of deposits as reserves and lends the rest; loans can become new deposits elsewhere. Under a simplified fixed reserve ratio and no currency leakage, a deposit expansion has an upper-bound multiplier of `1/reserve ratio`.
- **Monetary-control toolkit**: Distinguish open-market operations, interest paid on reserves, discount lending, and reserve requirements. Trace their effect on reserves, lending incentives, and the money supply rather than assuming perfect control.

## Key Concepts
- **Fiat money**: Money valued because it is accepted, not because of intrinsic material value.
- **Commodity money**: Money with intrinsic nonmonetary value.
- **Reserve**: Deposit a bank holds rather than lends.
- **Fractional-reserve banking**: Banking with reserves smaller than deposits.
- **Central bank**: Institution overseeing money and banking policy.

## Mental Models
- Bank deposits can function as money, so cash in wallets is not the entire money stock.
- Lending creates deposits only when banks lend and borrowers and the public behave as the model assumes.

## Anti-patterns
- **Applying the textbook multiplier mechanically**: Excess reserves and currency withdrawals reduce actual expansion.
- **Calling every liquid asset money**: A security that is readily sold is not necessarily a routine payment medium.

## Worked Example
With a 10% reserve requirement in the simplified chapter model, a bank receiving a $100 deposit holds $10 and lends $90. If the loan proceeds are redeposited, the next bank can hold $9 and lend $81. Repeating the process yields a theoretical total deposit expansion up to $1,000 from the initial $100. This is a model ceiling, not an empirical promise: banks can hold extra reserves and the public can hold currency.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the money measure before discussing its growth.
2. Trace bank balance sheets through lending and redepositing.
3. Treat multiplier results as assumption-dependent.
