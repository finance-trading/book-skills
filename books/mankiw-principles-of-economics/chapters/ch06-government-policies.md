# Chapter 6: Supply, Demand, and Government Policies

## Core Idea
Price controls and taxes change market outcomes through shortages, surpluses, and wedges between buyer and seller prices. Legal assignment of a tax does not determine its economic burden.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Binding-control test**: Compare a ceiling or floor with the unregulated equilibrium price. A ceiling binds only below it, creating a shortage; a floor binds only above it, creating a surplus. Then ask how the scarce transactions are rationed.
- **Tax-wedge analysis**: Draw the tax as a fixed gap between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive. Find the reduced traded quantity, then split the wedge using relative supply and demand elasticities.
- **Incidence rule**: The less elastic side of a market bears more of a tax because it changes quantity less readily. This holds whether the statute remits tax from buyers or sellers.

## Key Concepts
- **Price ceiling**: Legal maximum price.
- **Price floor**: Legal minimum price.
- **Binding**: Restriction that changes the equilibrium outcome.
- **Tax incidence**: How the economic burden of a tax is divided.
- **Tax wedge**: Difference between buyer and seller prices created by a per-unit tax.

## Mental Models
- A law can fix a posted price without eliminating the underlying scarcity or surplus.
- Separate who sends a tax payment to government from who loses purchasing power or income.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming rent control increases housing supply**: A binding ceiling reduces the incentive to provide rental units and creates non-price rationing.
- **Assuming employers pay all payroll tax**: Wage adjustment can shift part of the burden to workers.

## Worked Example
For rent control, first locate the market rent. If a cap lies below it, more tenants seek apartments than landlords offer; waiting, search, and selection replace some price rationing. For a payroll tax, draw a wedge between the wage firms pay and the wage workers receive. The burden depends on relative responsiveness of labor supply and demand, not on the name printed on the tax form.

## Key Takeaways
1. Check whether a control is binding before predicting effects.
2. Account for rationing and quality changes when price cannot clear a market.
3. Use elasticities to assess tax burden, regardless of statutory remittance.
