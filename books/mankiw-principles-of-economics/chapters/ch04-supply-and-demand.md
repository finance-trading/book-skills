# Chapter 4: The Market Forces of Supply and Demand

## Core Idea
In a competitive market, the intersection of buyers' willingness to buy and sellers' willingness to sell determines price and quantity. A changed price moves along a curve; a changed determinant shifts a curve.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three steps for analyzing changes in equilibrium**: Identify whether the event shifts demand, supply, or both; determine each shift's direction; compare the new intersection with the old one. If both shift, price or quantity may be indeterminate without relative magnitudes.
- **Demand and supply shifter checklist**: For demand, inspect income, related goods, tastes, expectations, and number of buyers. For supply, inspect input prices, technology, expectations, and number of sellers. Hold other factors constant when drawing each curve.
- **Surplus–shortage adjustment**: Above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds demanded and sellers have reason to lower price; below it, shortage gives price upward pressure.

## Key Concepts
- **Quantity demanded**: Amount buyers are willing and able to purchase at a stated price.
- **Demand shift**: Change in willingness to buy at every price.
- **Quantity supplied**: Amount sellers are willing and able to sell at a stated price.
- **Equilibrium**: Price and quantity where planned purchases equal planned sales.
- **Complement**: A good whose demand tends to fall when the price of a paired good rises.

## Mental Models
- Separate a movement along the demand curve caused by the good's own price from a demand-curve shift caused by another factor.
- If both curves move, state what is known and what requires a magnitude assumption.

## Anti-patterns
- **Price rose, so demand rose**: A higher price may instead follow a leftward supply shift.
- **Static shortage claim**: At a free price, shortage creates incentives to adjust; explain why price adjustment may be prevented.

## Worked Example
A Florida cold snap damages orange production. Supply of orange juice shifts left at each price while the demand schedule is unchanged. At the old price buyers want more than sellers can provide, so price rises and equilibrium quantity falls. By contrast, higher summer demand for Caribbean lodging shifts demand right and tends to raise both price and quantity. These examples show why the first task is classifying the shock.

## Key Takeaways
1. List non-price shifters before drawing arrows.
2. Trace the new intersection rather than assuming a price change implies a demand change.
3. Mark outcomes indeterminate when simultaneous shifts work in opposite directions.
