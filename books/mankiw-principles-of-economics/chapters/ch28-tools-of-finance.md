# Chapter 28: The Basic Tools of Finance

## Core Idea
Financial decisions compare cash flows at different dates and risks. Present value, diversification, and asset valuation provide the basic tools; assumptions about discount rates and cash flows drive the result.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Present-value rule**: For a certain future amount `FV` in `N` periods at rate `r`, compute `PV = FV/(1+r)^N`. Compare alternatives in the same date's dollars and use a rate matching risk and horizon.
- **Risk management**: Insurance pools idiosyncratic loss; diversification spreads independent or imperfectly correlated risks; neither removes economy-wide risk. Risk aversion explains why a person may accept a lower expected return for less uncertainty.
- **Asset-valuation principle**: Value a stock by the present value of future dividends plus eventual sale price, with forecasts and discounting explicit. Market efficiency is a hypothesis about how prices incorporate available information, not proof that any quote is correct.

## Key Concepts
- **Compounding**: Interest earning further interest over time.
- **Present value**: Today's amount equivalent to future cash given a discount rate.
- **Risk aversion**: Preference for a certain outcome over an equal-expected-value gamble.
- **Diversification**: Combining exposures to reduce asset-specific risk.
- **Efficient markets hypothesis**: Claim that market prices rapidly reflect available information.

## Mental Models
- A future dollar and a current dollar are not interchangeable; compare after discounting.
- Diversification changes the distribution of outcomes, while valuation still depends on underlying cash flows.

## Anti-patterns
- **Choosing the larger future number without discounting**: Time has value.
- **Assuming many assets eliminate all risk**: Common shocks affect diversified portfolios too.

## Worked Example
Mankiw compares $100 today with $200 in ten years. At 5% annual interest, $100 compounds to about $163; $200 received in ten years has present value about $123. Under that rate, $200 later is more valuable than $100 now. A different rate can reverse the comparison, so the discount-rate assumption must be visible.

## Key Takeaways
1. Align dates and risk before comparing amounts.
2. Distinguish idiosyncratic risk from market-wide risk.
3. Treat efficient-market claims and valuation inputs as assumptions to test.
