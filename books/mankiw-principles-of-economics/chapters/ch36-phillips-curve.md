# Chapter 36: The Short-Run Trade-Off between Inflation and Unemployment

## Core Idea
The Phillips curve describes a short-run inflation–unemployment trade-off, not a permanent menu. Expectations and supply shocks move the curve, while long-run unemployment returns toward its natural rate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Short-run Phillips curve**: A demand expansion can lower unemployment below its natural rate while raising inflation; a contraction can lower inflation while raising unemployment temporarily.
- **Expectations adjustment**: When people update expected inflation, wage and price setting adapt. The short-run curve shifts, leaving a vertical long-run Phillips curve at the natural unemployment rate in the model.
- **Supply-shock and disinflation audit**: An adverse oil or production shock can raise inflation and unemployment together. A credible disinflation may reduce the output cost relative to an unanticipated contraction, but the sacrifice ratio is uncertain and context dependent.

## Key Concepts
- **Phillips curve**: Short-run relation between inflation and unemployment.
- **Natural unemployment rate**: Long-run level determined by labor-market structures.
- **Expected inflation**: Inflation incorporated into current wage and price decisions.
- **Supply shock**: Event changing production costs or capacity.
- **Sacrifice ratio**: Output lost while reducing inflation, estimated rather than fixed.

## Mental Models
- An observed inflation–unemployment pair identifies neither the cause nor a stable policy lever by itself.
- To judge policy, ask whether inflation expectations and supply conditions are changing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Targeting unemployment permanently below natural rate through inflation**: Expectations eventually adapt.
- **Interpreting stagflation as proof the short-run model is useless**: Supply shocks shift the curve.

## Worked Example
After an oil-price jump, firms face higher costs, short-run aggregate supply shifts left, and the economy may see higher inflation with higher unemployment. A policymaker cannot read the old Phillips curve and claim one of those variables must fall. The shock has changed the curve itself; demand expansion might reduce unemployment but add further inflation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate movement along a short-run curve from a shift in it.
2. Include expected inflation in any medium- or long-run claim.
3. Assess disinflation costs as uncertain empirical estimates.
