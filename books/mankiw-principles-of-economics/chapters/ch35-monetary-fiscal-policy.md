# Chapter 35: The Influence of Monetary and Fiscal Policy on Aggregate Demand

## Core Idea
Policy changes aggregate demand through interest rates, government purchases, taxes, and expectations. Multipliers and crowding out affect the size and timing of the result.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Liquidity-preference channel**: At a given price level, money supply and demand set the interest rate. More money supply tends to lower the rate, stimulate interest-sensitive investment, and shift AD right; higher price levels raise money demand and tend to raise rates.
- **Fiscal multiplier versus crowding out**: Government purchases directly add to AD and can create further consumption rounds. Higher borrowing can also raise interest rates and displace private investment. A tax cut works through households' propensity to spend rather than one-for-one direct purchases.
- **Stabilization-lag test**: Separate recognition, legislative or decision, implementation, and effect lags. A policy arriving after conditions reverse can amplify rather than smooth fluctuations.

## Key Concepts
- **Liquidity preference**: Demand for money balances relative to interest-bearing assets.
- **Multiplier**: Total AD response exceeding an initial spending impulse through subsequent consumption.
- **Crowding out**: Private spending displaced by higher interest rates or financing needs.
- **Automatic stabilizer**: Tax or transfer rule that responds without new legislation.
- **Policy lag**: Delay between shock, action, and economic effect.

## Mental Models
- Distinguish direction of a policy shift from uncertain magnitude and timing.
- Evaluate a proposed stimulus against spare capacity, financing, household spending response, and lags.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming a dollar of spending has exactly a one-dollar total effect**: Subsequent spending can amplify it, financing can offset part of it.
- **Assuming a tax cut is fully spent immediately**: Households may save it or repay debt.

## Worked Example
An increase in government purchases raises AD directly. Households receiving the new income may spend part of it, producing a multiplier. If the government borrows to finance the purchases, higher money demand and interest rates may reduce private investment, partly crowding out the initial rise. Mankiw's framework asks the analyst to show both arrows before quoting a net multiplier.

## Key Takeaways
1. Trace monetary policy through rates and interest-sensitive spending.
2. Show both multiplier and crowding-out paths for fiscal policy.
3. Include timing and automatic stabilizers in policy evaluation.
