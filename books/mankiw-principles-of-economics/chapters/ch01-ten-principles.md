# Chapter 1: Ten Principles of Economics

## Core Idea
Scarcity forces choices. Mankiw organizes economic reasoning around ten principles concerning individual decisions, interaction through markets, and the economy as a whole.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ten Principles of Economics**: Use the ten principles as diagnostic questions, not as universal policy conclusions. At the individual level ask what is given up, what changes at the margin, and how incentives change behavior; at the interaction level ask what trade and prices coordinate and where market failure may arise; at the macro level ask about productivity, money growth, and short-run fluctuations.
- **Marginal decision rule**: When choosing an extra unit of an activity, compare its additional benefit with its additional cost, including opportunity cost. Ignore costs that cannot be changed by the choice.
- **Market-failure test**: When a market outcome looks undesirable, identify an externality, market power, or missing property right before proposing intervention; then account for policy incentives and implementation costs.

## Key Concepts
- **Scarcity**: Society has limited resources relative to desired uses.
- **Opportunity cost**: The value of the best forgone alternative of an action.
- **Efficiency**: Getting the most from scarce resources; distinct from equality of distribution.
- **Externality**: An uncompensated effect of one person's action on a third party.
- **Productivity**: Goods and services produced per unit of labor input; a central driver of living standards.

## Mental Models
- Treat every decision as a trade-off between feasible alternatives, then compare the next increment rather than averages.
- Treat prices as coordination signals, while checking whether participants bear the full costs and benefits.

## Anti-patterns
- **Free-lunch reasoning**: Counting a benefit without the resources or opportunities sacrificed hides the real choice.
- **One-sided policy analysis**: A rule changes behavior; the intended transfer alone does not describe the result.

## Worked Example
Mankiw contrasts the value of water with the decision to buy one more glass. Water is essential in total, but the marginal benefit of another glass can be small when water is plentiful. A buyer compares that marginal benefit with its price; the importance of the entire stock is not the correct comparison for the next unit. The same logic applies to study time, production, and pollution reduction.

## Key Takeaways
1. Name the forgone alternative before declaring an option cheap.
2. Test marginal benefit against marginal cost and anticipate incentive responses.
3. Separate market coordination from the specific reasons a market may fail.
