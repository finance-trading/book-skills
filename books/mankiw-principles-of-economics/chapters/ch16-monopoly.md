# Chapter 16: Monopoly

## Core Idea
A monopolist faces the market demand curve. Selling one more unit requires lowering price, so marginal revenue is below price and profit-maximizing output is below the competitive benchmark.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Monopoly pricing sequence**: Find the quantity where `MR = MC`; then read the price buyers will pay for that quantity from the demand curve. Do not set price by equating price directly to marginal cost.
- **Barrier-to-entry diagnosis**: Test for control of a key resource, legal exclusivity, or a natural monopoly in which one provider has lower average total cost than multiple providers.
- **Welfare and price-discrimination test**: Compare the monopoly quantity with the efficient quantity where demand value equals marginal cost. Discrimination can transfer surplus and sometimes expand output, but requires separating customers by willingness to pay and preventing resale.

## Key Concepts
- **Monopoly**: Sole seller of a good without close substitutes.
- **Natural monopoly**: Single provider can serve the market at lower cost than multiple firms.
- **Marginal revenue**: Incremental revenue including the price reduction on existing units.
- **Price discrimination**: Different prices to different buyers for the same product based on willingness to pay.
- **Deadweight loss**: Mutually beneficial trades forgone because output is restricted.

## Mental Models
- Market power is constrained by the demand curve: a monopolist cannot make customers buy an arbitrarily priced product.
- Separate monopoly profit, a transfer from buyers, from the lost surplus on units not sold.

## Anti-patterns
- **Charging any desired price**: Higher price lowers quantity demanded and may reduce profit.
- **Treating high profit alone as proof of inefficiency**: The welfare loss comes from restricted trade, not the transfer alone.

## Worked Example
The chapter asks why Microsoft does not price Windows at $1,000 or $10,000. Fewer customers would buy computers or they would seek other operating systems. In a demand schedule, lowering price adds a customer but also cuts revenue on units that would have sold at the old price. The firm compares that full marginal-revenue effect with marginal cost, chooses quantity, and only then reads the corresponding price.

Mankiw's single water producer makes the arithmetic explicit. At three gallons, each sells for $8 and total revenue is `$8 × 3 = $24`. To sell a fourth gallon, price must fall to $7 **on all four gallons**, so revenue becomes `$7 × 4 = $28`. Marginal revenue is only `$28 − $24 = $4`: the new gallon brings $7 but the first three gallons each lose $1, a $3 offset. At this point `MR < P`. If marginal cost of the fourth gallon exceeds $4, expanding is unprofitable even though its selling price is $7. The cost schedule is required to choose the optimal quantity; the revenue table alone cannot tell the final monopoly price.

## Reference Tables
| Water output | Price per gallon | Total revenue | Incremental revenue from previous output |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 gallons | $8 | $24 | — |
| 4 gallons | $7 | $28 | $4 |

`MR of fourth gallon = $7 new-unit receipts − 3 × $1 lower price on existing units = $4`. This decomposition is specific to a single posted price applied to all gallons; price discrimination changes it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Derive marginal revenue from demand, including the lost revenue on prior units.
2. Compare output with the efficient `P = MC` benchmark.
3. Evaluate antitrust or regulation against the specific entry barrier and cost structure.
