# Chapter 10: Externalities

## Core Idea
When a market participant's action affects an uncompensated third party, private demand or supply omits part of social value or cost. The efficient quantity may therefore differ from the market quantity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Social-cost and social-value test**: For a negative production externality, add external harm to private cost and reduce output toward the social optimum. For a positive externality, add external benefit to private value and expand output toward the social optimum.
- **Internalization menu**: Compare direct regulation, corrective taxes or subsidies, and tradable permits. Use a tax when a price on harm can induce flexible abatement; use permits when an aggregate quantity target is paramount.
- **Coase bargaining check**: Private bargaining may resolve an externality if rights are clear and transaction costs are low. With many parties or high bargaining costs, that solution becomes less practical.

## Key Concepts
- **Externality**: Unpriced effect of an action on a bystander.
- **Social cost**: Private cost plus external cost.
- **Corrective tax**: Tax intended to align private incentives with social cost.
- **Tradable permit**: Transferable right to undertake a limited amount of an activity.
- **Coase theorem**: Under suitable conditions, bargaining can reach an efficient allocation regardless of initial rights.

## Mental Models
- Price the marginal harm or benefit, not merely the presence of an unpopular activity.
- Ask whether a proposed remedy has information and enforcement requirements that can actually be met.

## Anti-patterns
- **Zero-pollution assumption**: Some activity can have benefits exceeding marginal harm.
- **Assuming bargaining is free**: Negotiation, monitoring, and enforcement costs matter.

## Worked Example
Pollution from a factory imposes costs on neighbors that the factory does not pay. At the private supply-and-demand intersection, more output is produced than the social optimum. A corrective tax equal to marginal external cost makes the firm face that cost when deciding the next unit. Alternatively, a fixed total of tradable pollution permits lets firms with lower cleanup costs sell rights to those facing higher cleanup costs; both seek cheaper abatement than uniform orders.

## Key Takeaways
1. Draw the external cost or benefit before choosing a remedy.
2. Compare policy tools by incentives, information, and enforcement.
3. Check transaction costs before relying on private bargains.
