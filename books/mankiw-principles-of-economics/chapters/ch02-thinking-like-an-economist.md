# Chapter 2: Thinking Like an Economist

## Core Idea
Economic models deliberately omit detail to expose a mechanism. Use them to separate testable claims about what happens from value judgments about what ought to happen.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Scientific method in economics**: State an assumption, derive a prediction, compare it with observations, then revise the model when evidence conflicts. Judge an assumption by whether it helps explain the question at hand.
- **Circular-flow diagram**: Trace households and firms through product and factor markets, distinguishing the flow of inputs and goods from the reverse flow of money. Use it to check who earns, spends, and produces.
- **Production possibilities frontier (PPF)**: Given resources and technology, map feasible output combinations. Points on the frontier are efficient, inside points are attainable but inefficient, and outside points require trade, technology, or more resources. Its slope expresses opportunity cost.

## Key Concepts
- **Microeconomics**: Study of individual households, firms, and their market interactions.
- **Macroeconomics**: Study of aggregate output, inflation, unemployment, and related trends.
- **Positive statement**: A claim about how the world is that can, in principle, be tested.
- **Normative statement**: A claim about what the world ought to be, requiring a value judgment.
- **PPF**: Boundary between attainable and currently unattainable production combinations.

## Mental Models
- Match the simplification to the question: a household–firm flow is useful for exchange, whereas a PPF is useful for scarcity and trade-offs.
- Before debating policy, distinguish a forecast of effects from the judgment that those effects are good or bad.

## Anti-patterns
- **Demanding a literal model**: A model that includes every detail can hide the causal relationship it is meant to clarify.
- **Treating correlation as cause**: Omitted factors or reverse causation can explain a graph's slope.

## Worked Example
The chapter's circular-flow model places households as sellers of labor and other factors, firms as buyers of those factors, firms as sellers of finished goods, and households as buyers of goods. Wages and other income flow toward households while consumption spending flows toward firms. For a claim that household spending has fallen, trace both the product-market revenue of firms and the factor-market income that may subsequently change; the diagram identifies channels, not the magnitude of either change.

## Key Takeaways
1. Specify the question and the simplifying assumptions before using a diagram.
2. Use the PPF slope to express what more of one output costs in units of another.
3. Label empirical and value claims separately.
