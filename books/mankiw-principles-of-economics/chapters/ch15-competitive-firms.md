# Chapter 15: Firms in Competitive Markets

## Core Idea
A price-taking firm chooses output where price equals marginal cost, but whether it produces now or remains in the industry depends on which costs can be avoided over the decision horizon.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Competitive output rule**: With many sellers of an identical product, `MR = AR = P`. Increase output while the next unit's marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost; stop near `P = MC` on the rising part of the cost curve.
- **Shutdown versus exit**: In the short run, fixed costs are sunk and production can continue when `P ≥ AVC`; shut down when price falls below average variable cost. In the long run, exit when `P < ATC` because all costs must be covered.
- **Entry–exit adjustment**: Positive economic profit attracts entry and pushes price down; losses cause exit and push it up. Under free entry, long-run economic profit tends toward zero, while normal returns remain covered in economic cost.

## Key Concepts
- **Price taker**: Firm too small to influence market price materially.
- **Marginal revenue**: Extra revenue from one more unit sold.
- **Sunk cost**: Past or committed cost unaffected by the present decision.
- **Shutdown**: Temporary cessation while remaining in the market.
- **Exit**: Long-run departure from the industry.

## Mental Models
- Use avoidable costs for temporary production and all opportunity costs for permanent participation.
- Zero economic profit is compatible with a normal return to labor and capital, not zero accounting earnings.

## Anti-patterns
- **Shutting down whenever total profit is negative**: Current production may still cover variable costs and part of fixed costs.
- **Treating a short-run profitable price as durable**: Entry can erase excess economic profits.

## Worked Example
A competitive gas station faces a market price it cannot set. At a low price that still covers fuel, labor, and other variable costs, it may open for the day even if revenue cannot cover rent already due; opening reduces its loss. If the same price is expected indefinitely and fails to cover rent and other total costs, exit becomes the rational long-run decision. The distinction follows the costs the manager can change.

The book's Vaca Family Dairy Farm takes the milk price of $6 per gallon. At zero output, fixed cost $3 gives a $3 loss. At three gallons, revenue is `$6 × 3 = $18`, total cost $12, and profit $6. A fourth gallon adds $6 revenue but only $5 cost, lifting profit to $7. A fifth adds $6 revenue and $6 cost, leaving profit at $7. A sixth would add $6 revenue but $7 cost, lowering profit. Thus either four or five gallons maximizes profit in the discrete table; the continuous `P = MC` rule is an approximation to this increment-by-increment comparison. The precise choice can be tied when marginal profit is zero.

The final column is the cost of moving to the next row of output, not the average cost of the displayed row. The numerical example presumes a constant market price for this small firm; a monopoly cannot use the same fixed-$6 marginal revenue.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compare marginal revenue with marginal cost for output.
2. Compare price with AVC for short-run shutdown and ATC for long-run exit.
3. Include normal opportunity returns when interpreting zero economic profit.
