# Chapter 11: Public Goods and Common Resources

## Core Idea
Whether users can be excluded and whether one person's use leaves less for others determines when private provision works and when free-riding or overuse is likely.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two-axis goods classification**: Ask whether exclusion is feasible, then whether use is rival. Private goods are both; public goods are neither; common resources are rival but hard to exclude; club goods are excludable but nonrival up to capacity.
- **Public-good provision test**: Sum the benefits across users and compare them with provision cost. Individual willingness to pay understates collective benefit when users can free-ride.
- **Common-resource management**: When individual users ignore depletion imposed on others, use property rights, permits, fees, quotas, or collective rules to restrain overuse.

## Key Concepts
- **Excludability**: Ability to prevent nonpayers from using a good.
- **Rivalry**: One person's use reduces what others can use.
- **Free rider**: Beneficiary who avoids paying for a nonexcludable good.
- **Common resource**: Rival good for which exclusion is difficult.
- **Cost–benefit analysis**: Comparison of total social benefits with total social costs.

## Mental Models
- Classify the good before prescribing a public or private supplier.
- For nonexcludable benefits, individual payment signals may be too small; for rival common use, individual withdrawal may be too large.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling every government service a public good**: Ownership does not determine rivalry or exclusion.
- **Assuming voluntary donations reveal full value**: Free-riding can suppress stated willingness to pay.

## Worked Example
National defense protects residents regardless of whether each pays, and one resident's protection generally does not subtract another's. Because exclusion is impractical, a private seller cannot reliably collect from all beneficiaries; government provision uses a collective tax decision. A fishery differs: one fisher's catch leaves fewer fish for others. Rules limiting catch or assigning rights address depletion rather than the provision problem of defense.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose exclusion and rivalry separately.
2. Sum benefits across users for public-good decisions.
3. Manage common-resource depletion as an incentive problem.
