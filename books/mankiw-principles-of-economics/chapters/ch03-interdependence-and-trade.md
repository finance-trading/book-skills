# Chapter 3: Interdependence and the Gains from Trade

## Core Idea
Trade is driven by comparative advantage: the producer with the lower opportunity cost should specialize, even if another producer is faster at making both goods.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Absolute versus comparative advantage**: First compare input time per unit for absolute advantage; then compute what each unit costs in the other good for comparative advantage. Base specialization on the second comparison.
- **Mutually beneficial trading range**: A trade price must lie between the two producers' opportunity costs. If it does, each can obtain a good for less than producing it alone would cost.
- **Production versus consumption frontier**: Without trade, consumption is constrained by one's own PPF. Specialization and exchange can permit a consumption bundle outside that PPF, while aggregate resources remain unchanged.

## Key Concepts
- **Absolute advantage**: Producing a unit with fewer inputs than another producer.
- **Comparative advantage**: Producing a unit at a lower opportunity cost.
- **Opportunity cost**: Output of the other good sacrificed to make one more unit.
- **Import**: A good produced abroad and purchased domestically.
- **Export**: A good produced domestically and sold abroad.

## Mental Models
- A producer can be best at every task in absolute terms yet still gain by delegating the task with the highest relative sacrifice.
- Compare exchange terms to both parties' own production trade-offs, not only to money prices.

## Anti-patterns
- **Winner-takes-all productivity ranking**: Picking the fastest producer for every good ignores opportunity cost.
- **Assuming trade creates output automatically**: Gains require a different allocation of production and terms both sides accept.

## Worked Example
Mankiw's rancher Ruby can produce both meat and potatoes faster than farmer Frank. In eight hours, Frank needs 60 minutes per ounce of meat and 15 per ounce of potatoes; Ruby needs 20 and 10 minutes. Ruby sacrifices two ounces of potatoes for an ounce of meat, whereas Frank sacrifices four. Ruby has comparative advantage in meat, Frank in potatoes. They specialize and trade at a ratio between those costs, leaving both with more of both goods than under the book's initial self-sufficient bundles.

The derivation matters: Frank can make four ounces of potatoes in the hour needed for one ounce of meat, so meat costs him four potatoes; Ruby can make two potatoes in the 20 minutes needed for one meat, so meat costs her two potatoes. For potatoes the reciprocal costs are 1/4 meat for Frank and 1/2 meat for Ruby. Ruby is absolutely faster at both goods, yet the opportunity-cost ranking differs by good. The book's illustrated trade of five ounces of meat for fifteen potatoes sets a price of three potatoes per meat, strictly between two and four. Ruby receives more potatoes than she gives up making five meat; Frank receives meat more cheaply than making it himself. The book's numerical bundles depend on the assumed eight-hour day and the chosen specialization, not on a general promise that every person gains from every specific trade.

## Reference Tables
| Producer | Minutes per ounce of meat | Minutes per ounce of potatoes | Opportunity cost of 1 meat | Opportunity cost of 1 potato |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Frank | 60 | 15 | 4 potatoes | 1/4 meat |
| Ruby | 20 | 10 | 2 potatoes | 1/2 meat |

Trade terms of three potatoes per meat fall between the two opportunity costs. If the price were outside that interval, one party could produce the good more cheaply alone and would reject the offer under the model's assumptions.

## Key Takeaways
1. Calculate opportunity costs in common units before assigning production.
2. Check that the exchange ratio lies between the two opportunity costs.
3. Keep the distribution of gains distinct from the existence of aggregate gains.
