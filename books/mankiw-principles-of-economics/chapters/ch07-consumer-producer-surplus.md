# Chapter 7: Consumers, Producers, and the Efficiency of Markets

## Core Idea
Consumer and producer surplus translate market exchange into measured gains. In a competitive market without important failures, equilibrium maximizes their sum, though it need not satisfy a separate goal of equality.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Surplus accounting**: Consumer surplus is willingness to pay minus price, represented by the area below demand and above price. Producer surplus is price minus sellers' cost, the area above supply and below price. Add them for total surplus.
- **Efficient allocation test**: Compare marginal buyer value with marginal seller cost. Trades with value above cost increase total surplus; trades with value below cost destroy it. Competitive equilibrium includes the former and excludes the latter under the model's assumptions.
- **Efficiency–equality distinction**: Efficiency concerns the size of the total gain; equality concerns its distribution. A policy judgment can require both, but do not treat one measure as the other.

The equilibrium result requires informed buyers and sellers, price taking, no third-party costs or benefits, and marginal willingness to pay and cost curves that represent social value and social cost. A monopoly, pollution, missing market, or important information asymmetry breaks one of those links. Even with efficiency, a policymaker may judge the distribution unacceptable; the theorem does not make that ethical choice.

**Why the result works**: At the equilibrium price, buyers with the highest willingness to pay purchase first and sellers with the lowest opportunity cost supply first. Reassigning an already traded unit to a buyer who values it less, or from a low-cost to a high-cost seller, cannot raise total gain. Reducing quantity below equilibrium sacrifices units for which marginal buyer value exceeds marginal seller cost. Increasing quantity beyond equilibrium adds units whose cost exceeds buyer value. This is the book's benevolent-social-planner thought experiment: even an all-knowing planner cannot improve the *sum* of private surplus by changing those allocations under the stated model. The argument does not assess inequality, environmental damage, or whether people can actually observe quality.

## Key Concepts
- **Willingness to pay**: Highest price a buyer would accept for a good.
- **Cost**: Seller's opportunity cost of producing a good.
- **Consumer surplus**: Buyer value less payment.
- **Producer surplus**: Seller receipt less cost.
- **Total surplus**: Sum of consumer and producer surplus.

## Mental Models
- The demand curve is an ordering of buyers' marginal values; the supply curve is an ordering of sellers' marginal costs.
- Check whether an intervention creates additional high-value trades or blocks trades whose value exceeds cost.

## Anti-patterns
- **Counting transfers as destroyed value**: A price change can move surplus between groups without changing its total by the same amount.
- **Equating efficiency with fairness**: A maximum total surplus does not by itself decide its distribution.

## Worked Example
Mankiw auctions a rare Elvis Presley album among four buyers. Whitney will pay $1,000, Ella $800, Mariah $700, and Karen $500. With one copy, bidding reaches about $800: Whitney receives it and gets `$1,000 − $800 = $200` consumer surplus. With two identical copies at about $700 each, Whitney gets $300 and Ella gets $100, for $400 total consumer surplus. The demand staircase orders the marginal buyer's value and the area above price recovers these amounts.

In a separate house-painting auction, Andy's opportunity cost is $2,000, Pablo's $2,400, Claude's $3,200, and Vincent's $3,600. With one job at about $2,400, Andy earns `$2,400 − $2,000 = $400` producer surplus. With two jobs priced about $3,200 each, Andy earns $1,200 and Pablo $800, totaling $2,000. The supply staircase orders marginal cost and the area below price recovers these amounts. The album and painting are distinct markets, so **do not add their surpluses together**. Within a single market transaction, consumer surplus plus producer surplus equals buyer value minus seller cost; the transaction price divides that gain but does not change its sum.

| Elvis album buyers | Same WTP schedule | Two copies at ~$700 each | CS ~$300 + ~$100 = ~$400 |
| House painters | Cost: Andy $2,000; Pablo $2,400; Claude $3,200; Vincent $3,600 | One job at ~$2,400 | Andy PS ~$400 |
| House painters | Same cost schedule | Two jobs at ~$3,200 each | PS ~$1,200 + ~$800 = ~$2,000 |

The auctions illustrate the geometry of surplus; the exact indifference-boundary price can be slightly above or below the displayed threshold. For a combined total-surplus calculation, use buyer and seller schedules **for the same good**, add CS and PS, and compare marginal buyer value with marginal seller cost. If the next value falls below the next cost, that trade lowers total surplus.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use willingness to pay and opportunity cost, not only transaction price, to measure gains.
2. Separate a surplus transfer from lost or newly created total surplus.
3. State the competitive-market assumptions before claiming efficiency.
