# Chapter 18: Oligopoly

## Core Idea
With few sellers, each firm's output or price affects rivals. Joint profit favors collusion, but each participant may gain by defecting, producing an outcome between monopoly and perfect competition.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Strategic-interdependence test**: Before changing output, forecast the response of the other few firms. A market-share gain for one seller can lower profits for all through price effects.
- **Cartel versus Nash outcome**: A cartel acts as one monopolist and restricts total output. Each member has an incentive to exceed its quota; if all do, quantity rises and price falls. A Nash equilibrium is a set of choices in which no player benefits from unilateral change.
- **Prisoners' dilemma**: Use a payoff matrix to ask whether individually rational defection defeats a jointly better cooperative outcome. Repeated interaction can alter incentives but does not guarantee cooperation.

## Key Concepts
- **Oligopoly**: Market dominated by a small number of sellers.
- **Collusion**: Agreement on output or prices.
- **Cartel**: Group of firms acting jointly as a monopolist.
- **Nash equilibrium**: Strategies stable against unilateral deviation.
- **Antitrust law**: Rules aimed at preserving competition, especially against collusion.

## Mental Models
- Distinguish what firms would choose collectively from what each chooses given rivals' likely behavior.
- More independent sellers generally push outcomes toward the competitive benchmark.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming cartel quotas enforce themselves**: Each participant may profit from secretly selling more.
- **Treating every collaboration as illegal collusion**: Some joint activity may have legitimate efficiency benefits; inspect the conduct.

## Worked Example
Mankiw uses the tennis-ball market to motivate a few-seller setting. If two sellers jointly restrict balls, they can mimic monopoly profit. After agreeing on a quota, each has reason to add units while the market price remains high. If both do so, the price drops and neither captures the full cartel gain. A payoff matrix reveals why a mutually attractive agreement can be unstable without credible enforcement.

The book's numerical duopoly is Jack and Jill selling water with zero marginal pumping cost. Acting jointly, they can sell 60 gallons total at $60 and split production 30/30 for $1,800 profit each. After the agreement, each can instead produce 40 gallons. If Jill stays at 30, Jack's profit rises to $2,000, while Jill earns $1,500. If Jill also switches to 40, Jack still earns more by choosing 40 ($1,600 instead of $1,500). The incentives are symmetric, so both choose 40 and earn $1,600 each—less than the $1,800 each under cooperation. The self-enforcing outcome follows the payoff comparisons, not simply the label "prisoners' dilemma."

## Reference Tables
| Jack / Jill output | Jill 30 gallons | Jill 40 gallons |

| Jack 30 gallons | Jack $1,800; Jill $1,800 | Jack $1,500; Jill $2,000 |
| Jack 40 gallons | Jack $2,000; Jill $1,500 | Jack $1,600; Jill $1,600 |

The matrix uses the chapter's demand schedule and zero marginal cost. A different demand curve or cost structure changes the payoffs, and repeated dealings can change the incentives to deviate.

## Key Takeaways
1. Model rivals' reactions, not a firm's choice in isolation.
2. Compare cartel, Nash, and competitive quantities and prices.
3. Apply antitrust analysis to specific strategic conduct.
