## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Ten Principles of Economics
Use the ten principles as questions about a decision: What alternative is sacrificed? What happens at the margin? Which incentives change? Who gains from trade? Does a market failure justify intervention? In macro questions, distinguish productivity-driven living standards, sustained money growth and inflation, and the short-run inflation–unemployment relationship. The principles organize analysis; they do not by themselves settle a policy choice.

### Three Steps for Analyzing Changes in Equilibrium
For a market shock, identify whether demand or supply shifts, determine direction, then compare the new intersection with the old. A good's own price change is movement *along* a curve; income, costs, expectations, tastes, technology, or participant counts can shift a curve. If both shift, one outcome may remain indeterminate without magnitude evidence. Use [ch04](chapters/ch04-supply-and-demand.md) and add elasticity from [ch05](chapters/ch05-elasticity.md) when size matters.

### Opportunity cost and comparative advantage
Value an action by its best forgone alternative, including implicit costs. For trade, calculate each producer's opportunity cost in the other good and specialize by **comparative**, not absolute, advantage. A mutually accepted exchange ratio must lie between opportunity costs. Use [ch03](chapters/ch03-interdependence-and-trade.md) before making claims about trade gains and [ch09](chapters/ch09-international-trade.md) to analyze the distribution of those gains.

### Welfare and market failures
Count consumer surplus, producer surplus, and, where relevant, government revenue separately. Competitive equilibrium maximizes their sum only under assumptions such as no important externalities and informed, competitive exchange. A tax can transfer surplus and destroy some through lost trades; greater elasticity generally enlarges the loss. Diagnose third-party costs, public-good free riding, or common-resource overuse before selecting corrective tax, subsidy, permit, property-right, or provision tools. Use [ch07](chapters/ch07-consumer-producer-surplus.md), [ch08](chapters/ch08-costs-of-taxation.md), [ch10](chapters/ch10-externalities.md), and [ch11](chapters/ch11-public-goods-common-resources.md).

### Costs, competition, and strategic structure
For a competitive firm, compare price with marginal cost for output, average variable cost for short-run shutdown, and average total cost for long-run exit. A monopolist instead chooses where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, then reads price from demand. Monopolistic competition adds differentiated products with free entry; oligopoly adds strategic reactions and possible cartel defection. Diagnose the market structure before applying a pricing rule. See [ch14](chapters/ch14-costs-of-production.md)–[ch18](chapters/ch18-oligopoly.md).

### Macro measurement before explanation
GDP is domestic final production in a period and satisfies `Y = C + I + G + NX`; use real GDP to separate quantity from price. CPI tracks a fixed consumer basket and has substitution, new-good, and quality limitations. Living standards depend on output per worker, which reflects capital, skills, resources, and technology. In a closed economy `S = I`; in an open economy `S = I + NCO` and `NX = NCO`. Define period, border, price base, and economy type before interpreting a statistic. See [ch24](chapters/ch24-gdp.md)–[ch27](chapters/ch27-saving-investment-financial-system.md) and [ch32](chapters/ch32-open-economy-basics.md).

### Time, risk, and monetary reasoning
Compare future cash flows in today's dollars using `PV = FV/(1+r)^N` with a risk-appropriate rate. Diversification reduces firm-specific, not all, risk. Persistent inflation is linked to money growth relative to real output under assumptions about velocity and long-run output; the quantity equation `MV = PY` is an identity, not a causal conclusion by itself. Use [ch28](chapters/ch28-tools-of-finance.md), [ch30](chapters/ch30-monetary-system.md), and [ch31](chapters/ch31-money-growth-inflation.md).

### Short-run stabilization and evidence
Classify an output shock as aggregate-demand or short-run-supply before predicting prices and unemployment. Money and fiscal policy can shift demand, while policy lags, multipliers, and crowding out affect the result. A short-run Phillips curve shifts when inflation expectations or supply conditions change; it is not a permanent menu. Test empirical claims for confounding, reverse causality, and uncertainty before treating a correlation as a lever. Use [ch34](chapters/ch34-aggregate-demand-supply.md)–[ch38](chapters/ch38-economists-use-data.md).

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the tenth-edition PDF, including its 37 chapters and data-analysis appendix. The chapter files synthesize the book; they do not substitute for the original figures or full numerical tables. Docling structured extraction improved headings and formulas, but its table parser reported some unmatched cells. Before using a specific table value, equation typesetting, or policy statistic in a calculation, compare it with the PDF page. Worked examples are teaching cases, not current market estimates or policy recommendations.
