## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Separate income, assets, liabilities, and cash flow
Use this before judging whether a purchase or career choice improves financial position. Track three distinct questions:

- Does it produce or consume cash after realistic costs?
- Does it increase net worth, and how liquid is that value?
- Does it depend on continued labor, debt, market appreciation, or a business system?

The book's memorable rule—wealthy people buy assets, poor people have expenses, and the middle class often buys liabilities they call assets—is a teaching shortcut, not a replacement for accounting definitions. A home can be an accounting asset and still be a monthly cash drain; an investment can have positive cash flow and still be overpriced or risky.

### 2. Keep a job if useful; build an owned asset base in parallel
“Mind your own business” means distinguish the job that currently pays you from the asset base you own and develop. Employment, freelancing, or a profession can provide capital, stability, relationships, and skills. The question is whether all surplus is consumed or whether some is deliberately converted into reserves, business equity, securities, intellectual property, or another understandable asset.

### 3. Escape the income–expense treadmill through choice, not a slogan
The “rat race” is a loop: more income produces more spending or debt, which preserves dependence on labor. When emotion rises around money, pause and inspect the incentive and cash-flow mechanism. Do not respond to fear with an impulsive resignation, or to ambition with leverage. Increase choice by improving savings capacity, skills, asset cash flow, and decision quality.

### 4. Treat financial intelligence as a skill stack
The book groups financial intelligence around accounting, investing, market understanding, and law. Later, it emphasizes complementary skills such as communication, sales, and management. Use the stack to find blind spots: if you cannot explain the cash flow, customer, legal rights, tax treatment, or downside, the opportunity is not yet understood.

### 5. Understand structures and rules before optimizing taxes
Companies and other legal structures can affect ownership, liability, taxes, and how a business operates. Compare after-tax, after-fee, after-financing results—not headline returns. The book's legal and tax examples are historical and primarily U.S.-based; current rules depend on jurisdiction, entity, contracts, and facts. Consult qualified local professionals before acting.

### 6. Invest as information gathering plus risk management
Investment is not merely buying. Verify the source, economics, counterparties, costs, liquidity, leverage, and exit path. Define the maximum loss and the conditions that would invalidate the thesis. Use advisers to fill genuine knowledge gaps, but understand the assumptions yourself. Risk is managed, not wished away; “no money down” and guaranteed-return stories deserve extra scrutiny.

### 7. Convert knowledge into small, reversible practice
The book's action section pairs a strong personal reason with daily choices, continuous education, careful selection of mentors, self-discipline, professional help, learning from examples, and iterative action. Use a loop: observe → study → ask practitioners → model cash flows → test small → review → change or stop. Action means a controlled experiment, not a rushed trade.

### 8. Name the five obstacles
When knowledge is not producing action, check **fear**, **cynicism**, **laziness**, **bad habits**, and **arrogance**. Put each obstacle into observable behavior: avoiding numbers, dismissing every opportunity, postponing budgeting, upgrading consumption, or refusing expertise. Design a guardrail—automatic saving, a written downside, a review date, or a second opinion—then measure whether behavior changes.
