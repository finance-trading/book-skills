## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Start with an edge of observation, not a tip
Use ordinary life, work, suppliers, customers, and local businesses as a source of leads. A lead is not a buy signal. Convert it into a company story, then test that story against earnings, financial condition, valuation, and the reason the opportunity is not already fully recognized.

The amateur's advantage is often **time, attention, and local knowledge**. Professionals may have more data but can be constrained by mandates, benchmark pressure, committee decisions, and short review horizons. Use that advantage only inside a circle of competence: invest where you can explain the business plainly.

### 2. The six stock categories
Classify the company before asking what it should do in your portfolio:

- **Slow growers**: mature businesses, usually dividend-oriented; expect modest growth and watch whether the dividend and earnings support the thesis.
- **Stalwarts**: large, resilient companies with moderate growth; useful for defense and steady compounding, but price matters.
- **Fast growers**: small or mid-sized companies capable of rapid earnings growth; highest upside and meaningful execution/saturation risk.
- **Cyclicals**: earnings rise and fall with an industry cycle; understand inventory, prices, capacity, and timing rather than applying a permanent growth multiple.
- **Turnarounds**: distressed or broken stories where recovery, not ordinary growth, is the thesis; balance-sheet survival is the first test.
- **Asset plays**: a valuable asset, subsidiary, land position, cash balance, or other hidden value that the market may be overlooking.

The category determines what evidence matters, the likely reason to own the stock, and the correct sell trigger. Do not use a fast-grower checklist on a cyclical or a turnaround.

### 3. Develop the story before buying
Treat the first observation like an anonymous tip. In a short first pass, answer: what does the company do; why can earnings rise; what is the catalyst or runway; what can go wrong; and what evidence would disprove the thesis? Then read the annual and quarterly reports, understand the balance sheet, compare valuation with growth, and write the reason you own it.

A good story is specific enough to monitor. “This is a great company” is not a story. “It can replicate a profitable store format across an underpenetrated region while funding expansion internally” is.

### 4. Earnings, assets, and price are the bridge from business to stock
A share is part ownership of a business, not a lottery ticket. Lynch gives earnings priority: future earnings and the multiple investors pay for them drive value. Use the P/E ratio as a starting comparison, not a verdict. Compare the company's P/E with its growth rate, its own history, peers, industry cyclicality, debt, and the durability of the earnings.

A rough growth/valuation heuristic is the **PEG-style comparison**: a company growing earnings around 15% may be more reasonably priced at a P/E near 15 than at 40, all else equal. It is not a law; adjust for debt, cyclicality, margins, one-time items, and the quality of growth.

### 5. Match research intensity to the thesis
The two-minute drill is a triage device, not a replacement for research. In a quick scan, identify the business category, recent earnings direction, P/E, balance-sheet risk, and the one-sentence story. If the story cannot survive this screen, stop. If it can, investigate through filings, company calls, broker research used critically, management conversations, store visits, product observation, and industry sources.

### 6. Recheck the story; sell when facts change
Review the latest earnings, reports, and operating evidence every few months. Fast growers move from start-up to rapid expansion to maturity; the middle phase can offer the best runway, while saturation demands a new growth engine. Sell because the original story is broken, the valuation becomes unreasonable relative to the story, the company changes category, or a better use of capital appears—not merely because the quote moved.

### 7. Build a portfolio around knowledge and conviction
Own enough positions to reduce single-company risk, but not so many that you cannot follow the stories. Add to winners when the facts and valuation still support the thesis; do not automatically add to losers. “Watering the weeds” means funding weak ideas while neglecting the strongest ones. Portfolio design should reflect the investor's time, skill, risk tolerance, and the categories represented—not an arbitrary stock count.

### 8. Resist price myths and macro distraction
A stock that fell can fall further; a low nominal share price is not automatically cheap; a high price can rise further; and a good company can be a bad investment at an excessive price. Do not wait for a perfect market forecast. If the company story and valuation are sound, market noise is usually a weaker signal than business facts. Options, futures, and shorting add complexity and risk that do not improve the basic process for most long-term investors.
