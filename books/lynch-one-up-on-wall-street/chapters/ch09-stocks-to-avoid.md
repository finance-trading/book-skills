# Chapter 9: Stocks I'd Avoid

## Core Idea
Some stock stories are structurally dangerous: the hottest successor, diversification that weakens the core, whispered secrets, fragile middlemen, and companies whose appeal is mostly an exciting label.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Beware the next something**: Use this when a company is promoted chiefly as “the next” successful firm. Compare business models, unit economics, competition, and valuation instead of borrowing the earlier winner's reputation.
- **Avoid diworsification**: Use this when management expands into unrelated businesses. Test whether acquisitions earn attractive returns or merely enlarge the empire.
- **Beware whispers, middlemen, and exciting names**: Use these filters against secrecy, weak bargaining power, and marketing language. Public evidence should carry the thesis.

## Key Concepts
- **Diworsification**: Lynch's term for diversification that worsens business quality or shareholder results.
- **Whisper stock**: A story dependent on rumors, secret technology, or inaccessible evidence.
- **Middleman risk**: Vulnerability when suppliers or customers can bypass the company or squeeze its margin.
- **Promotion risk**: Hype that substitutes a name or narrative for financial performance.
- **Empire building**: Acquisitions driven by management ambition rather than owner value.

## Mental Models
- Think of diversification as neutral until returns on the new activity are proven.
- Use secrecy as a negative: if the thesis cannot be checked, position size should normally be zero.
- Ask who has power in the chain among supplier, company, and customer.

## Anti-patterns
- **Analogy investing**: Buying a company because it resembles a past winner ignores the price and competitive differences.
- **Conglomerate optimism**: Assuming every acquisition adds value hides weak reinvestment.
- **Name-based enthusiasm**: Biotechnology, electronics, or another fashionable word in a name is not an advantage.

## Worked Example
A profitable niche manufacturer uses its cash to buy unrelated restaurants and a speculative technology venture. The investor separates the original high-return business from the new activities, checks management's record on acquisitions, and treats the unrelated expansion as a reason to lower confidence unless returns and strategy are clearly demonstrated.

## Key Takeaways
1. “The next” famous stock is a sales pitch until proven otherwise.
2. Unrelated diversification can destroy a good core business.
3. Rumors and exciting labels are weak evidence.
4. Middlemen need a durable reason for their margin.
