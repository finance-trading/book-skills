# Chapter 16: Designing a Portfolio

## Core Idea
Portfolio construction should reflect how many stories the investor can genuinely follow and how the positions complement one another. More names do not automatically mean more safety.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **How many is too many**: Use knowledge and monitoring time to set the number of holdings. A smaller understood portfolio can be safer than a long list of forgotten stories.
- **Spreading it around**: Use diversification across genuine risks and categories, not duplication of the same bet under different ticker symbols.
- **Watering the weeds**: Use this warning when tempted to add money to losers while neglecting winners. Add to positions whose facts and valuation remain favorable; do not fund weakness by habit.

## Key Concepts
- **Conviction weighting**: Giving more capital to the best-understood and most attractive ideas.
- **Monitoring capacity**: The number of stories an investor can recheck responsibly.
- **Diversification**: Exposure to different drivers of return and failure.
- **Winner reinforcement**: Continuing support for a thesis that is working and still reasonably priced.
- **Weed**: A holding whose operating story is deteriorating or was weak from the start.

## Mental Models
- Think of the portfolio as a garden: cultivate healthy plants and remove weeds.
- Use correlation of stories, not merely the count of stocks, to assess concentration.
- Let position size follow evidence and downside resilience.

## Anti-patterns
- **Token diversification**: Owning dozens of similar companies creates the appearance of safety without reducing the shared risk.
- **Automatic averaging down**: Buying more solely because the price fell ignores new facts and opportunity cost.
- **Indiscriminate selling of winners**: Cutting the strongest, still-attractive stories merely to equalize position sizes can remove the portfolio's best compounding engines.

## Worked Example
An investor owns eight regional banks with the same interest-rate and credit exposure and calls the portfolio diversified. Lynch's framework treats that as one large bet. The investor instead compares the banks' stories, keeps the strongest, and considers a genuinely different business only if its thesis is equally well understood.

## Key Takeaways
1. Own as many stocks as you can follow well.
2. Diversify by underlying story and risk, not ticker count.
3. Do not automatically water weeds.
4. Let improving evidence, not pride or habit, guide additions.
