# Chapter 6: Stalking the Tenbagger

## Core Idea
The search for a tenbagger—a stock that rises many times in value—often starts close to home, but only disciplined follow-up turns exposure into an investment edge.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Close-to-home search**: Use this when looking for leads. Observe employers, customers, suppliers, stores, products, and professional changes before scanning unfamiliar industries.
- **Lead versus tip**: Use it to classify information. A crowd at a retailer or a price increase at a supplier is a prompt for research, not a recommendation.
- **Double edge**: Use it to recognize that proximity cuts both ways. Employees may see a change early, but loyalty, inside information rules, and overconfidence can distort judgment.

## Key Concepts
- **Tenbagger**: Lynch's term for a stock that multiplies in price manyfold; the label describes outcome, not a guarantee.
- **Local knowledge**: Familiarity with a product, workplace, or geography that can reveal a change early.
- **Professional circle**: Industry participants who may notice pricing, demand, or competitive shifts.
- **Follow-up**: The required investigation of financials and valuation after a lead.
- **Edge**: A specific informational or analytical advantage, not a general feeling.

## Mental Models
- Think of daily life as a stream of testable hypotheses.
- Use your job and consumption patterns to narrow the research universe.
- Ask, “What do I know earlier or understand better than the average investor?”

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying the familiar automatically**: Loving a product does not establish attractive economics or price.
- **Ignoring the home field**: Restricting research to famous stocks wastes the investor's most natural information advantage.
- **Acting on nonpublic information**: Workplace insight must stay within applicable law and company policy; material nonpublic information is not a permissible edge.

## Worked Example
A supplier sees a retail chain opening profitable stores and paying reliably. That is the lead. The investor then identifies the public company, checks whether expansion can continue, examines sales and earnings trends, debt, competition, and store-level economics, and buys only if the valuation leaves room for the expected growth.

## Key Takeaways
1. Search first where your knowledge is strongest.
2. A tenbagger begins as a lead, not a conclusion.
3. Customers, employees, and suppliers may notice changes before analysts.
4. Familiarity creates both opportunity and bias.
