# Chapter 4: Passing the Mirror Test

## Core Idea
A good stock can still be the wrong purchase if the investor's finances, time horizon, or temperament cannot support it. Suitability comes before security selection.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Mirror Test**: Use this before studying any company. Ask whether you already have basic financial security, whether you need the money soon, and whether your behavior under losses will let you hold a sound investment.
- **House-before-portfolio question**: Use it to prioritize essential personal finances and liquidity. Stock ideas do not override an unstable financial foundation.
- **Personal qualities test**: Use it to assess patience, independence, tolerance for boredom, and willingness to do homework.

## Key Concepts
- **Suitability**: Fit between an investment and the owner's circumstances.
- **Liquidity need**: A known near-term use of money that makes volatility unacceptable.
- **Patience**: Willingness to let business results, not daily prices, decide the outcome.
- **Self-reliance**: Ability to examine evidence rather than outsource conviction.
- **Loss tolerance**: Capacity to avoid panic selling during a normal decline.

## Mental Models
- Think of the investor as part of the investment system; weak temperament can ruin a strong thesis.
- Use personal constraints as hard filters before valuation.
- Separate “Can this stock work?” from “Can I responsibly own it?”

## Anti-patterns
- **Starting with a famous company**: A strong business does not answer whether the buyer should own stocks at all.
- **Borrowing conviction**: Depending on another person to remain calm substitutes their temperament for yours.

## Worked Example
Someone asks whether a major industrial company is a good investment. The first questions are instead: Is emergency savings handled? Will this money be needed for a house, tuition, or living expenses? Will the buyer read reports and sit through a 20–30% decline without abandoning the thesis? Only passing answers move the discussion to the company.

## Key Takeaways
1. Investor readiness precedes stock analysis.
2. Near-term spending needs argue against volatile equity exposure.
3. Temperament is a practical risk factor.
4. A good company is not automatically a suitable holding.
