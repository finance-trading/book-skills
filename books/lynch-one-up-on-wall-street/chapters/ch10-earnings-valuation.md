# Chapter 10: Earnings, Earnings, Earnings

## Core Idea
Stock value ultimately follows earnings and assets, especially earnings. Price ratios are tools for comparing those fundamentals, not independent reasons to buy.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Earnings-and-assets valuation**: Use this for every operating company. Estimate what the business can earn and what financial resources support those earnings, then compare that picture with the current price.
- **P/E as a comparative tool**: Use the price/earnings ratio against the company's growth, history, peers, and the market. A low or high number means nothing until context is attached.
- **Growth-to-P/E heuristic**: Use a rough comparison between earnings growth and P/E as a first valuation screen. A moderate P/E with durable fast growth is more interesting than a high P/E supported only by excitement.

## Key Concepts
- **Earnings**: The profit available to owners, checked for quality and recurrence.
- **Assets**: Resources that can produce earnings or be sold, net of obligations.
- **P/E ratio**: Price divided by earnings; a shorthand for what buyers pay for profits.
- **Growth rate**: The expected pace of earnings expansion, not revenue alone.
- **Market P/E**: The valuation backdrop used for comparison, not a trading signal.

## Mental Models
- Think of a share as a claim on a business, comparable to buying a local laundromat or apartment building.
- Use earnings as the primary engine and assets as support, collateral, or an alternative source of value.
- Treat future earnings as an estimate requiring evidence, not a number to accept from enthusiasm.

## Anti-patterns
- **Lottery-ticket thinking**: Treating a low-priced share as a cheap claim ignores earnings and share count.
- **P/E absolutism**: Calling 10 cheap or 30 expensive without growth, cyclicality, debt, and margin context.
- **Peak-earnings complacency**: Using unusually high cyclical earnings can make a stock look cheaper than it is.

## Worked Example
A company earns $1 per share and trades at $15, so its P/E is 15. If evidence supports roughly 15% durable annual earnings growth, the growth and multiple are balanced on Lynch's rough screen. If growth is 5%, the same P/E is less compelling; if earnings are cyclical and currently at a peak, even a P/E of 8 can be misleading. Debt, margins, and one-time gains are checked before accepting the ratio.

## Key Takeaways
1. Earnings and assets anchor business value.
2. The P/E ratio must be contextualized.
3. Growth can justify a higher multiple, but only while it is credible.
4. Asset value matters most when earnings alone do not explain the opportunity.
