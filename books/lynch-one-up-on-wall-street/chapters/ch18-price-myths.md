# Chapter 18: The Twelve Silliest (and Most Dangerous) Things People Say About Stock Prices

## Core Idea
Common remarks about price often substitute intuition for valuation. Lynch uses memorable fallacies to force attention back to earnings, assets, and business conditions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Price-fallacy filter**: Use this whenever a buy or sell argument mentions only past price movement, nominal share price, or a psychological “bottom.” Translate the remark into a claim about business value and test that claim.
- **Nominal-price warning**: Use it when a low dollar price is called safe. Price per share without share count, earnings, debt, and assets says nothing about cheapness.
- **Reversal and continuation skepticism**: Use it against claims that a fallen stock cannot fall more or a rising stock cannot rise more. Both directions remain possible until fundamentals decide.

## Key Concepts
- **Nominal price**: The quoted price per share, which depends arbitrarily on share count.
- **Bottom**: A retrospective low, not something reliably announced in advance.
- **Cheapness**: A relationship between price and business value.
- **Loss potential**: The portion of capital that can be lost, which can be 100% at any nominal price.
- **Expectation**: The future performance already implied by the current price.

## Mental Models
- Think of share price as a numerator, never as the whole valuation.
- Use business deterioration to explain why a stock can keep falling.
- Ask what earnings or asset outcome would justify the current and projected price.

## Anti-patterns
- **“It can't go much lower”**: A large prior decline does not create a floor.
- **“It can't go higher”**: Strong business progress can continue to surprise.
- **“It's only $3”**: Three dollars per share can still represent total loss, while a high-priced share can be inexpensive relative to earnings.
- **“You can tell the bottom”**: Attempts to identify it from price behavior alone are unreliable.

## Worked Example
A stock falls from $40 to $12 and is promoted as safe because it is “down so much” and “only $12.” The investor calculates market value, remaining earnings power, debt, and liquidity. If earnings have collapsed and liabilities threaten the equity, $12 can still be expensive. If earnings remain intact and the balance sheet is strong, the decline may be an opportunity—but the low number itself proved neither conclusion.

## Key Takeaways
1. Price movement is not a valuation.
2. There is no dependable way to announce a bottom from price alone.
3. Low nominal prices can still produce total losses.
4. Rising stocks can remain attractive when business value rises faster.
