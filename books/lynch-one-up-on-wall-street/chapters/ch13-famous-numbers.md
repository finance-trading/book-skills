# Chapter 13: Some Famous Numbers

## Core Idea
A handful of financial relationships reveals whether the story is financially credible. Lynch emphasizes practical interpretation over complicated formulas.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Famous-numbers screen**: Use sales, earnings, P/E, cash, debt, dividends, book value, and cash flow together. No single figure is allowed to carry the decision.
- **Balance-sheet survival test**: Use cash and debt to determine whether the company can endure weakness or fund its plan. This is especially important for cyclicals and turnarounds.
- **Cash-flow and dividend check**: Use cash generation to test earnings quality and dividend sustainability. A dividend funded by an exhausted business is not automatically attractive.

## Key Concepts
- **Cash**: Liquidity that can support operations, expansion, or survival.
- **Debt**: Fixed obligations that magnify both gains and failure risk.
- **Book value**: Accounting equity that may differ greatly from economic value.
- **Cash flow**: Cash generated by operations after necessary distinctions between maintenance and growth spending.
- **Dividend**: Cash returned to holders when management cannot reinvest it attractively—or, less favorably, when the payout is unsustainable.

## Mental Models
- Think of numbers as questions: growth funded how, earnings convertible to cash how, and downside survived how.
- Use changes over several periods rather than one isolated year.
- Match each ratio to the category. Debt that is routine for one business may threaten another.

## Anti-patterns
- **Single-ratio investing**: Buying only because of a low P/E, high dividend, or discount to book value.
- **Ignoring the denominator**: Share issuance can make sales growth coexist with weak per-share progress.
- **Accounting literalism**: Treating book value or reported earnings as economic truth without examining assets and one-time items.

## Worked Example
A company reports rising earnings and a high dividend. The investor checks whether cash is also rising, whether receivables or inventories are ballooning, whether debt funds the payout, and whether shares outstanding are increasing. If earnings are not turning into cash or the dividend depends on borrowing, the apparently attractive yield does not confirm the story.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use a small set of numbers consistently.
2. Cash and debt determine resilience and funding capacity.
3. Per-share results matter more than headline growth.
4. Dividends and book value require quality checks.
