## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Read statements adversarially, not cynically
Financial reporting communicates information and helps companies obtain capital; management also has incentives to present results favorably. Assume neither perfect neutrality nor inevitable fraud. Translate the narrative into accounting effects, cash consequences, and incentives, then corroborate material claims across statements and notes. Be especially skeptical when reported growth is smooth despite a volatile business, explanations rely on one-off adjustments, or compensation rewards the reported metric (Ch 1, 9, 11).

### Reconstruct the economics behind each statement
The balance sheet is a dated stock of resources and claims; the income statement allocates performance across a period; cash flow reconciles cash movement but does not by itself establish sustainable earnings. Reconcile all three. Common-size statements reveal composition and trends; ratios are prompts for investigation, not conclusions. Distinguish accounting recognition from the underlying event and ask what estimate or policy links them (Ch 2–4).

### Test earnings quality with recognition, conversion, and recurrence
For each revenue or expense, ask: Was it recognized in the right period? Is its measurement supportable? Does it recur? Does cash or a balance-sheet movement corroborate it? A receivable build faster than sales, changing contract terms, capitalized costs, delayed expense recognition, or recurring “special” charges can weaken reported earnings. No single flag proves manipulation; seek a coherent pattern and consider benign business explanations (Ch 5–7, 11).

### Treat EBITDA as a partial lens
EBITDA removes financing, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, but equipment still wears out, replacement may cost more than historic depreciation, working capital consumes cash, and debt requires payment. Use it only with capital expenditure, working-capital, and fixed-charge analysis. A convenient adjusted metric is not equivalent to distributable cash (Ch 8).

### Turn historical relationships into explicit forecasts
Forecast sales from industry and economic drivers, then derive margins, expenses, working capital, investment, and financing assumptions. State what must happen for each assumption to hold; use scenarios where cyclicality, competition, or management action creates uncertainty. Project statements together so growth has a funding path and forecast ratios remain internally consistent (Ch 12).

### Separate credit capacity from equity upside
Credit analysis asks whether and when obligations can be paid, including contingent and off-balance-sheet claims, refinancing needs, and downside recoveries. Equity analysis values residual claims on uncertain future earnings or dividends and must account for dilution, leverage, cyclicality, and discount-rate risk. Use ratios consistently among peers, but stop when more ratios add no insight: the objective is a risk judgment, not a ratio count (Ch 13–14).
