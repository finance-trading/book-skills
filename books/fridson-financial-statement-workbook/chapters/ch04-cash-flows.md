# Chapter 4: The Statement of Cash Flows

## Core Idea
The cash-flow statement explains how operating, investing, and financing activities changed cash. It complements accrual earnings but is not a standalone measure of value: cash may be temporarily improved by deferring payments, selling assets, or raising financing.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-section bridge**: Classify cash movement into operating, investing, and financing activities; identify the source of cash and whether it can recur.
- **Earnings-to-cash reconciliation**: Compare net income with operating cash flow and explain major noncash charges, working-capital movements, and classification effects.
- **Funding-cycle review**: Compare operating cash generation with capital investment, distributions, and debt needs to determine whether growth is self-funded or depends on external capital.

## Key Concepts
- **Operating cash flow (CFO)**: Cash flow classified as arising from operating activities; classification rules and working-capital timing matter.
- **Capital expenditure (capex)**: Cash investment in long-lived assets, generally classified as investing activity.
- **Working capital**: Short-term operating assets and liabilities; growth can absorb cash.
- **Free cash flow**: A derived measure, commonly CFO less capex, whose exact definition must be stated.

## Mental Models
- Profitability and liquidity answer different questions.
- A source of cash can be temporary even if reported in a recurring section.
- Follow cash through all three sections; the ending cash balance alone hides financing dependence.

## Anti-patterns
- Calling CFO minus capex “cash available to equity” without considering debt service, leases, acquisitions, or other claims.
- Equating a single positive CFO period with sustainable conversion.

## Worked Example
If earnings increase but CFO falls, bridge the difference by identifying receivables, inventory, payables, and noncash items. Then check whether receivables growth reflects faster sales, slower collections, or a change in terms. Determine whether capex and financing cash flows preserve adequate liquidity; do not infer fraud from a single working-capital swing.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reconcile earnings to operating cash, not just net change in cash.
2. Identify whether cash comes from operations, asset sales, or financing.
3. Analyze working-capital intensity alongside growth.
4. State the definition whenever using free cash flow.
