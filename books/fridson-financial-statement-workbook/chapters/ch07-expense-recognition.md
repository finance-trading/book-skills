# Chapter 7: Expense Recognition

## Core Idea
Expense choices are a mirror image of revenue choices. Management can alter reported results by accelerating, deferring, capitalizing, or reclassifying costs. The analyst reconstructs when economic consumption occurred and whether unusual items are genuinely rare.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Matching test**: Compare expense recognition with the period in which the related revenue or economic benefit was consumed. Investigate large deviations, particularly at period ends.
- **Capitalization audit**: Identify costs moved from expense to assets, the lives and assumptions used, and the cash effect of later impairment or amortization. A capitalized cost reduces current expense but does not eliminate the investment.
- **Recurring-surprise test**: Track “one-time,” “restructuring,” or “unusual” items by frequency and total. If they recur, reclassify them as part of normal cost economics.

## Key Concepts
- **Depreciation life**: An accounting assumption affecting reported earnings and asset carrying value; it does not determine actual asset wear.
- **Capitalization**: Treating a cost as an asset and recognizing it over time rather than immediately.
- **Restructuring charges**: Costs of changing operations; the economic cost may exceed the booked amount or recur.
- **Provision**: A liability estimate for uncertain future costs; its adequacy must be reassessed.

## Mental Models
- A cost is economically real even when accounting defers it.
- “One-time” is an operating description until evidence proves rarity.
- Compare expense policy changes before and after adoption to isolate the accounting effect.

## Anti-patterns
- Ignoring capitalization because it flatters reported earnings.
- Treating repeated unusual items as nonrecurring.

## Worked Example
A company capitalizes more software or inventory-related costs and reports a margin improvement. Reconstruct total economic cost by adding depreciation/amortization and working-capital effects, compare policy changes to peers, and inspect impairment history. If margins depend on longer assumed lives or weak review, treat the improvement with caution.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask when the related benefit was consumed.
2. Rebuild total costs after deferral and reclassification.
3. Track “unusual” items over time.
4. Review assumptions, not just the recognized expense.
