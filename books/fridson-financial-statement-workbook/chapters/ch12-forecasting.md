# Chapter 12: Forecasting Financial Statements

## Core Idea
A forecast extends historical relationships under explicit assumptions about economic conditions, competition, and managerial actions. Forecasts matter because future earnings, dividends, and debt-service capacity drive equity and credit judgments.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Driver-based forecast sequence**: Forecast demand and sales; translate them into volume, pricing, and mix; forecast gross margin and operating costs; project working capital and investment; then model financing, taxes, and cash. Keep the statements linked.
- **Industry-fit method**: Use macroeconomic relationships where the business is economically sensitive and relationships are stable; use product, market-share, customer, or pipeline drivers when technology or “hit” outcomes weaken macro linkage.
- **Assumption and scenario discipline**: Record the evidence, owner, and sensitivity for each material assumption. Build base, downside, and upside paths where competition, demand, or execution is uncertain.

## Key Concepts
- **Sales forecast**: Estimate of future revenue grounded in market demand and company position.
- **Gross margin**: Revenue less direct costs as a share of sales; affected by price, input costs, product mix, and competitive intensity.
- **Segment information**: Disaggregated reporting that can improve forecasts but may omit expense allocations or gross-margin detail.
- **Sensitivity analysis**: Examination of outcome changes as an assumption varies.

## Mental Models
- Forecasts are conditional maps, not predictions with certainty.
- Every percentage growth path implies a scale, capacity, and funding requirement.
- A forecast that grows sales but ignores working capital and investment is incomplete.

## Anti-patterns
- Extrapolating a recent growth rate without testing the market ceiling or competitive response.
- Forecasting segment margins from partial data without making allocation assumptions explicit.

## Worked Example
To forecast an industrial producer, estimate end-market volumes from industry sources and economic drivers; add company-specific share and pricing assumptions; translate sales into gross margin based on input costs and competition. Project receivables, inventory, capex, and debt alongside earnings. Stress lower demand and tighter pricing to see whether cash can meet obligations.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make every major assumption explicit and evidence-linked.
2. Match the sales method to the industry’s economic drivers.
3. Project income, balance sheet, and cash flows together.
4. Stress the assumptions that dominate value or repayment capacity.
