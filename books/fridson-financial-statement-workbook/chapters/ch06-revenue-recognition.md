# Chapter 6: Revenue Recognition

## Core Idea
Revenue is the top-line anchor, but recognition rules and estimates determine how much is recognized when. The analyst tests whether reported revenue reflects transfer of goods or services, is measurable, and is collectible under the firm’s actual commercial terms.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Revenue five-point probe**: For material revenue, check (1) the transaction and its economic substance, (2) the point in time when control or performance obligation is satisfied, (3) measurability, (4) collectibility, and (5) disclosures about significant judgment. Apply current standard terminology while retaining the workbook's underlying logic.
- **Channel and timing check**: Compare revenue growth with receivables, billings, backlogs, returns, discounts, customer concentration, and contract changes. Ask whether sales are being accelerated into the period.
- **Contractual benchmarking**: Read significant contracts or disclosures for delivery, acceptance, warranty, repurchase, refund, and payment terms; use them to test management’s recognition story.

## Key Concepts
- **Collectibility**: The probability that promised consideration will be received; doubtful or extended terms weaken revenue quality.
- **Return and allowance policy**: Revenue may need offset for expected returns or variable consideration.
- **Principal versus agent**: Whether revenue is reported gross or net depends on the firm’s role in transferring the good or service.
- **Backlog and order flow**: Committed future revenue, not the same as cash sales.

## Mental Models
- Revenue is a claim to be supported, not the conclusion of the analysis.
- A rise in bookings does not establish a rise in cash-earning sales.
- Look for the customer’s incentives as well as the seller’s.

## Anti-patterns
- Accepting “recognition upon shipment” without inspecting acceptance, returns, and pricing terms.
- Treating a strong order backlog as assured future revenue.

## Worked Example
Revenue grows faster than receivables in one quarter but receivables then spike while cash flow slows. Reconcile the gap using average days sales outstanding, new terms, customer mix, and returns. A benign explanation might be a large new order; an aggressive one would pair late-period sales with extended terms and weak collections. Corroborate before choosing.

## Key Takeaways
1. Test recognition timing against the commercial contract.
2. Pair revenue growth with cash collection and returns.
3. Scrutinize judgment-heavy disclosures and customer concentration.
4. Distinguish gross from net revenue when the firm’s role is ambiguous.
