# Chapter 24: Valuing Private Firms

## Core Idea
Private-company valuation depends on the buyer, the transaction purpose, and information quality. A public-company DCF needs adjustments for owner compensation, capital structure, diversification, control, and marketability.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Private-firm input reconstruction**: Normalize financials, separate owner benefits from business expenses, estimate beta from traded comparables, and determine a feasible capital structure.
  - When to use: Private sale, IPO, venture stake, or owner planning.
- **Buyer-dependent value**: A diversified acquirer, undiversified owner, and public-market investor can assign different required returns and synergies to the same cash flows.
- **Control and liquidity separation**: Distinguish operating changes a buyer can cause from the cost or delay of selling a private stake.

## Key Concepts
- **Owner compensation adjustment**: Replace discretionary salary/perquisites with a market cost for equivalent management.
- **Bottom-up beta**: Business-risk estimate from public peers adjusted for the target's financial leverage.
- **Illiquidity discount**: Adjustment for inability to sell a private stake promptly, dependent on purpose and buyer.
- **Control premium**: Value of decision rights when a buyer can change operations or financing.
- **Venture capital stake**: Often has staged funding, preferred terms, and contingent ownership.

## Mental Models
- Do not apply one universal “private-company discount”; specify which risk or transaction friction the adjustment represents.
- Normalize cash flows before adjusting the discount rate, or the same owner-specific issue can be counted twice.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using the owner's reported earnings as distributable cash**: Owner pay and private expenses may distort it.
- **Applying public beta to an undiversified buyer without analysis**: The buyer's inability to diversify may change the relevant risk.
- **Adding a control premium to a control-value DCF**: The operating improvements may already be inside the forecast.

## Worked Example
The chapter analyzes Chez Pierre, a private restaurant, and InfoSoft, a private software company. For each, an analyst normalizes owner compensation and expenses, estimates business risk from comparable public firms, and changes assumptions for a private sale versus an IPO. The same restaurant may be worth more to an operator who can improve margins than to a passive minority investor, but the difference must be traced to specific cash flows, control rights, and required returns rather than a generic premium.

## Key Takeaways
1. Specify buyer, stake, and transaction purpose before valuing.
2. Normalize private accounts and derive risk from business comparables.
3. Tie control and illiquidity adjustments to real rights and frictions.
