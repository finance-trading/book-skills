# Chapter 34: Overview and Conclusion

## Core Idea
Choose the valuation approach from the asset's cash-flow structure, stage, information quality, and the decision being made. No single model is universally best; consistency of claim, assumptions, and purpose matters more than complexity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Approach selection**: Use intrinsic valuation for estimable cash flows, pricing for meaningful comparables, and contingent-claim models only for genuine optionality.
  - When to use: At the start of every assignment, before selecting spreadsheet templates.
- **Intrinsic model selection**: Decide equity versus firm claim, dividends versus FCFE versus FCFF, and stable versus multi-stage growth based on payout and capital structure.
- **Pricing model selection**: Choose an equity or enterprise multiple with a denominator that carries economic meaning for the company and peer group.
- **Model reconciliation**: Explain why DCF value and peer-implied price differ in their growth, return, risk, and market-expectation assumptions.

## Key Concepts
- **Model fit**: Alignment of valuation method with the asset, claimant, and available evidence.
- **Status-quo value**: Value under current management and strategy.
- **Optimal-management value**: Value after feasible improvements, requiring their costs and probability.
- **Underpriced versus undervalued**: Cheap relative to peers versus cheap relative to intrinsic cash-flow value; either can occur without the other.
- **Option-like asset**: Right with asymmetric payoff and a meaningful exercise decision.

## Mental Models
- Start from the decision: what claim is being bought, and what evidence can actually support its future cash flows?
- More models do not provide independent confirmation if they share the same growth story or market inputs.

## Anti-patterns
- **Template-first valuation**: It hides whether the model fits the business.
- **Mixing claim levels across methods**: Equity DCF and EV multiples need a bridge before comparison.
- **Interpreting cross-method divergence as a calculation error by default**: It may reveal different embedded assumptions.

## Decision Table
| Situation | Primary approach | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Stable payout business | DDM or FCFE | Are distributions sustainable? |
| Operating firm with changing leverage | FCFF/WACC or APV | Does financing treatment remain consistent? |
| Meaningful public peers | Relative pricing | Are multiples and fundamentals comparable? |
| Patent or deferrable project | Real-option valuation | Is exercise right genuine and uncounted in base DCF? |
| Young or distressed firm | Driver/scenario DCF, with option check if relevant | Can it survive to forecast cash flows? |

## Worked Example
The chapter distinguishes a firm that is undervalued by its cash-flow forecast from one that is underpriced relative to its peers. The first requires an explicit forecast and discount-rate case; the second requires comparable definitions and a peer-group case. A company can satisfy one test while failing the other if the whole sector is richly priced or if its operating outlook differs from peers. The analyst should state which claim supports the investment decision, then reconcile the other model rather than averaging two incompatible outputs.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choose the model only after specifying asset, claimant, and evidence.
2. Keep equity/firm and growth/risk assumptions consistent across methods.
3. Use divergent outputs to investigate assumptions, not to manufacture precision.
