# Chapter 27: Valuing Other Assets

## Core Idea
An asset should be valued according to whether it produces cash, serves as a collectible, or confers a scarce trophy benefit. Techniques that fit a franchise or practice do not automatically fit art or a one-of-a-kind asset.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cash-flow-producing asset DCF**: Forecast the income attributable to the specific license, franchise, practice, or restaurant and subtract renewal, maintenance, and regulatory costs.
  - When to use: Legal rights and operating assets with identifiable earning power.
- **Comparable pricing for collectibles**: Use recent transactions and condition/provenance adjustments when cash flow is absent.
- **Trophy-asset assessment**: State explicitly that status or enjoyment, not distributable cash, motivates a buyer and makes the value buyer-dependent.

## Key Concepts
- **Franchise right**: License to operate under a brand or restricted market, subject to fees and contract terms.
- **Scarcity rent**: Economic benefit from limited supply or legal restriction.
- **Renewal risk**: Chance that a license or franchise cannot be extended on similar terms.
- **Collectible**: Asset with resale/consumption value but no conventional operating cash flow.
- **Trophy asset**: Asset prized for prestige, uniqueness, or personal utility.

## Mental Models
- A legally scarce right can lose value if a technology or rule change destroys scarcity.
- If the asset lacks cash flow, a DCF cannot manufacture objectivity from an assumed resale price alone.

## Anti-patterns
- **Capitalizing peak cash flow forever**: Limited rights and competitive change matter.
- **Using public-company multiples for a personal practice**: The owner's labor may generate much of the earnings.
- **Calling subjective enjoyment an investment cash flow**: It belongs in a buyer-specific decision, not an operating DCF.

## Worked Example
The chapter values a New York City cab medallion by forecasting fare economics under a restricted-license regime, then exposes its sensitivity to competition and regulation. A dental practice requires an adjustment for the dentist's own labor before treating earnings as transferable business profit. The Lutèce restaurant illustration similarly separates the franchise's reputation from its costs and the continued role of its people. The cases show why “same earnings multiple” would miss asset-specific rights and transferability.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify the asset before choosing cash-flow valuation or comparable pricing.
2. Test legal duration, renewal, labor transferability, and disruption.
3. Treat collector and trophy prices as buyer-dependent evidence.
