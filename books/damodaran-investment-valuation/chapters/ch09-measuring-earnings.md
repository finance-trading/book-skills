# Chapter 9: Measuring Earnings

## Core Idea
Reported earnings are a starting point for valuation. Update them for the valuation date, normalize temporary items, and recast expenditures that build future assets rather than merely support current sales.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Updated earnings**: Combine the most recent annual report with later quarterly disclosures to produce a current twelve-month base before projecting.
  - When to use: Whenever the latest fiscal year is stale relative to the valuation date.
  - How: Replace the prior-year quarter(s) with the newly reported quarter(s), then reconcile to the company's reporting calendar.
- **R&D capitalization**: Treat research with a multi-year payoff as an investment; build an amortizing research asset from past R&D and adjust operating income by adding current R&D and subtracting amortization.
  - Why it works: It aligns the cost with years of expected benefit rather than forcing the entire investment into the current period.
- **Lease and one-time-item normalization**: Convert financing-like operating leases into debt-like commitments where appropriate, and separate nonrecurring gains and charges from sustainable operating earnings.

## Key Concepts
- **Trailing twelve months (TTM)**: Latest four-quarter operating period, constructed without double counting.
- **Research asset**: Estimated unamortized past R&D after capitalization.
- **Amortization life**: Number of years over which an investment's benefit is assumed to persist.
- **Normalized earnings**: Estimate of earnings from continuing operations under ordinary conditions.
- **Operating lease**: Contractual right to use an asset with payments that may have financing characteristics.

## Mental Models
- An accounting expense can be an economic investment; capitalizing it raises both earnings and invested capital.
- A one-time charge is not automatically ignorable: repeated “one-time” charges are part of the economics.

## Reference Formulas
- `TTM earnings = last reported fiscal-year earnings + current YTD earnings − comparable prior-year YTD earnings`. Use like periods and the same consolidated scope.
- `Adjusted operating income = reported operating income + current R&D expense − amortization of the research asset`. The research asset is the unamortized balance of prior research spending, using a stated useful life; depreciation/amortization and invested capital must be adjusted alongside earnings.

## Anti-patterns
- **Projecting from an obsolete annual number**: It can miss a major subsequent change.
- **Adding back every expense called unusual**: This systematically inflates the forecast base.
- **Capitalize only the income statement effect**: The asset, amortization, reinvestment, and return on capital must move together.

## Worked Example
The chapter updates Apple's earnings with interim results before using them in a valuation. It separately capitalizes Amgen's R&D: collect past research spending, choose a defensible amortization life, compute the remaining research asset, add current R&D back to operating income, and deduct current amortization. The adjustment changes both the current earnings base and the capital on which future returns are judged; it is not a free earnings boost. The Gap lease example similarly converts commitments consistently rather than adding lease debt without adjusting operating costs and asset value.

## Key Takeaways
1. Establish an as-of-date earnings base before forecasting.
2. Recast investment-like expenses symmetrically in income and capital.
3. Demand a consistent reason for excluding unusual items.
