# Chapter 3: Understanding Financial Statements

## Core Idea
Financial statements are raw material for valuation, not a finished economic description. Recast accounting numbers to identify operating assets, financing claims, profitability, and risk.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four financial-statement questions**: What assets does the firm own, how are they financed, how much do the assets earn, and how uncertain are those earnings?
  - When to use: At the beginning of any company valuation.
  - How: Map balance-sheet assets and liabilities, connect operating earnings to invested capital, and inspect coverage, volatility, and contingent claims.
- **Accounting-to-financial balance sheet**: Separate operating assets from cash/nonoperating holdings; distinguish debt from equity and operating liabilities.
  - Why it works: A valuation of operations can then be reconciled to equity without omitting or counting a claim twice.
- **Return versus cost of capital test**: Compare return on invested capital with the financing cost and return on equity with the cost of equity, using compatible numerators and denominators.

## Key Concepts
- **Book value**: Accounting measurement of assets or equity; it need not equal market value.
- **Invested capital**: Capital committed to operating assets by debt and equity providers.
- **ROIC**: After-tax operating earnings relative to invested capital.
- **ROE**: Net income relative to book equity, affected by operating performance and leverage.
- **Interest coverage**: Earnings available to service contractual interest.
- **Operating liability**: Liability arising from operations rather than a financing claim.

## Mental Models
- A firm can report high ROE because of leverage while earning a weak return on its assets.
- Accounting classifications help organize claims, but market-value reconciliation is a separate exercise.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equating book and market values**: Historical-cost and acquisition accounting can obscure asset economics.
- **Comparing mismatched returns**: ROIC and ROE answer different questions and require different hurdle rates.
- **Ignoring off-balance-sheet or contingent obligations**: Financing and risk can reside outside headline debt.

## Worked Example
The chapter compares RTX (formerly Raytheon) and Home Depot financial statements. The two firms report different mixes of tangible assets, intangibles, debt, operating liabilities, and earnings. An analyst would reconstruct each firm's invested capital and operating profit before comparing ROIC; comparing raw book assets or ROE alone would mix operating economics with financing and acquisition-accounting effects. The case also illustrates why reported EBITDA is not equivalent to cash flow: taxes, working capital, and reinvestment still have to be funded.

## Key Takeaways
1. Translate statement line items into operating assets and financing claims.
2. Compare returns with a hurdle rate for the same claimant.
3. Trace adjustments through income, balance sheet, and cash flow together.
