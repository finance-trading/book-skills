# Chapter 1: Introduction to Valuation

## Core Idea
An asset's value rests on the cash flows it can generate, their timing, growth, and risk. A market price is an observable transaction; a valuation is an explicit set of assumptions about those drivers.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pricing versus valuation**: Use valuation to estimate an asset's cash-flow-based worth and pricing to infer what others pay for comparable assets. State which question you are answering before selecting a model.
  - When to use: Before accepting a multiple, a DCF estimate, or a transaction price as evidence of value.
  - How: Identify the asset and claimant, forecast the claimant's cash flows, choose a consistent risk-adjusted discount rate, and compare the result with the observed price. For pricing, normalize a peer multiple and test what the peer set embeds.
- **The Bermuda Triangle of Valuation**: Beware the interaction of bias, complexity, and uncertainty. More model detail can conceal subjective judgments rather than remove them.
  - How: Declare the investment story and conflicts, concentrate effort on material drivers, and show a range when estimation is uncertain.

## Key Concepts
- **Intrinsic value**: Cash-flow-based estimate of what an asset is worth under stated assumptions.
- **Price**: Amount paid in a market transaction; it can depart from an analyst's value estimate.
- **Margin of safety**: Gap between estimated value and purchase price that cushions error in the valuation.
- **Bias**: Tendency to make model choices favor a desired conclusion.
- **Uncertainty**: Inability to know future operating outcomes or discount rates precisely.

## Mental Models
- Treat valuation as a discipline for exposing assumptions, not as a machine for producing a precise number.
- Ask whether disagreement with price comes from cash flows, risk, growth, or investor expectations.

## Anti-patterns
- **Bigger-fool purchase**: Buying solely because someone else may pay more does not establish underlying worth.
- **False precision**: A detailed spreadsheet cannot eliminate uncertain inputs or biased assumptions.
- **Mechanical market-efficiency claim**: Neither assume every price is right nor ignore the information aggregated in price.

## Worked Example
The chapter contrasts valuing a property with valuing a traded stock: the information and mechanics differ, but both require an expectation of future benefits and a discount for their risk. For a stock, the analyst first specifies which cash flows belong to equity holders; for a building, rent, upkeep, vacancy, and eventual sale value take that role. If a buyer instead points only to a recent sale or a peer's quoted multiple, the exercise has switched from intrinsic valuation to pricing. The comparison can be useful, but it cannot answer the same question without examining its underlying drivers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Name the claimant and the cash flows before choosing a model.
2. Separate an estimate of value from a market quote and explain the difference.
3. Make bias and uncertainty visible in the assumptions and range.
