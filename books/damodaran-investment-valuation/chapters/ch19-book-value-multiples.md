# Chapter 19: Book Value Multiples

## Core Idea
Book-value multiples link market price to invested capital. The economic question is whether the firm can earn returns above its required return on the capital recorded in the denominator.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Price-to-book (P/B) analysis**: Compare equity market value with book equity, then explain the ratio using return on equity, growth, and cost of equity.
  - When to use: Book equity is meaningful, especially for financial institutions or asset-heavy firms.
- **Enterprise value-to-invested-capital**: Compare operating value with operating invested capital and explain the ratio using ROIC relative to WACC.
- **Tobin's Q**: Compare market value of assets with replacement cost where that cost can be estimated credibly.

## Key Concepts
- **Book equity**: Accounting residual after liabilities, not a market valuation.
- **ROE**: Equity earnings divided by book equity; leverage and accounting policies affect it.
- **ROIC**: Operating return on capital available to all providers.
- **Replacement cost**: Cost to recreate comparable operating assets.
- **Goodwill**: Acquisition premium recognized in accounting that can distort book comparisons.

## Mental Models
- A high P/B can be justified by persistent ROE above cost of equity; a low P/B may signal poor returns rather than a bargain.
- Changes in accounting book values can move P/B without changing economic value.

## Reference Formula
For a stable equity business with meaningful book value, `P/B = ROE × payout ratio / (Ke − g)`. If `g = (1 − payout ratio) × ROE`, this becomes `P/B = (ROE − g) / (Ke − g)`. This is a *model-derived justified multiple*, not a universal market identity. It fails as a usable shortcut when book equity is distorted or negative, return and payout cannot remain stable, or `g ≥ Ke`.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating P/B below one as automatically cheap**: The business may destroy value on equity capital.
- **Comparing book values across incompatible accounting regimes**: R&D, acquisitions, write-downs, and buybacks alter denominators.
- **Ignoring financial leverage**: Debt can lift ROE while increasing equity risk.

## Worked Example
The chapter uses Jenapharm as a privatization candidate to derive a P/B from expected ROE rather than from a generic industry average. The analyst asks whether future returns exceed the cost of equity, how long the excess lasts, and how much earnings are retained to support growth. A firm with weak expected ROE can rationally trade below book even if its assets look substantial on the balance sheet. The chapter's two-stage model then lets excess profitability fade instead of assuming it lasts forever.

## Key Takeaways
1. Read P/B through future returns on equity, not book value alone.
2. Check whether accounting makes book equity comparable across peers.
3. Use EV/invested capital for operating capital when financing mixes differ.
