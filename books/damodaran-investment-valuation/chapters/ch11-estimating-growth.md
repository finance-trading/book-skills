# Chapter 11: Estimating Growth

## Core Idea
Growth has value only when it is supported by investment and returns on that investment. Historical growth and analyst forecasts may inform a projection but should not substitute for an operating model.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fundamental growth**: Link growth in operating income to the reinvestment rate and return earned on new capital, rather than extrapolating a historical percentage.
  - When to use: Building a DCF growth path.
  - How: Estimate reinvestment from capex, acquisitions, and working capital; estimate marginal return on new investments; fade both toward sustainable levels.
- **Top-down revenue model**: Begin with addressable market, market share, revenue, operating margin, reinvestment/sales-to-capital, and taxes, then derive free cash flow.
  - When to use: Young firms, negative-earnings firms, or a business model changing rapidly.
- **Historical-growth diagnostic**: Compare arithmetic and geometric growth, base-year dependence, outliers, negative earnings, and company scale before using past trends.

## Key Concepts
- **Reinvestment rate**: Share of after-tax operating earnings reinvested in the business.
- **Return on invested capital (ROIC)**: Profitability of capital already committed.
- **Marginal return on capital**: Return expected on newly invested capital, which may differ from historical ROIC.
- **Sales-to-capital ratio**: Revenue generated per incremental unit of invested capital.
- **Growth persistence**: Degree to which high growth survives competition and scale.

## Mental Models
- Decompose an attractive growth story into market size, share, margin, and capital needs; each claim can be checked separately.
- A very high growth rate becomes harder to sustain as the company becomes larger.

## Reference Formulas
- With a stable return on capital, `expected EBIT growth = reinvestment rate × return on invested capital`.
- `Reinvestment rate = (net capital expenditure + Δ non-cash working capital) / [EBIT × (1 − tax rate)]`, where net capital expenditure is capex less depreciation and includes investment-like R&D or acquisitions when those are needed for the modeled growth.
- If return on capital changes, growth is not just reinvestment times the old return: the chapter adds the change in return on existing capital. For negative or near-zero EBIT, the ratio can be undefined or unstable; forecast revenue, margin, and capital needs directly instead.

## Anti-patterns
- **Historical average as an unconditional forecast**: Growth from a tiny or negative base can mislead.
- **Analyst consensus as independent evidence**: Forecasts can share incentives and narratives.
- **Raising growth without raising reinvestment**: It creates value from an internally inconsistent assumption.

## Worked Example
The chapter uses Amazon's 2013–2023 figures to compare arithmetic and geometric growth and to show how the starting year changes an apparent growth rate. For a younger company such as Airbnb, it proceeds from market opportunity and share to revenue, estimates a path to sustainable operating margin, and asks how much capital supports incremental sales. The resulting cash flow may be negative during rapid expansion even when eventual operating income rises.

## Key Takeaways
1. Tie growth to incremental investment and returns.
2. Examine whether the current scale permits the projected rate.
3. Translate a narrative into revenue, margin, reinvestment, and tax assumptions.
