# Chapter 16: Estimating Equity Value per Share

## Core Idea
An operating DCF is not yet common equity value per share. Add or subtract nonoperating assets and claims, account for employee options and restricted shares, then divide by an appropriate diluted share count.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Operating-to-equity bridge**: Add cash and marketable securities to operating value, value cross-holdings separately when appropriate, and subtract debt, minority claims, and other obligations.
  - When to use: After firm DCF or enterprise-value pricing.
  - How: Identify which cash flows and assets are already included; avoid double counting cash or subsidiaries.
- **Stock-based compensation treatment**: Recognize option and restricted-share claims through future compensation costs or separate option value/dilution, consistently with share count.
  - Why it works: Employees' claims reduce what belongs to existing shareholders even if they are noncash in the current period.
- **Voting-right adjustment**: Different share classes can have different values when control rights matter and trading evidence supports a premium.

## Key Concepts
- **Nonoperating cash**: Cash not needed to run projected operations and not already included in forecast cash flows.
- **Cross-holding**: Ownership stake in another company requiring separate treatment if not consolidated in operating forecasts.
- **Minority interest**: Portion of a consolidated subsidiary owned by outside investors.
- **Employee option overhang**: Dilutive value transferred to option holders.
- **Diluted share count**: Share base accounting for equity claims that can become common shares.

## Mental Models
- Trace each asset or claim exactly once through either forecast cash flow or the terminal bridge.
- A high cash balance does not always add dollar-for-dollar value if it is invested poorly, trapped, or needed for operations.

## Anti-patterns
- **Add consolidated subsidiary value twice**: If the subsidiary's cash flows are in the DCF, the ownership stake is not a second free asset.
- **Ignoring employee options**: A per-share estimate based only on basic shares overstates existing holders' claim.
- **Assuming every dollar of reported cash is excess**: Restricted and operating cash may not be distributable.

## Worked Example
The chapter compares valuing a cash-rich company as one consolidated entity with valuing operations and cash separately. If cash earns below-market returns, its effect on current operating earnings and its separate asset value must be reconciled so the same cash is not counted twice. Cross-holding examples add the value of a separate stake only after checking whether the other company's earnings and assets were consolidated. Employee-option claims then reduce the amount attributable to current common shareholders before dividing by shares.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reconcile enterprise, nonoperating assets, debt, and other claims in a written bridge.
2. Use one consistent method for stock compensation and dilution.
3. Distinguish operationally required cash from distributable surplus cash.
