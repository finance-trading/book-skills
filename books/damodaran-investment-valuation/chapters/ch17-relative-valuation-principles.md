# Chapter 17: Fundamental Principles of Relative Valuation

## Core Idea
Relative valuation compares standardized prices, but a low multiple is meaningful only after consistent definitions, distribution checks, fundamental analysis, and peer selection.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four basic steps to using multiples**: (1) Define and measure the multiple consistently, (2) examine its distribution within the sector and the whole market, (3) identify the fundamentals that drive it, and (4) compare with the right firms while controlling for remaining differences.
  - When to use: Every time a company is called cheap or expensive on a multiple.
  - Why it works: It prevents a convenient peer average or inconsistent numerator from masquerading as valuation.
- **Equity versus enterprise multiple test**: Pair equity value with equity earnings/book value and enterprise value with pre-financing operating metrics.
- **Relative/intrinsic reconciliation**: Ask whether the pricing result or DCF embeds different growth, risk, payout, or cash-flow conversion assumptions.

## Key Concepts
- **Equity multiple**: Equity price scaled by an equity measure such as EPS or book equity.
- **Enterprise multiple**: Enterprise value scaled by an operating measure such as EBITDA or revenue.
- **Median multiple**: Middle observation, often more informative than a mean distorted by extreme positive outliers.
- **Peer group**: Firms with comparable fundamentals, not merely a shared industry label.
- **Selection bias**: Exclusion of negative or undefined denominator cases that changes the apparent sector average.

## Mental Models
- A multiple is shorthand for expected growth, risk, margins, and cash distributions.
- Ask why a peer trades at its multiple before applying that multiple to the subject company.

## Anti-patterns
- **Price/EBITDA**: Equity numerator and firm-level earnings denominator are inconsistent when leverage differs.
- **Treating a sector mean as fair value**: A whole sector may be richly priced, and the mean can be skewed.
- **Unadjusted negative-earner exclusion**: Omitting loss-making firms can make the surviving peer sample look cheaper or more profitable.

## Worked Example
The chapter shows that Nvidia's P/E in May 2024 changes with current, trailing, or forward earnings and with basic versus diluted shares. A bullish analyst could choose a forward denominator to make the ratio look lower; a bearish analyst could use stale earnings to make it look higher. The first step is to settle the precise definition and as-of date. A beverage-company comparison then controls for growth and risk rather than transferring a peer P/E mechanically.

## Key Takeaways
1. Normalize definitions and financial period before comparison.
2. Inspect the full distribution and denominator exclusions.
3. Explain multiple differences through fundamentals, not labels.
