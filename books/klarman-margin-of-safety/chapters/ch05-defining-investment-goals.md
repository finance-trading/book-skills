# Chapter 5: Defining Your Investment Goals

## Core Idea
A portfolio needs an explicit goal, horizon, and definition of acceptable loss before it needs a list of opportunities. Return targets that ignore constraints produce unsuitable risk.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Goals Before Securities**: Define return objective, time horizon, liquidity needs, and tolerance for interim decline.
  - When to use: Beginning or revising any portfolio.
  - How: Write the cash needs and maximum acceptable permanent loss first. Let those statements constrain security selection.
- **Absolute Return Over Relative Comfort**: Prefer achieving the owner's objective to matching a benchmark.
  - When to use: Choosing between holding cash and buying a fully valued market.
  - How: Hold cash when opportunities fail the value test. Do not dilute standards to remain fully invested.
- **Risk Capacity Versus Risk Appetite**: Distinguish money that can tolerate volatility from money required for near-term obligations.
  - When to use: Allocating capital across accounts or time horizons.
  - How: Match illiquid or volatile bargains only with patient capital. Do not let temperament exceed financial capacity.

## Key Concepts
- **Investment objective**: The specific result the capital must accomplish.
- **Horizon**: The period before cash is required.
- **Opportunity cost**: The return forgone by choosing one use of capital.
- **Cash as optionality**: Dry powder that can be deployed when prices improve.
- **Suitability**: Fit among goal, security, liquidity, and downside.

## Mental Models
Think of each portfolio rule as a boundary around future behavior. The rule must be strong enough to survive a bull market, when the pressure to abandon it is strongest.

## Anti-patterns
- **Setting a return target without a valuation process**: The target encourages progressively weaker purchases.
- **Borrowing the objective of a peer or index**: Someone else's benchmark may not match the owner's liabilities.
- **Treating all cash as idle failure**: Cash can be the rational result of disciplined standards.

## Worked Example
Klarman asks investors to decide what the capital must do before discussing where it is invested. For a practical version, separate capital needed within three years from long-term capital. The near-term portion cannot own an illiquid bargain merely because its discount is large. The long-term portion can, but only at a price that protects against being wrong.

## Why It Works / Failure Modes
Goals clarify which risks are acceptable, but a statement of goals does not itself provide an edge. It fails when objectives are so ambitious that no conservative portfolio can meet them, leading to hidden leverage or speculation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Policy comes before selection.
2. Absolute objectives can justify cash and inactivity.
3. Liquidity needs set a hard limit on otherwise attractive bargains.
4. A return goal cannot be allowed to lower the purchase standard.
