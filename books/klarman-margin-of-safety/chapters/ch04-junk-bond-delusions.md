# Chapter 4: Delusions of Value: Junk Bonds in the 1980s

## Core Idea
A historical yield series does not underwrite a new issue. Junk bonds of the 1980s changed in quality, structure, and purpose, while buyers treated them as the same asset class.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Yield Is Not Value**: A high promised coupon neither creates asset coverage nor compensates for an inadequate process.
  - When to use: Evaluating below-investment-grade debt or any security marketed through historical average returns.
  - How: Examine issuer cash flow, collateral, covenants, seniority, issuance purpose, and default-loss severity.
- **The Fallen Angel Versus New-Issue Distinction**: Bonds downgraded after issuance differ from bonds weak by design at issuance.
  - When to use: Comparing apparently similar high-yield securities.
  - How: Reconstruct the balance sheet and deal purpose at origination. Identify whether debt funded a conservative business or a speculative transaction.
- **Risk Migration**: When sophisticated buyers reject the risky layer, that layer is repackaged for less discriminating buyers.
  - When to use: Innovations, securitizations, and fashionable private-market structures.
  - How: Follow who used to hold the risk, why they stopped, and what new label made it saleable.

## Key Concepts
- **Fallen angel**: A formerly stronger bond that becomes distressed or downgraded.
- **Original-issue junk**: Debt issued with speculative characteristics.
- **Covenant protection**: Contractual limits that preserve cash and priority for lenders.
- **Loss severity**: What remains unrecovered after default, not merely default frequency.
- **Financial innovation**: A structure whose novelty may conceal transferred risk.

## Mental Models
Use the junk-bond boom as a template for every claim that "this time the asset class is safe because its average historical return was attractive." The composition of the average may have changed.

## Anti-patterns
- **Arguing from yield spreads alone**: Spreads incorporate price but omit covenant and collateral quality.
- **Accepting a rating as analysis**: Ratings can lag structure, incentives, and leverage.
- **Assuming diversification cures correlated underwriting errors**: Similar bad loans remain bad in a portfolio.
- **Letting innovation bypass first principles**: New packaging does not repeal cash flow or priority.

## Worked Example
Klarman examines 1980s junk bonds that financed leveraged transactions and were sold on the evidence of older fallen-angel returns. A faithful reconstruction starts with the use of proceeds, then subtracts cyclicality and leverage from management projections. If promised yield depends on perfection, historical default averages are irrelevant. The buyer must underwrite the individual issuer and its position in the capital structure.

## Why It Works / Failure Modes
The case works as a warning because buyers replaced security-specific analysis with a market narrative. It should not be reduced to "all high-yield bonds are bad." A distressed bond bought below conservative asset value may later be attractive; the flaw was paying a full new-issue price for deteriorating protection.

## Key Takeaways
1. Historical class averages cannot price a structurally different security.
2. Covenant, seniority, collateral, and purpose determine credit risk.
3. Novelty and strong demand are reasons for more analysis, not less.
4. Recovery and default probability both matter.
