# Chapter 3: The Institutional Performance Derby: The Client Is the Loser

## Core Idea
Short-term ranking against peers changes the investment being attempted. Institutions often accept poor absolute decisions in order to avoid looking wrong alone.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Performance Derby**: Clients hire and fire managers by recent relative results, so managers optimize career and business risk.
  - When to use: Assessing any strategy constrained by quarterly benchmarks.
  - How: Extend the evaluation window, define absolute-return objectives, and permit tracking error when valuation demands it.
- **Agency-Risk Stack**: Consultant, sponsor, committee, and manager can each behave rationally while the beneficiary loses.
  - When to use: Reviewing mandates, guidelines, and performance reports.
  - How: Trace each constraint to the risk it controls. Remove constraints that merely prevent embarrassment.
- **Indexing as a Default, Not a Discipline**: Owning the market avoids some active-management costs but also abandons valuation.
  - When to use: Choosing between active and passive exposure.
  - How: Index only where costs and selection disadvantages dominate. Do not treat capitalization weighting as a valuation process.

## Key Concepts
- **Business risk**: A manager's risk of losing clients, distinct from the portfolio's risk.
- **Relative performance**: Return compared with a benchmark or peer group.
- **Tracking error**: Deviation from benchmark returns, often penalized even when beneficial.
- **Closet indexing**: Charging active fees for benchmark-like exposure.
- **Beneficiary**: The person whose capital ultimately bears gains and losses.

## Mental Models
Think of an institutional mandate as two portfolios: the securities and the manager's career exposure. When those objectives conflict, price discipline usually loses.

## Anti-patterns
- **Quarterly scoreboard management**: Selling bargains or buying market leaders to reduce visible deviation.
- **Diversifying across managers without diversifying their incentives**: Several benchmark-huggers do not create independent thinking.
- **Using index membership as a quality signal**: Price-weighted popularity is not business value.

## Worked Example
A pension committee compares two managers after a rally. One lags because it holds cash and unpopular securities; the other matches the index. Klarman's analysis says the first may have served the beneficiary better despite the embarrassing report. The useful evaluation asks whether each holding had a margin of safety, not whether the quarter tracked the benchmark.

## Why It Works / Failure Modes
The model explains persistent buying of overvalued assets and neglect of small opportunities. It fails when relative evaluation is used only as diagnosis and not as a control system. A benchmark can reveal drift; it should not dictate purchases.

## Key Takeaways
1. Relative performance can be rational for managers and irrational for owners.
2. Judge decisions by process, price, and downside, not by short-term ranking.
3. Benchmark constraints can force purchase of what is popular.
4. Passive investing avoids some agency costs but imports market valuation errors.
