## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. The 15-point growth-stock test
Use this before valuation. A company need not score perfectly, but several weak answers are disqualifying:

1. Is there a large market for the product/service for at least several years?
2. Is management committed to developing new products/processes that extend growth?
3. Is R&D productive relative to company size and competitors?
4. Is the sales organization superior, including distribution and customer service?
5. Are profit margins high?
6. What is management doing to maintain or improve margins?
7. Are labor and personnel relations strong?
8. Do senior managers work well together?
9. Is management depth sufficient for the next stage of growth?
10. Are cost analysis and accounting records accurate enough to manage the business?
11. Does the company have an industry-specific advantage or distinctive competence?
12. Are near-term and long-term earnings prospects strong?
13. Can growth be financed without excessive dilution of existing holders?
14. Does management report bad news as candidly as good news?
15. Is management’s integrity beyond question?

### 2. Scuttlebutt: evidence before the executive meeting
Use external inquiry to learn what management cannot or will not tell you. Interview customers, competitors, suppliers, former employees, technical experts, distributors, and industry observers. Ask open questions, follow the answer with a better question, and triangulate repeated signals. Prepare first: Fisher’s rule is to know at least half of what matters before meeting management. The meeting validates hypotheses; it does not replace fieldwork.

### 3. Growth quality beats growth appearance
Prefer durable, internally generated growth over one-time gains, temporary industry booms, accounting effects, or financial leverage. Distinguish “fortunate because the industry grew” from “capable and therefore able to exploit growth.” Examine the future earnings curve, not just the last five years.

### 4. Margin and reinvestment flywheel
Sales growth matters only when it becomes expanding earnings. Track multi-year margins, relative margins, and management’s actions to improve them. A modest margin advantage can compound; reinvestment in R&D, sales capability, capacity, and people is valuable when returns are high.

### 5. Price and time
The right company at a reasonable price is more important than macroeconomic forecasts. Buy when the thesis is sound and temporary operating difficulty creates a credible entry point. Do not sell a superior company merely because its P/E is above the market, its price has risen, or a normal bear market is feared.

### 6. Sell only on thesis failure
Sell when the original facts have materially deteriorated: management quality declines, growth opportunity is exhausted, the 15-point profile breaks, integrity is doubtful, or a clearly superior opportunity is available and the comparison is robust. Price movement alone is not a sell signal.

### 7. Dividends are capital-allocation evidence
Judge dividends by what the retained dollar can earn. A high-growth company may create more value by reinvesting than by distributing cash. Prefer a consistent policy and inspect whether management uses retained earnings productively; do not confuse current yield with long-term return.

### 8. Concentration with safeguards
Diversify enough to survive mistakes, but do not dilute judgment by owning many mediocre companies. Never invest money that cannot tolerate loss. Position size should reflect uncertainty, business quality, and whether a small company is still in a high-risk phase.
