## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Identify a psychological crowd by interaction, not headcount
A collection of people is not automatically a psychological crowd. Look for a common stimulus, synchronized attention or emotion, social confirmation, and a measurable loss of room for independent judgment. The same person can respond differently alone, in an institution, or in a highly charged gathering. Group context changes behavior; it does not reveal an immutable personal character. **Use** this model to analyze meetings, movements, online communities, and public reactions without treating every group as irrational. See [Ch 2](chapters/ch02-psychological-crowd.md).

### 2. Trace the stimulus–suggestion–contagion chain
Le Bon describes how a stimulus invites an interpretation, that interpretation is repeated or confirmed socially, and reactions spread. This model is useful for tracing rumors, panic, enthusiasm, and collective misperception. Separate the first observation from later retellings; multiple accounts that share one source are not independent evidence. Emotional intensity and repetition explain spread, not truth. See [Ch 3](chapters/ch03-crowd-mind.md) and [Ch 6](chapters/ch06-leaders-persuasion.md).

### 3. Separate long-term conditions from immediate triggers
Le Bon distinguishes **indirect factors**, which prepare the ground for some beliefs, from **direct factors**, which give them a concrete form and can precipitate action. For an apparently sudden movement, identify background institutions, experiences, interests, and norms, then identify the immediate event, message, or organizer. Do not mistake the spark for the accumulated conditions, or accept Le Bon's racial determinism as an explanation. See [Ch 4](chapters/ch04-preconditions-of-belief.md) and [Ch 5](chapters/ch05-direct-factors.md).

### 4. Audit the message, not just its popularity
Images, slogans, and repeated assertions can make an idea memorable and familiar. Ask what picture a phrase evokes, what different audiences mean by it, what details it leaves out, and whether repetitions trace back to independent sources. Prestige explains why an audience may listen; it does not establish the claim. Use this framework to evaluate communication, not to exploit an audience. See [Ch 5](chapters/ch05-direct-factors.md), [Ch 6](chapters/ch06-leaders-persuasion.md), and [Ch 11](chapters/ch11-electors.md).

### 5. Distinguish durable belief from short-lived opinion
A fast change in public expression does not by itself prove a deep shift in values or institutions. Compare statements with behavior, organizational choices, formal rules, and longer time series. Beliefs, practices, and institutions can change at different speeds. Le Bon's own account overstates unified “national” psychology; test claims across groups and contexts. See [Ch 7](chapters/ch07-belief-change.md).

### 6. Group decisions depend on procedure and power
Le Bon applies his theory to criminal crowds, juries, voters, and parliaments. Use these cases to ask who sets the agenda, how evidence is presented, whether dissent is possible, how authority affects judgment, and who is accountable. A crowd explanation can illuminate pressure without excusing harm; a formal institution is not automatically rational, nor a crowd automatically irrational. Prefer procedural safeguards, independent verification, transparent commitments, and retained dissent. See [Ch 8](chapters/ch08-crowd-types.md) through [Ch 12](chapters/ch12-parliaments.md).

### 7. Keep the historical critique attached
This is a nineteenth-century work with influential observations about suggestion, imitation, rhetoric, and collective behavior mixed with sweeping claims about race, national character, women, class, and intellect. Treat those claims as historical context, not modern evidence. Replace essentialist explanations with observable incentives, institutions, media conditions, history, and individual variation. Distinguish descriptive mechanisms from moral or legal judgments.

## Scope & Limits

This skill synthesizes the specified Chinese PDF, not a definitive account of contemporary crowd psychology. Use current empirical research for real-world claims, legal and political sources for institutional analysis, and independent evidence for factual judgments. The book's nineteenth-century assumptions and source examples should be contextualized rather than treated as established science.
