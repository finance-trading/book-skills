# Decision Cheatsheet

Use this as a diagnostic lens, not a universal law or a persuasion manual. Le Bon's 1895-era claims, especially racial and gender generalizations, are historically situated and not reliable modern science.

## Decision rules

| If you observe… | Then… | Because… |
|---|---|---|
| Many people repeat the same claim | Trace the earliest source and independent evidence | Repetition can create familiarity, not proof |
| Witnesses agree after discussing an ambiguous event | Separate accounts and check physical evidence | Shared interpretation can contaminate memory |
| A crowd appears to form suddenly | Separate long-term conditions from the immediate trigger | A trigger alone rarely explains readiness to act |
| A slogan drives a debate | Ask what different groups mean by the key word | Shared terms can hide different images and goals |
| A group makes a high-stakes decision | Set evidence rules, independent review, dissent and records | Procedure can limit anchoring and authority effects |
| A claim rests on a prominent speaker | Check the claim independently and within their expertise | Prestige explains influence, not truth |
| Public opinion shifts rapidly | Compare statements with behavior, institutions and longer trends | Short-lived opinion differs from durable belief |
| Collective harm occurs | Examine organization, orders, choices, exit options and accountability | Context can explain behavior without excusing it |
| A political promise sounds compelling | Specify cost, legal authority, owner, deadline and measure | Verifiability enables accountability |

## Causal map

1. **Context**: what institutions, experiences, traditions or shared interests predisposed the group?
2. **Trigger**: what event, message or image made the issue immediate?
3. **Transmission**: who repeated or reframed the claim, and were sources independent?
4. **Interaction**: how did emotion, identity, leadership and dissent shape the response?
5. **Action**: what rules, resources or constraints enabled the outcome?
6. **Correction**: what evidence, procedure or accountability could change course?

## Fast tells and smells

- Agreement built from a single source is not corroboration.
- Emotional certainty is not factual certainty.
- A formal group is not automatically rational; a crowd is not automatically irrational.
- A leader can coordinate, but unchecked authority suppresses correction.
- Group dynamics describe pressures, not immutable individual traits.
- If analysis relies on racial, gender or national character, replace that premise with observable incentives, institutions and history.
- Separate an explanation of behavior from moral or legal judgment.
