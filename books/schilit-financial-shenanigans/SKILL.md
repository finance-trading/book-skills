## Core Frameworks and Mental Models

### Classify the distortion first

The authors organize the book into four **Financial Shenanigans** families. A suspicious result can involve more than one family.

1. **Earnings Manipulation Shenanigans** — examine whether revenue or expense is invented, prematurely recorded, deferred, omitted, or presented as recurring when it is not. The seven numbered techniques are: recording revenue too soon; recording bogus revenue; boosting income with one-time or unsustainable activities; shifting current expenses later; hiding expenses or losses by other means; shifting current income later; shifting future expenses into the current period. Use [ch03](chapters/ch03-recording-revenue-too-soon.md)–[ch09](chapters/ch09-shifting-expenses-earlier.md).
2. **Cash Flow Shenanigans** — distinguish customer-generated operating cash from financing, reclassified payments, or timing gains. The three numbered techniques are: financing inflows in CFFO; operating outflows moved elsewhere; and unsustainable CFFO boosts. Use [ch10](chapters/ch10-financing-inflows-as-operating.md)–[ch12](chapters/ch12-unsustainable-operating-cash.md).
3. **Key Metric Shenanigans** — test management's substitute for revenue, earnings, cash, or balance-sheet health. The two numbered techniques are misleading performance metrics and distorted balance-sheet metrics. Use [ch13](chapters/ch13-misleading-performance-metrics.md)–[ch14](chapters/ch14-distorting-balance-sheet-metrics.md).
4. **Acquisition Accounting Shenanigans** — compare target and buyer before and after closing. The three numbered techniques inflate revenue/earnings, reported cash flow, or key metrics. Use [ch15](chapters/ch15-acquisition-earnings.md)–[ch17](chapters/ch17-acquisition-key-metrics.md).

### Apply cross-checks before interpreting intent

- **Three-statement triangulation**: trace a transaction through profit, assets/liabilities, and cash. Revenue booked early may appear as receivables; capitalized operating costs may raise earnings and CFFO while weakening free cash flow. Start with [ch02](chapters/ch02-just-touch-up-the-x-rays.md).
- **Timing versus substance**: a genuine sale booked too early is different from a sale lacking an independent buyer or payment duty. Use the contract-to-cash trace in ch03–ch04.
- **Repeatability versus real cash**: a receivable sale or supplier-payment delay can produce genuine current cash while failing as a durable source of CFFO growth. Rebuild CFFO by source in ch10–ch12.
- **Metric reconstruction**: when a highlighted ratio improves, examine its numerator, denominator, definition, exclusions, and transaction effects. Recompute DSO with factored receivables restored; bridge adjusted income to GAAP. Use ch13–ch14.
- **Deal-cohort normalization**: compare the same collection of businesses in both periods. Obtain target stub-period history and pro forma sales; present cash after acquisition spending for a serial acquirer. Use ch15–ch17.

### Carry the forensic mindset

The closing chapter gives ten habits: skepticism; asking **“why?” and “why now?”**; searching beneath accounting symptoms for business problems; inspecting culture; testing management's profitability framework; following compensation incentives; reading where disclosures are placed; counting small clues; recognizing patterns; and remaining humble and curious. Use [ch19](chapters/ch19-the-forensic-mindset.md) to turn these into a dated red-flag log. A red flag is a hypothesis to test, not proof of fraud or a prediction of the exposure date.
