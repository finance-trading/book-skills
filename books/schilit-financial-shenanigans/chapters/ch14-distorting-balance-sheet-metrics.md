# Chapter 14: Key Metric Shenanigan No. 2 — Distorting Balance Sheet Metrics to Avoid Showing Deterioration

## Core Idea
Management can improve a ratio without improving the balance sheet. Reconstruct receivables, inventory, financial-asset values, and debt on a comparable economic basis.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four balance-sheet metric checks**: challenge receivable measures, inventory turnover or reserves, financial-asset presentation, and debt/liquidity measures.
- **Denominator reconstruction**: when DSO or leverage improves, add back assets sold or obligations hidden through transactions and recalculate the ratio.

## Key Concepts
- **Days sales outstanding (DSO)**: receivables relative to sales, sensitive to receivable sales and reclassifications.
- **Notes receivable**: receivables reclassified into a form that may disappear from standard AR ratios.
- **Inventory metric**: turnover or days of inventory, sensitive to purchases and write-downs.
- **Financial-asset impairment**: decline in value that may be obscured by presentation changes.
- **Debt metric**: reported borrowings or leverage; economic obligations can lie elsewhere.

## Mental Models
- Ask **did the business improve, or did the denominator move?**
- Compare the balance sheet **before and after non-operating transactions** such as factoring or asset transfers.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading lower DSO as faster customer payment** without adjusting for factored receivables.
- **Using one disclosed debt line as total leverage** without checking financing structures and obligations.

## Worked Example
Sanmina-SCI sold receivables and subsequently highlighted lower DSO and stronger CFFO. The book points out that both effects can follow mechanically from the sale, even if customers paid no faster. An analyst would add sold receivables back to the working-capital base for a comparable DSO and isolate the factoring cash in CFFO.

## Detection Matrix
| Metric improves | Potential mechanical cause | Recast |
|---|---|---|
| DSO falls | Receivables sold or converted to notes | Restore comparable receivable balance |
| Inventory turnover rises | Inventory moved, written down, or underpurchased | Reconcile units and carrying values |
| Financial-asset quality appears stable | Presentation or category changes | Trace assets across categories and fair-value notes |
| Debt leverage falls | Obligation moved to another vehicle or line | Map financing commitments and recourse |

## Key Takeaways
1. Rebuild ratios after unusual asset sales and reclassifications.
2. Inspect notes for debt and financial assets omitted from headline tables.
3. Seek operational corroboration before calling a balance-sheet ratio better.
