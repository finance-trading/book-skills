# Chapter 19: The Forensic Mindset

## Core Idea
Forensic investing is a repeatable habit of skeptical, curious, evidence-based comparison. It looks for the business problem underneath the accounting symptom.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The book's ten mindset rules**: skepticism; ask “why?” and “why now?” about change; look for business problems beneath accounting problems; inspect culture; reject untested profitability frameworks; study compensation; search the location of disclosures; count small clues; recognize patterns; stay humble and curious.
- **Change log**: record each accounting policy, metric definition, transaction, executive incentive, and disclosure-location change; assign an economic explanation or an unresolved question.
- **Pattern-of-behavior test**: aggregate individually modest signals across periods and documents rather than demanding one decisive anomaly.

## Key Concepts
- **Forensic mindset**: objective investigation of how business reality differs from reported presentation.
- **Why now?**: timing question that ties a change to incentives and business pressure.
- **Disclosure location**: where information is placed, which can affect whether investors notice it.
- **Incentive alignment**: compensation terms that shape reporting behavior.
- **Pattern of behavior**: repeated small choices that reveal a reporting tendency.

## Mental Models
- Think of skepticism as a **competitive advantage** in markets prone to amplified good news.
- Treat every new disclosure or unusual change as **one shot in a cumulative scorecard**.

## Anti-patterns
- **Blind adoption of management's profitability framework**: it may exclude the cost of sustaining the business.
- **Overconfidence in a single clue**: many accounting choices have innocent explanations and require corroboration.

## Worked Example
When a company changes its adjusted-earnings definition while CFFO diverges from net income, the forensic process is: date the definition change, rebuild prior adjusted results under the new rule, reconcile CFFO line items, read compensation targets, and search footnotes for transactions that explain the gap. The result should state supported facts, plausible interpretations, and what remains unresolved. This is a synthesis of the chapter's rules, not a claim that every divergence is fraud.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask why a reporting or metric change occurred now.
2. Record and test patterns across periods, not just anomalies.
3. Preserve uncertainty and keep investigating the underlying business.
