# Chapter 1: 25 Years of Shenanigans

## Core Idea
Fraud often has an observable economic and cash-flow footprint before a restatement. An investor should test the whole reporting system, not grant the audit opinion or headline earnings the last word.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Six historical warnings**: use the cases of Waste Management, CUC/Cendant, Enron, WorldCom, Lehman Brothers, and Valeant to ask what a clean-looking number could conceal. Compare reported earnings with cash, deal activity, balance-sheet movements, and management's preferred metrics.
- **Free cash flow cross-check**: when earnings climb, subtract capital expenditure from cash flow from operations (CFFO) and ask whether cash generation corroborates the story. Reconcile any widening gap to disclosed investments and working capital.

## Key Concepts
- **Financial shenanigan**: a reporting choice that misleads investors about economic performance or financial health.
- **CFFO**: cash flow from operations; its classification can itself be manipulated.
- **Free cash flow**: CFFO less capital expenditure in the chapter's working definition.
- **Reserve release**: reversal of a prior accrual that can raise later earnings.
- **Acquisition dependence**: serial deals that replenish accounting opportunities and obscure organic trends.

## Mental Models
- Use a **three-statement triangulation** when one statement appears unusually strong: a distorted income statement often leaves clues in cash flow or the balance sheet.
- Treat an acquisition series as a possible **accounting reset**, then examine the target's pre-close results, purchase accounting, and subsequent organic growth.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating an audit as a guarantee**: Waste Management illustrates that auditors may fail to stop misleading reporting.
- **Accepting numbers too good to be true**: Enron's extraordinary appearance deserved an independent economic test.

## Worked Example
WorldCom grew through more than 70 acquisitions. The book traces how it wrote off costs, created reserves, and later released those reserves into income. Its planned Sprint acquisition did not close, limiting the supply of new reserves. Meanwhile, line costs shifted from expense to capital assets. A practitioner would rebuild free cash flow, inspect capital spending and reserve changes, and compare those against the earnings trend before accepting management's growth story.

## Key Takeaways
1. Read the income statement, balance sheet, cash-flow statement, and footnotes together.
2. Investigate the mechanism behind unusually smooth earnings or persistent deal-driven growth.
3. Treat a restatement as the end of a long signal chain, not the first possible warning.
