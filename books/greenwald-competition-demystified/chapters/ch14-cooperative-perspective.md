# Chapter 14: Cooperation without Incarceration — Bigger Pies, Fairly Divided

## Core Idea
The cooperative perspective asks two questions: how large a joint surplus is possible, and how it could be divided so every participant prefers the arrangement to its outside option. It is an analytic lens; any real-world coordination must stay within competition law.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Outcomes First**: Start with possible industry outcomes rather than individual moves.
  - When to use: Assessing alliances, standards, joint ventures, or bargaining within legal bounds.
  - How: Estimate the maximum joint gain, identify each claimant's independent payoff, then test whether a feasible allocation makes all parties willing to participate.
- **Nash Fairness Conditions**: Apply **individual rationality**, **symmetry**, and **linear invariance** to judge whether a division can persist.
  - When to use: Negotiating a multi-party surplus.
  - How: Ensure each party exceeds its threat point; divide equal claimants equally; scale shares with legitimate relative economic position where claimants differ.

## Key Concepts
- **Joint surplus**: Total benefit achievable by coordinated, lawful action beyond noncooperation.
- **Threat point / BATNA**: The payoff a party can obtain without the agreement.
- **Individual rationality**: No participant receives less than its noncooperative alternative.
- **Symmetry**: Essentially equal parties obtain equal portions of a common surplus.
- **Linear invariance**: A proportionality condition for differing economic positions.
- **Cooperative equilibrium**: An allocation no party wants to abandon given the available alternatives.

## Mental Models
- Use a **two-stage lens**: enlarge the pie, then allocate it.
- A proposed split that fails a party's BATNA is **unstable regardless of aggregate gains**.
- Treat fairness as a sustainability condition, not as a moral slogan.

## Anti-patterns
- **Maximizing joint gains while ignoring division**: A shortchanged participant can leave.
- **Giving identical parties unequal shares without an economic basis**: Grievance undermines cooperation.
- **Turning analytical cooperation into unlawful price or market coordination**: Legal constraints determine which actions are available.

## Worked Example
Suppose two otherwise comparable firms can produce a lawful shared gain of 12 units through a common technical standard. If each can independently gain 4 units, a proposed 10/2 split fails individual rationality for the second firm. A 6/6 split clears both threat points and satisfies symmetry. If one party contributes twice the scarce resource, a proportionality analysis may support a different division, provided both still exceed their outside options. This is an illustrative reconstruction of the chapter's fairness logic, not a numeric example from the text.

## Key Takeaways
1. Quantify what each party can achieve alone before negotiating a joint arrangement.
2. Separate creating surplus from dividing it.
3. Test agreements for stability through the three fairness conditions.
4. Keep any real collaboration within legal and contractual boundaries.
