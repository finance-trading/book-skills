# Chapter 2: Competitive Advantages I — Supply and Demand

## Core Idea
Differentiation by itself does not protect profit. A durable advantage requires an entrant to face a cost or customer-access disadvantage it cannot easily duplicate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Supply Advantages: Competitive Costs**: An incumbent can produce at a lower cost because of privileged inputs, proprietary process, or other hard-to-copy supply conditions.
  - When to use: When an incumbent claims technology or procurement superiority.
  - How: Identify the cost item, quantify the gap, test whether rivals can buy, license, imitate, or reverse-engineer it, and assess its likely duration.
- **Demand Advantages: Customer Captivity**: Habit, switching costs, and search costs can keep customers attached even when rivals offer similar products.
  - When to use: When share and margin persist despite available alternatives.
  - How: Observe repeat purchase, customer effort to change providers, compatibility or retraining costs, and difficulty of judging substitutes. Distinguish genuine captivity from mere brand awareness.

## Key Concepts
- **Differentiation myth**: A distinctive product or famous brand is not automatically a barrier to entry.
- **Customer captivity**: Continued patronage that makes a new rival spend more to win an equivalent customer.
- **Habit**: Repeated low-stakes purchase that favors a familiar provider.
- **Switching cost**: Time, money, risk, or relearning required to change providers.
- **Search cost**: Effort and uncertainty involved in identifying a satisfactory alternative.
- **Supply advantage**: An incumbent-specific cost or capability that a rival cannot match on comparable terms.

## Mental Models
- Use **entry, exit, and long-run profitability** together: high current margins are weak evidence unless rivals have failed to enter or scale.
- Think of brand strength as an empirical customer-behavior question, not a marketing label.
- Distinguish **better product** from **harder-to-replace provider**.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming premium positioning equals a moat**: Entry can fragment volume while premium prices remain, leaving fixed costs spread over fewer units.
- **Treating proprietary technology as permanent**: Patents expire and manufacturing methods diffuse.
- **Mistaking a low-cost operator for a structural supply advantage**: Superior execution can be copied or lost; test the source.

## Worked Example
Cadillac and Mercedes-Benz had differentiated luxury brands and commanded premium prices. New European and Japanese luxury models entered; incumbents did not necessarily have to cut sticker prices, but their sales volume and share fell while the fixed costs of sustaining differentiation remained. The case separates the ability to price above a generic car from the ability to keep entrants from competing away economic returns.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask what an entrant cannot reproduce, not whether customers recognize the brand.
2. Test supply claims against imitation and access to the same inputs.
3. Test demand claims through habit, switching, and search behavior.
4. Use sustained returns and failed entry as corroboration of a proposed mechanism.
