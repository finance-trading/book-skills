# Chapter 1: Strategy, Markets, and Competition

## Core Idea
Strategy concerns decisions whose outcomes depend on the actions and reactions of other economic actors. Start by asking whether barriers to entry protect any incumbent in a precisely defined market; only then model rivalry.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **One Single Force**: Prioritize barriers to entry among Porter's five forces. When entrants can replicate an incumbent's position, excess returns invite entry and disappear.
  - When to use: At the start of an industry or company strategy review.
  - How: Define the market, test whether incumbents have defensible advantages, then choose the branch below.
- **The Process of Strategic Analysis**: First establish whether competitive advantage exists, next identify its source and owner, then analyze interaction among the few protected competitors. If none exists, concentrate on operational effectiveness.
  - When to use: To allocate scarce senior-management attention.
  - How: Draw a narrow market map; examine persistent share and returns; distinguish one dominant incumbent from a few roughly matched firms; use game theory, simulation, or cooperative analysis only in the latter case.

## Key Concepts
- **Strategic decision**: A choice whose payoff changes with an external actor's response, regardless of its size.
- **Tactical decision**: A choice whose outcome largely depends on internal execution.
- **Barrier to entry**: An economic condition that prevents entrants or expanding rivals from matching an incumbent's position.
- **Economic profit**: Returns above the cost of capital, which open entry tends to erode.
- **Local champion**: A firm dominant in a bounded geographic or product market, even if small nationally.

## Mental Models
- Use the **level playing field / protected market** divide before debating competitive moves.
- Think **ant versus elephant** when one firm owns a barrier and weaker firms cannot reproduce it; the ant should not assume a fair fight.
- Think **few-player game** only when several firms each have meaningful barriers and can affect one another.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling every large commitment strategy**: Size or duration does not establish that competitor responses matter.
- **Starting with an undifferentiated five-force checklist**: It obscures the entry question that determines the rest of the analysis.
- **Treating an attractive market as an attractive entry**: The incumbent's barrier may make the opportunity inaccessible.

## Worked Example
IBM's choice to source the PC operating system from Microsoft and the microprocessor from Intel appeared to be a build-or-buy implementation choice. It determined which markets IBM would own and which suppliers could build durable franchises as the PC standard spread. The strategic lesson is to test whether a seemingly tactical choice changes who controls a barrier and who can capture future profit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define strategy by dependence on external reactions, not by budget or time horizon.
2. Test entry barriers before forecasting industry profits.
3. In an unprotected market, improve operations; in a protected market, defend the source of advantage.
4. Reserve game analysis for markets with a few genuine competitors.
