# Chapter 6: Niche Advantages and the Dilemma of Growth — Compaq and Apple

## Core Idea
An attractive niche can be profitable while the larger industry remains open to entry, but rapid growth and standardization can make the niche's advantages temporary.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Niche Advantage Test**: Compare the firm's advantage within a customer or product submarket with the entry opportunities created as that submarket expands.
  - When to use: Evaluating a differentiated high-growth business.
  - How: Define the niche; inspect customers' willingness to pay, component supply, costs, and entry; monitor whether specialized suppliers remove the incumbent's integration advantage.
- **Adapt When the Cost Structure Changes**: If external component suppliers achieve scale, buy rather than insist on producing every part internally.
  - When to use: After industry growth or modularization changes build-versus-buy economics.
  - How: Recompare internal cost and quality with specialist vendors; change operations before the old premium disappears.

## Key Concepts
- **Box maker**: A PC assembler in a segment with many competitors and shifting shares.
- **Temporary niche**: A customer segment in which one firm has a short-lived quality or distribution edge.
- **Component specialization**: Suppliers' ability to spread engineering costs over many assemblers.
- **Open architecture**: A standard that eases imitation and weakens assembly-level barriers.

## Mental Models
- Think **expanding market, falling fixed-cost hurdle**: entrants and specialist suppliers can reach viable scale as demand grows.
- Use **segment-specific share**: profitability of a niche does not establish dominance of all PCs.
- Treat superior management as a possible explanation for returns where structural barriers are weak.

## Anti-patterns
- **Projecting niche returns into a vast new market**: Growth attracts entrants and makes customers less captive.
- **Clinging to internal manufacturing as a badge of quality**: Specialists may overtake it on price and performance.
- **Conflating Apple's integration with a transferable moat**: Its distinctive system had advantages but also lacked the volume of the common PC standard.

## Worked Example
Compaq initially sold reliable, premium IBM-compatible PCs to corporate buyers and earned strong margins despite owning under 9% of the broader PC market. As PC demand expanded, specialist component makers gained enough volume to provide comparable quality cheaply. Compaq's internally engineered components lost their cost justification; new leadership shifted toward outside sourcing and a lower-cost structure. The case shows when a profitable operating model must be revised even though its original execution was excellent.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate niche profitability from industry-wide barriers.
2. Reassess scale advantages as total market volume changes.
3. Watch specialist suppliers as a signal that internal integration is losing value.
4. Change the operating model when the market's cost structure changes.
