# Chapter 13: No Instant Gratification — Kodak Takes On Polaroid

## Core Idea
A large company can be a disadvantaged entrant in a narrow market. Kodak's scale in conventional photography did not erase Polaroid's customer captivity, installed base, scale, and patents in instant photography.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Elephant and Tiger**: Compare the incumbent's strength in the *target market* with the entrant's transferable advantages, rather than comparing company sizes.
  - When to use: A diversified leader enters an adjacent but protected product market.
  - How: Define the target narrowly; test customer switching costs, installed base, economies of scale, patents, and distribution; price the incumbent's response and the entrant's learning curve.
- **Entrant Response Test**: Model what a protected incumbent can do after launch.
  - When to use: Before committing to production and promotion.
  - How: Ask whether it can match quality or price, improve distribution, invoke IP rights, or make the entrant's profit pool too small.

## Key Concepts
- **Market-space locality**: Advantage is bounded by the customers, product, and geography it actually protects.
- **Customer captivity**: Buyers' habit, experience, and installed equipment favor an incumbent.
- **Patents**: Legal exclusion can reinforce the commercial barrier, though scope and enforceability require separate evaluation.
- **Installed base**: Existing cameras create follow-on demand for film.
- **Pretax return on invested capital**: Polaroid's high historical returns were evidence of a protected position, not proof its management always allocated capital well.

## Mental Models
- Think **market boundary before company size**: Kodak was powerful in film, but Polaroid owned instant photography.
- Treat **adjacency as a hypothesis**. Shared technology or distribution is useful only if it defeats the target's barrier.
- Use **post-entry outcomes** to test whether a claimed synergy actually produced an advantage.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming brand and resources guarantee entry**: They do not neutralize an incumbent's target-market advantages.
- **Counting technical capability as a moat**: A product can be competent and still fail to gain profitable share.
- **Ignoring legal barriers**: Patents can change entry payoffs decisively.

## Worked Example
Polaroid had supplied all instant cameras and film before Kodak's 1976 launch and had earned exceptional returns for years. Kodak introduced the EK 4 and EK 6 with major advertising and retailer training, but its products were bulky relative to Polaroid's SX-70 and did not force Polaroid to surrender its position. The patent dispute later added a large liability. Kodak also entered copiers against Xerox while presuming its technology and sales force transferred. Both cases show why a corporate asset is not automatically an entry barrier in a new market.

## Key Takeaways
1. Bound the market narrowly before measuring who has an advantage.
2. Test each purported transferable strength against the incumbent's actual barriers.
3. Model both competitive and legal responses before investing.
4. Do not equate a differentiated product with profitable entry.
