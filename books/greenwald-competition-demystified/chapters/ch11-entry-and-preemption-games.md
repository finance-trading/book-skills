# Chapter 11: Games Companies Play — Entry and Preemption

## Core Idea
Capacity and entry choices differ from reversible price moves because they take time and commit capital. Analyze the sequence of entrant and incumbent choices with a decision tree and work backward from payoffs.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Entry/Preemption Game — Extensive Form**: Map the entrant's enter/stay-out choice, the incumbent's accept/resist response, and any later persist/draw-back choice.
  - When to use: Stores, plants, routes, product lines, or other durable capacity moves.
  - How: Assign incremental payoffs to terminal branches; start at the last decision and reason backward; include each actor's nonfinancial objectives and credibility.
- **War-Game Simulation**: Give participants competitor profiles and repeatedly play plausible action sequences.
  - When to use: When real choices and motives are too complex for a small payoff matrix.
  - How: Specify actors, available actions, motivations, information, and payoffs; compare simulated paths with historical behavior.

## Key Concepts
- **Extensive form**: A tree showing sequential moves and outcome payoffs.
- **Backward induction**: Choosing earlier moves in light of rational later responses.
- **Preemption**: Committing capacity first to influence rivals' entry decisions.
- **Accommodation**: An incumbent accepts entry when fighting would be worse.
- **Credible commitment**: A threat or promise whose execution remains rational or unavoidable when the decision point arrives.
- **Constant-sum game**: A game in which relative winners matter more than total rewards, reducing scope for cooperation.

## Mental Models
- Use **tree for durable sequential investments; matrix for repeated, reversible prices**.
- Think **deterrence before entry, accommodation after entry**: the incumbent's incentives can change once entry is a fact.
- Treat a crowded roster of more than roughly six genuine rivals as a clue that direct game modeling may be the wrong tool.

## Anti-patterns
- **Threatening retaliation that will be irrational to carry out**: Entrants may discount it.
- **Assuming a first move wins an unoccupied market**: It may simply reveal an opportunity to other firms.
- **Ignoring sunk capacity**: A mutually damaging expansion can persist after firms recognize the mistake.

## Worked Example
The book's stylized Home Depot–Lowe's tree sets staying out to zero for each. If Home Depot enters and Lowe's accepts, Home Depot gains $1 million and Lowe's loses $2 million. If Lowe's resists and Home Depot draws back, Home Depot gains $0.2 million and Lowe's loses $1 million; if Home Depot persists, both lose more. Work backward: Home Depot would draw back rather than persist, Lowe's would resist rather than accept, so Home Depot should evaluate entry against the modest $0.2 million outcome, not the unopposed $1 million.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make the move order and capacity irreversibility explicit.
2. Evaluate threats at the moment they would have to be executed.
3. Work backward from terminal outcomes before choosing entry.
4. Use simulation and historical cases to test a simple tree's assumptions.
