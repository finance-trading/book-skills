# Chapter 12: Fear of Not Flying — Kiwi Enters the Airline Industry

## Core Idea
In an industry without durable entry barriers, an entrant can sometimes earn a modest living by remaining too small and distinct to provoke incumbents. Scaling that niche can destroy the very conditions that made entry tolerable.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Entry/Preemption Game**: Evaluate an entrant's plan against the incumbent's incentives at each stage, not against a static market forecast.
  - When to use: Before entering a concentrated-looking market with mobile capacity.
  - How: Define the entrant's intended scale and customer segment; estimate what each incumbent loses; ask whether retaliation would be profitable; revisit the answer at each expansion step.
- **Niche Accommodation**: A small entrant can avoid a response if it takes little revenue, does not slash prices, and serves customers incumbents value less.
  - When to use: Testing a limited route, location, or product niche.
  - How: Make capacity, customer choice, and pricing disciplined; identify the growth threshold at which the incumbent's tolerance changes.

## Key Concepts
- **Low entry barriers**: Aircraft, routes, and labor can be obtained by would-be carriers; apparent market share does not guarantee a moat.
- **Incumbent's loss**: The specific profit surrendered to the entrant, which determines whether retaliation is worth its cost.
- **Credible niche**: A bounded position that incumbents can rationally ignore.
- **Scale trigger**: The point where entrant growth changes incumbents' payoffs and provokes a response.
- **Strategic discipline**: Sustaining the initial logic of entry even when expansion is tempting.

## Mental Models
- Use **marginal incumbent loss**, not total industry size, to gauge retaliation.
- Think of **growth as a new move in the game**: each new route can change the rival's response.
- Treat employee enthusiasm and founder ambition as possible drivers of capacity growth beyond the original strategy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equating an attractive fare niche with a scalable moat**: Incumbents can copy or respond when the niche becomes material.
- **Raising capital to grow before reassessing the game**: More flights can invite retaliation and add complexity.
- **Using strategy to rationalize growth after the fact**: It conceals that the entry assumptions have changed.

## Worked Example
Kiwi began with two leased Boeing 727s, a simple network from Newark, and a focus on small business travelers. It offered extra convenience without starting a fare war, and its limited capacity barely affected large carriers. By September 1994 it had twelve aircraft and forty-seven daily flights; by March 1995 it had fifteen aircraft and sixty-two flights, with more destinations planned. Its original promise to stay small, simple, and nonthreatening had disappeared. The case illustrates that the first entry decision and the later expansion decision require separate payoff analyses.

## Key Takeaways
1. Map incumbent incentives at the proposed scale, then remap them before each expansion.
2. Distinguish a workable niche from a defensible franchise.
3. Keep route complexity and fixed commitments consistent with the initial entry thesis.
4. Treat an industry's long record of poor investor returns as evidence about its competitive structure.
