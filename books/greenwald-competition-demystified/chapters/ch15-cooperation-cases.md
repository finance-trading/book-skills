# Chapter 15: Cooperation — The Dos and Don'ts

## Core Idea
Cooperation depends on credible mutual benefit and restraint. Nintendo damaged its network by extracting too much from game developers and retailers; the lead-additive case shows a stable but legally sensitive producer arrangement; Sotheby's and Christie's show the severe consequences of unlawful collusion.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Virtuous Circle of Complementors**: A platform's installed base attracts complementary suppliers, which improves the platform's appeal.
  - When to use: Assessing a system business with developers, distributors, or other partners.
  - How: Map the platform, complementors, and buyers; identify each side's gain and bargaining power; ask whether the platform's terms keep complementors invested across technology cycles.
- **Cooperation Sustainability Test**: Joint gain requires credible participation and a division each party accepts.
  - When to use: Evaluating lawful partnerships or standards.
  - How: Check outside options, proportionality, monitoring, adaptability, and antitrust limits.

## Key Concepts
- **Complementor**: A party whose product raises the value of another firm's system.
- **Installed base**: Existing customers attract game developers and retailers, reinforcing adoption.
- **Technology reset**: A new console generation weakens the old installed-base advantage.
- **Overcapacity**: Excess production makes price rivalry especially destructive.
- **Buyer’s premium**: Auction-house charge to buyers, an important element of the Sotheby's/Christie's case.
- **Antitrust constraint**: Competition law limits or forbids coordination among rivals.

## Mental Models
- Think **partner payoffs across the next cycle**, not just current platform share.
- Use **individual rationality** from Ch 14 to test whether developers and distributors will continue cooperating.
- Treat a declining market with excess capacity as especially prone to destructive competition and heightened compliance risk.

## Anti-patterns
- **Exploiting complementors because current share is high**: They can migrate when technology resets the platform.
- **Confusing observed industry discipline with lawful conduct**: Practices may attract enforcement even if profitable.
- **Directly fixing prices or fees with a rival**: The auction-house episode led to legal sanctions.

## Worked Example
Nintendo's 8-bit console enjoyed a powerful loop: more buyers attracted game writers, and more games attracted buyers. Strict licensing and aggressive terms extracted value from writers and retailers. When new chips made the 8-bit installed base less useful, these partners had less reason to stay loyal, enabling rival systems. The example shows how a platform can weaken its own protective network by ignoring the surplus its complementors need to retain.

## Key Takeaways
1. Preserve complementors' incentives, especially before a technology transition.
2. Check whether the claimed cooperative surplus can survive each party's outside option.
3. Analyze historical cooperation with explicit legal boundaries.
4. Avoid treating present dominance as permanent when the installed base can reset.
