# Chapter 16: Valuation from a Strategic Perspective — Improving Investment Decisions

## Core Idea
Value a business in layers of increasing uncertainty: reproducible assets, normalized current earnings power, then growth. The gap between asset value and earnings power is a test of competitive advantage; growth creates value only when incremental returns exceed capital cost behind a defensible position.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three Cuts of Strategic Valuation**: Estimate **asset value**, **earnings power value (EPV)**, and **value of growth** separately.
  - When to use: Valuing a company, acquisition, or investment project.
  - How: Estimate asset reproduction cost less liabilities; normalize maintainable cash earnings and capitalize them at an appropriate cost of capital; compare EPV with asset value; add growth only after identifying a sustainable barrier.
- **EPV–Asset Configuration**: Interpret the relation of the first two cuts.
  - When to use: Diagnosing franchise value and capital allocation.
  - How: EPV above asset value suggests a protected franchise; rough equality suggests competitive returns; EPV below asset value suggests poor use of resources or a disadvantaged position.
- **Growth Quality Test**: Compare incremental return on invested capital with cost of capital, then ask whether rivals can replicate the return.
  - When to use: Evaluating expansion assumptions in a DCF or project proposal.
  - How: Verify historical economics, identify barriers, and test whether expansion stays inside the protected market.

## Key Concepts
- **Asset value**: Reproduction value of present resources net of obligations.
- **EPV**: Capitalized value of normalized current earning power without growth.
- **Franchise value**: EPV in excess of asset value, sustainable only with barriers to entry.
- **Cost of capital**: Required return for capital of comparable risk.
- **Terminal value risk**: A remote cash-flow estimate that can dominate a conventional NPV while resting on fragile assumptions.

## Mental Models
- Treat **asset → earnings → growth** as a confidence ladder, not interchangeable estimates.
- Use **EPV > assets** as a prompt to find the moat, not as proof of one.
- Think **growth multiplies existing economics**: it helps only if each added dollar earns more than it costs.

## Anti-patterns
- **Hiding competitive assumptions inside a DCF terminal value**: Precision in arithmetic does not validate the franchise.
- **Rewarding growth at sub-cost returns**: More investment deepens value destruction.
- **Capitalizing cyclical peak earnings as permanent**: EPV requires normalization.

## Worked Example
The chapter's illustrative project invests $100 million at a 10% capital cost. If the business earns 8% on that investment, annual earnings are $8 million against $10 million required, a $2 million annual shortfall. Growth destroys value even though revenue and earnings rise. If a protected franchise can earn more than 10% on comparable incremental capital, growth may add a third layer of value; the analyst must still establish that protection and its market boundary.

## Key Takeaways
1. Calculate asset value and normalized EPV before assigning growth value.
2. Reconcile EPV above assets with a specific, sustainable barrier.
3. Reject growth assumptions unsupported by incremental returns and protected market space.
4. Use NPV as arithmetic, with strategic analysis governing the inputs.
