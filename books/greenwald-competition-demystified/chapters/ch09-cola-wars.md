# Chapter 9: Uncivil Cola Wars — Coke and Pepsi

## Core Idea
Strong shared barriers do not guarantee high profits when two incumbents repeatedly attack each other. Strategy must account for management incentives and the bottling and distribution structure, not just brand strength.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Competitive-Regime Diagnosis**: Establish that both rivals have stable share and high returns before modeling their direct game.
  - When to use: In duopolies that appear profitable yet wage costly battles.
  - How: Map concentrate, bottling, distribution, and retail; test consumer habit and local scale; distinguish brand and bottler economics.
- **Prisoner's-Dilemma Case Analysis**: Track the initiator, rival response, share result, and margin cost of each campaign.
  - When to use: Evaluating discounts, advertising spikes, and rival-targeted launches.
  - How: Ask whether a move can gain lasting share once the competitor retaliates, then compare that gain with joint profit lost.

## Key Concepts
- **Concentrate business**: Syrup-making layer with high brand and advertising scale.
- **Bottler network**: Local production and distribution system that can reinforce scale advantages.
- **Cola Wars**: Repeated Coke–Pepsi contests in price, promotion, products, and shelf space.
- **Customer habit**: Repeated beverage choice supporting demand-side captivity.

## Mental Models
- Think **two elephants on protected turf**: entrants may be excluded while incumbents still hurt one another.
- Distinguish **attacks on the rival's distributor** from attacks on the parent company's economics; retaliation may be unavoidable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing share gain with value creation**: Price cuts can purchase sales at worse returns.
- **Assuming culture is irrelevant to payoffs**: A management team focused on beating a rival may reject a profit-maximizing truce.
- **Treating a successful brand campaign as proof of a new barrier**: The opposing brand can remain strong.

## Worked Example
In 1977 Coca-Cola tried a price assault in a bottler's territory to recover share. Pepsi supported its bottler by cutting concentrate prices and increasing advertising. Both absorbed lower margins, and neither destroyed the other's protected position. The Pepsi Challenge and New Coke episodes also show how perceived share threats can push firms toward decisions not justified by long-run profit. The case favors calculating retaliatory cost before approving a rival-focused promotion.

## Key Takeaways
1. Map local bottler and concentrate economics separately.
2. Test whether a promotion wins durable customers after retaliation.
3. Use return on capital, not share alone, to judge competitive outcomes.
4. Include management culture and objectives in the payoff model.
