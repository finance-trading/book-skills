# Chapter 4: Assessing Competitive Advantages

## Core Idea
Competitive advantage is a market-level empirical claim. Map the value chain, look for durable incumbent dominance and returns, then identify the supply, demand, or scale mechanism that explains them.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three Steps**: (1) Identify the competitive landscape and the markets in which the firm actually operates; (2) test each market for advantage; (3) identify the likely source and its owner.
  - When to use: Before forecasting a company's growth or valuing its segments.
  - How: Divide the industry into economically distinct segments, measure changes in share and return on invested capital over time, then test cost, captivity, and scale explanations against entrants' experience.
- **Industry Map**: Separate suppliers, components, assemblers, software, and customers rather than assigning one moat to a broad sector.
  - When to use: When a company participates in several activities or sells a bundle.
  - How: Draw each segment and link; identify firms, shares, entry and exit, and where cash returns accrue.

## Key Concepts
- **Share stability**: Persistent incumbent share despite attempts by entrants to displace it.
- **Return on invested capital**: Operating return relative to capital required, used with share stability to test for protected economics.
- **Segment boundary**: A market division at which competitors and sources of advantage change.
- **Synergy claim**: A proposed benefit from combining activities that needs its own economic mechanism.

## Mental Models
- Use **share stability plus high returns** as evidence, not proof; examine industry conditions and accounting before naming a moat.
- Think **many segments, many competitive regimes**: CPU design, operating systems, PC assembly, and applications need separate tests.

## Anti-patterns
- **Declaring a whole technology ecosystem protected**: A company may be strong in one segment and weak in the next.
- **Assuming integration creates three from one plus one**: Without a preexisting advantage, rivals can often match the bundle.
- **Mistaking short-term leadership for a barrier**: The record of entry, exit, and changing shares matters.

## Worked Example
In the book's historical Apple analysis, Intel's CPU share stayed around 90% and its returns remained high, while Microsoft held more than 80% of operating systems. PC assemblers had frequent entry, exit, and large share shifts. Apple participated across these segments, but integration did not transfer Intel's or Microsoft's barrier to Apple's assembly business. The analyst should value and strategize the pieces separately, including any narrower captive applications niche.

## Key Takeaways
1. Draw the market map before using company-wide labels such as “platform” or “ecosystem.”
2. Test persistent share and returns in each segment.
3. Explain observed returns with a concrete, entrant-resistant mechanism.
4. Discount synergies that lack a demonstrable cost or customer-access advantage.
