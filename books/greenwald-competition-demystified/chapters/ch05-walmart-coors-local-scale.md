# Chapter 5: Big Where It Counts — Wal-Mart, Coors, and Local Economies of Scale

## Core Idea
Dominance in a compact relevant market can support a stronger barrier than greater company-wide size. Growth that leaves the local cost and customer structure behind may destroy profitability.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Local Economies of Scale**: Compare fixed distribution, advertising, store-supervision, and logistics costs with sales density inside a geographic market.
  - When to use: Retail, distribution, local media, and other businesses with regional fixed costs.
  - How: Test local share and cost per unit against rivals; examine whether the footprint is dense and contiguous; verify that customers remain sticky enough to defend share.
- **Core-to-Edge Expansion**: Replicate a protected local position in adjacent markets while retaining density.
  - When to use: Evaluating store rollouts or geographic acquisitions.
  - How: Extend the existing distribution network and managerial span; compare incremental economics with a scattered national rollout.

## Key Concepts
- **Local density**: Concentration of sales within a delivery and supervision territory.
- **Distribution cost per sale**: A market-specific expense that falls with nearby volume.
- **Customer captivity in retail**: Habit or convenience that limits an entrant's ability to take a leader's sales.
- **Expansion dilution**: Loss of local fixed-cost leverage when sales are added in distant markets.

## Mental Models
- Think **big where it counts**, not big everywhere.
- Use **growth quality** rather than growth rate: ask whether each new territory inherits the old economics.
- Treat an online channel as a separate competitive regime whose low search and switching costs may weaken captivity.

## Anti-patterns
- **Attributing all success to vendor bullying**: Wal-Mart's early edge appeared before national purchasing scale, and its gross margin did not rise with size.
- **Confusing a celebrated brand with local economics**: Coors's wider distribution did not reproduce its original protected position.
- **Scaling stores in disconnected territories**: It raises fixed costs before regional density arrives.

## Worked Example
The book contrasts Wal-Mart's dense rollout from small-town Arkansas with Coors's national expansion. Wal-Mart was already more profitable than larger Kmart when it was much smaller nationally; contiguous stores let it exploit local distribution and supervision economies. Coors gained a broader market but incurred distribution costs and faced established local beer competitors, so sales growth did not preserve its earlier margins. The decision rule is to model the economics of the *next local market*, not extrapolate company-wide sales.

## Key Takeaways
1. Locate the fixed-cost boundary before calculating scale.
2. Check whether the incumbent was superior before it became nationally large.
3. Prefer contiguous expansion that preserves density and customer habit.
4. Treat declining margins during rapid rollout as a possible sign that the moat does not travel.
