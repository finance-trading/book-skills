# Chapter 8: Games Companies Play — The Prisoner's Dilemma

## Core Idea
When a few protected firms compete on price, each may gain by cutting alone even though all lose when every firm cuts. Model the incentives and change the structure or response pattern before assuming “rational” cooperation will persist.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Prisoner's Dilemma / Normal Form**: Put players' price choices and payoffs into a matrix; identify each firm's best response and the stable outcome.
  - When to use: Pricing, promotions, features, sales-force intensity, or advertising contests among a few rivals.
  - How: Define high/low actions, quantify each joint outcome, include motives beyond current profit, then test whether either firm benefits by deviating from a proposed outcome.
- **Structural Adjustments**: Change the opportunity to attack, for example through differentiated geographic or product niches, instead of relying only on restraint.
  - When to use: When repeated price contests consume joint profit.
  - How: Identify overlap that invites direct contest and evaluate lawful segmentation and positioning that reduces it.
- **Tactical Responses**: Respond promptly to a rival's move while leaving a path back to a less destructive state.
  - When to use: When structural separation is unavailable.
  - How: Make the response credible and proportionate; avoid escalation whose cost exceeds the protected value. Follow competition law.

## Key Concepts
- **Normal form**: A matrix of actions and resulting payoffs.
- **Equilibrium**: An outcome from which no player expects to gain by changing alone.
- **Defection**: A unilateral action that improves one firm's payoff if rivals keep their actions unchanged.
- **Joint payoff**: Total reward to all firms, which may be lower at the noncooperative equilibrium.
- **Repeated game**: A setting where current moves affect expectations about later moves.

## Mental Models
- Think **individual incentive versus joint outcome** before asking firms to “be sensible.”
- Use **price, features, and advertising** as alternative ways to spend margin for share.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming high prices are self-enforcing**: A unilateral discount can be attractive despite hurting the group.
- **Trying to eliminate a strong rival with a price war**: Protected, well-run firms rarely disappear cheaply.
- **Treating a formal matrix as a complete forecast**: Payoffs depend on culture, information, and repeated responses.

## Worked Example
The book uses Lowe's and Home Depot as stylized rivals. If both charge a higher price, each earns more than if both charge a lower one; yet each can win business by cutting while the other holds. After both cut, neither wants to raise its price alone. A payoff matrix makes the trap visible. The useful question becomes whether each firm's behavior, market positioning, and lawful response policies can reduce the temptation and harm of defection.

## Key Takeaways
1. Model the payoff to each actor for every plausible pair of moves.
2. Check unilateral incentives rather than assuming a jointly superior outcome is stable.
3. Prefer structural changes in direct overlap where they are lawful and feasible.
4. Price wars are only one version of the same margin-consuming game.
