# Patterns and Techniques

## Start with the Entry Barrier
**When to use**: Opening any industry or company strategy review.
**How**: Define a narrow market; inspect persistent share and excess returns; identify supply, demand, or local-scale protection; only then choose operational, entry, or rivalry analysis.
**Trade-offs**: A narrow definition reveals real local power, but defining it too narrowly can hide substitutes.

## Map the Source and Owner of Advantage
**When to use**: A firm appears unusually profitable.
**How**: Distinguish lower costs from customer captivity and scale; ask who controls the scarce resource; test whether a rival can reproduce it at comparable cost and time.
**Trade-offs**: Historic returns are evidence, not proof; accounting, cycles, and management quality can mislead.

## Test Local Scale Before National Scale
**When to use**: Retail, distribution, local media, and geographically dense services.
**How**: Compare fixed costs and customer density inside each local market; trace expansion market by market.
**Trade-offs**: Growth outside the stronghold can dilute the original advantage.

## Use a Payoff Matrix for Reversible Rivalry
**When to use**: A few protected rivals repeatedly choose price, promotion, or capacity utilization.
**How**: Specify players, feasible moves, and each payoff; identify best responses and the joint outcome; examine whether history supports the modeled incentives.
**Trade-offs**: A simple matrix omits changing information and complex motives; do not turn analysis into unlawful coordination.

## Use a Decision Tree for Durable Entry
**When to use**: Stores, factories, network launches, routes, or product platforms.
**How**: Order entrant and incumbent moves; estimate incremental payoffs at each terminal branch; work backward and test whether retaliation threats remain credible.
**Trade-offs**: Sunk costs and strategic motives may change payoffs over time; rerun after material expansion.

## Test a Niche Entrant's Scale Trigger
**When to use**: A small entrant expects incumbents to ignore it.
**How**: State capacity, customer segment, and pricing discipline; estimate incumbent profit loss; define the scale at which tolerance ends.
**Trade-offs**: A niche can sustain operations without creating a scalable franchise.

## Check Sustainable Cooperation
**When to use**: Lawful alliances, shared standards, or supplier-platform relationships.
**How**: Estimate joint gain, each participant's BATNA, and a division satisfying individual rationality, symmetry, and proportionality where applicable; review legal limits.
**Trade-offs**: Creating more surplus does not guarantee a stable division; competitor agreements face antitrust constraints.

## Value in Three Cuts
**When to use**: Company, project, or acquisition valuation.
**How**: Estimate net reproduction asset value, normalized EPV, and only then growth value; explain EPV above assets with a barrier and growth with incremental returns above capital cost.
**Trade-offs**: Asset values can be hard to estimate and even protected growth can be uncertain; retain each layer separately.

## Audit a Takeover Premium
**When to use**: M&A proposals.
**How**: List verified cost savings, subtract execution risk, compare present value with the premium, and ask whether contracts can capture the same gain without ownership.
**Trade-offs**: Conservative screening may pass over rare revenue synergies, but guards against paying for unprovable claims.

## Focus an Unprotected Business
**When to use**: Entry barriers are absent or weak.
**How**: Benchmark comparable unit costs and service; pick a narrow productivity target; maintain management attention and measure results.
**Trade-offs**: Operational gains can be copied and may not create a durable moat, yet can materially improve returns.
