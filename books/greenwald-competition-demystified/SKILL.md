## Core Frameworks and Mental Models

### One single force: barriers to entry

Start a strategic analysis by defining the **specific market** and asking whether an incumbent has a defensible barrier to entry. An industry can be attractive to customers yet poor for investors if entrants can reproduce the incumbent's position. Supply advantages, customer captivity, and economies of scale coupled with demand advantages are the principal mechanisms developed in Chapters 2–3. Stable share and returns above capital cost are useful evidence, but cycles, accounting, and superior operations can imitate them. Use the three-step landscape and financial test in [Ch 4](chapters/ch04-assessing-competitive-advantages.md).

The barrier belongs to a *market*, not necessarily to an entire corporation. Wal-Mart's store density, distribution, and advertising economics were local; Coors's position changed as its geographic reach changed. A firm large nationally may lack the relevant local scale, while a smaller local champion may own it. Use [Ch 5](chapters/ch05-walmart-coors-local-scale.md) when a strategy depends on geography or distribution.

### Choose the analysis that fits the market

If there is **no durable barrier**, expect excess returns to draw entry. Concentrate on operational effectiveness, costs, service, and disciplined resource use. If **one incumbent owns the barrier**, an entrant should not assume that corporate size or a good product removes the incumbent's target-market advantages. Kodak's move against Polaroid in [Ch 13](chapters/ch13-kodak-polaroid.md) illustrates this. If **a few competitors are protected**, model their interactions. For repeated, reversible price or promotion choices, use a payoff matrix and best responses ([Ch 8](chapters/ch08-prisoners-dilemma.md) and [Ch 9](chapters/ch09-cola-wars.md)); for durable capacity and entry moves, use a sequential tree and work backward from terminal payoffs ([Ch 11](chapters/ch11-entry-and-preemption-games.md)).

### Advantages can erode as the firm grows

Scale works only relative to the relevant market's fixed costs and loyal demand. Moving beyond that boundary can dilute rather than strengthen the franchise. Compaq and Apple show niche-versus-growth trade-offs ([Ch 6](chapters/ch06-niche-advantages-and-growth.md)); production know-how can be learned or bypassed ([Ch 7](chapters/ch07-production-advantages-lost.md)); Kiwi's small, nonthreatening airline niche lost its original logic when capacity and routes multiplied ([Ch 12](chapters/ch12-kiwi-airline-entry.md)). Reassess the moat and rivals' payoffs at every expansion step.

### Cooperation: enlarge the pie, then divide it

A cooperative lens first estimates possible joint surplus and then tests whether each party receives at least its noncooperative alternative. The book uses Nash's **individual rationality**, **symmetry**, and **linear invariance** as conditions for a stable division ([Ch 14](chapters/ch14-cooperative-perspective.md)). Nintendo's relations with game writers and retailers show how extracting too much from complementors can break a platform's virtuous circle ([Ch 15](chapters/ch15-cooperation-cases.md)). Apply this lens to lawful partnerships and negotiation; historical accounts of rival coordination are not instructions to fix prices or divide markets.

### Strategic valuation: assets → earnings → growth

Value a business in three cuts ([Ch 16](chapters/ch16-strategic-valuation.md)): (1) reproduction value of existing assets, net of obligations; (2) normalized **earnings power value (EPV)** without growth; (3) growth value, if justified. EPV above asset value is a prompt to identify a durable franchise. EPV below asset value calls for operational or capital-allocation scrutiny. Growth adds value only where incremental returns exceed the cost of capital and competitors cannot erase the spread. Do not hide the moat assumption inside a distant DCF terminal value.

For acquisitions ([Ch 17](chapters/ch17-corporate-development.md)), require identifiable, sustainable gains, largely cost savings, that exceed the takeover premium. Compare ownership with gains available through lawful contracts or cooperation. A brand or a fast-growing market is an asset or opportunity, not proof of a barrier. In unprotected markets, sustained management focus and productivity can still produce strong results ([Ch 18](chapters/ch18-level-playing-field.md)).
