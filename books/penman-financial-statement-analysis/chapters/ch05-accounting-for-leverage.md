# Chapter 5: Accounting for Leverage

## Core Idea
Financing leverage can magnify ROE, EPS, and their growth without making the underlying business more valuable. Separate operating activities from financing before interpreting performance or valuing equity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Reformulated balance sheet**: `Common Equity = Net Operating Assets (NOA) − Net Financing Debt (ND)`; `NOA = Operating Assets − Operating Liabilities`; `ND = Debt Liabilities − Debt Assets`. Classify each line item by economic function, including cash and short-term investments held as financing assets.
- **Reformulated income statement**: Separate after-tax operating income from after-tax net financing expense. Compare operating returns with the cost of operating capital, then account for financing separately.
- **ROE leverage identity**: `ROE = RNOA + LEV × (RNOA − NBC)`, where `LEV = Net Debt / Common Equity` and `NBC` is net borrowing cost. Use it to identify the financing contribution to ROE.
- **Unlevered valuation**: Value the enterprise's operations from operating income and NOA, then subtract net debt to reach common equity value. Do not mistake debt-financed share repurchases for organic earnings growth.

## Key Concepts
- **Financial leverage**: Net debt relative to common equity; amplifies shareholder risk and return.
- **Operating liability leverage**: Suppliers and customers finance operating assets; its economics differ from interest-bearing debt.
- **RNOA**: Return on net operating assets, the operating profitability measure.
- **NBC**: Net borrowing cost on net financing debt.
- **Market leverage**: Market-valued financing exposure; can change abruptly with the stock price.

## Mental Models
- Ask whether a higher ROE came from better operations or simply from a smaller equity denominator.
- A repurchase can raise EPS by reducing shares without improving operating income.
- Debt can transfer and magnify risk even when it does not create enterprise value.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying levered accounting numbers**: High ROE, EPS growth, or a changed P/E may come from financing mechanics.
- **Using reported totals unchanged**: Mixing debt assets and operating assets corrupts operating-return calculations.
- **Treating market leverage as static**: A price decline can make leverage risk look much larger.

## Worked Example
The chapter reformulates Microsoft's and Chesapeake Energy's statements. The identity shows that Microsoft's negative net debt can reduce ROE relative to RNOA, while Chesapeake's positive leverage magnified an already weak operating return. The operating comparison tells a different story from standalone ROE. The Netflix vignette likewise shows market leverage rising as its share price falls even when book leverage appears modest.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reformulate the statements before calculating operating profitability or residual operating income.
2. Trace every apparent improvement in ROE and EPS to operations or financing.
3. Value operations and financing separately.
