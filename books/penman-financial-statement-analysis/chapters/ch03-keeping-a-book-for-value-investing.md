# Chapter 3: Keeping a Book for Value Investing

## Core Idea
Cash distributions and free cash flow are not themselves measures of value creation. Accrual accounting links invested capital and residual earnings so the investor can anchor value on the balance sheet and add value earned in the income statement.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Clean-surplus book**: `Equity_t = Equity_(t−1) + Earnings_t − Net Dividends_t`. Use this reconciliation to ensure distributions are not mistaken for operating success.
- **Residual earnings (RE)**: `RE_t = Earnings_t − r × Book Value_(t−1) = (ROE_t − r) × Book Value_(t−1)`. Use when evaluating whether profit exceeds the required return on opening capital.
- **Balance-sheet anchor**: `Value = Balance Sheet Value + Value Added in the Income Statement`. Forecast residual earnings instead of forecasting a distant cash-flow terminal value without a credible base.
- **No-arbitrage principle**: The value of a claim is the discounted value of its future payoffs, with risk and timing recognized; the accounting book is a way to operationalize this without treating near-term cash outlays as lost value.

## Key Concepts
- **Dividend irrelevance**: Distribution changes the form in which shareholders receive value but does not by itself create it.
- **Free cash flow (FCF)**: Cash from operations minus cash investment; an investment-heavy firm can have negative FCF while creating value.
- **Accrual earnings**: Recognize revenues and costs as economic activities occur, subject to accounting quality checks.
- **Residual income**: Earnings remaining after the charge for invested book capital.
- **Continuing value**: The forecast component beyond the explicit horizon; especially sensitive to speculative assumptions.

## Mental Models
- Think of investment as exchanging cash for an operating asset: the cash outflow alone is not a loss.
- Pair every income statement numerator with the balance-sheet investment that generated it.
- If forecast ROE merely equals `r`, book value is the value anchor; only returns above `r` add value.

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing dividend yield**: A payout may come at the expense of future reinvestment or balance-sheet strength.
- **Treating low FCF as low value**: Growth investment depresses current FCF even if it earns more than its cost.
- **Buying earnings from investment without a capital charge**: It ignores the cost of acquiring those earnings.

## Worked Example
The book compares cash flows from Netflix and other firms and uses Starbucks to expose a DCF difficulty: investment consumes cash now, reducing FCF just when a firm may be adding future value. Recast the investment into the balance sheet, forecast earnings on that capital, subtract the required return on opening book value, and value only the residual earnings above it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use FCF to understand financing and liquidity, not as a stand-alone score of value generation.
2. Reconcile earnings, equity, and distributions before forecasting.
3. Forecast growth in residual earnings rather than growth in earnings alone.
