# Chapter 1: Opening the Book

## Core Idea
Value investing is an accounting discipline: translate knowledge about a business into a defensible value anchor, then ask what speculative growth the market price requires. The primary investment risk is paying too much.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Keep an accounting book for value**: Use when an attractive business story needs to be translated into a price decision. Understand the business, separate known facts from speculation, record assets, obligations, and value-added earnings, and challenge the quoted price.
- **Ten commonsense principles (P.1–P.10)**: Buy a business rather than a ticker; know it; distinguish price from value; treat overpayment as the primary risk; use information; separate knowledge from speculation; anchor value on knowledge; beware growth; never insert market price into the value calculation meant to challenge that price; and allow time for price to converge.
- **Value = Anchoring Accounting Value + Speculative Value**: Use to identify which part of a quote is supported by current accounting and near-term forecasts, and which part depends on uncertain future performance.

## Key Concepts
- **Price**: The market's current offer, not an independent estimate of value.
- **Accounting anchor**: Balance-sheet value and earnings forecasts supported with reasonable confidence.
- **Speculative value**: The part dependent on less certain growth and future returns.
- **Fundamental analysis**: Selecting and organizing relevant information to challenge the market price.
- **Defensive active investing**: Analyzing a holding even if the answer is that price is fair, to understand overpayment risk.

## Mental Models
- Treat Mr. Market's price as a proposal to examine, not a command to accept.
- Separate business quality from purchase quality: a great business can be a poor investment at an excessive price.
- Use the balance sheet and income statement as a disciplined compression of disparate business information.

## Anti-patterns
- **Circular valuation**: Feeding market price into the model used to judge market price hides mispricing.
- **Fantasy thinking**: Building precise forecasts from a story without acknowledging uncertainty.
- **Passive ownership without price scrutiny**: Index exposure still contains stocks that can be overpriced.

## Worked Example
At the start of 2022, Starbucks was widely seen as having a brand moat, while Netflix faced competition and Rivian's market capitalization exceeded that of established automakers despite very small vehicle sales. The authors do not infer a buy or sell from these descriptions. They ask which business economics and future growth each quoted price requires, and which of those assumptions can be supported by accounting and business evidence.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with business economics and the accounting record before using a valuation multiple.
2. Name the speculative component instead of disguising it as a certain forecast.
3. Regard a fall in price as a new question, not automatic evidence of a bargain.
