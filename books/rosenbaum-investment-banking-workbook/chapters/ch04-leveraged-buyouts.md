# Chapter 4: Leveraged Buyouts

## Core Idea
An LBO acquires a company using a mix of sponsor equity and debt, relying on the business's cash generation to service and repay debt. Candidate quality depends on durable cash flow, manageable reinvestment, asset/debt capacity, and credible value-creation and exit paths—not leverage alone.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **LBO suitability screen**: Evaluate stability and predictability of cash flows, downside resilience, capital expenditure and working-capital needs, debt capacity, asset base, management, and exitability.
  - When to use: Screening a potential take-private, sponsor acquisition, or investment case.
  - How: Start from enterprise economics; stress cash conversion and cyclical troughs; test debt service and covenant headroom; then assess operational improvements and exit options.
- **Sources and uses**: Uses capture purchase consideration, debt refinance, fees, and other transaction needs; sources capture new debt, sponsor equity, and any permitted rollover or co-investment. Sources must equal uses.
- **Debt paydown logic**: Operating cash flow after interest, taxes, capex, and working-capital needs repays debt according to the financing waterfall and cash-sweep terms.
- **Sponsor return drivers**: Entry valuation, operating performance, debt reduction, and exit valuation jointly determine equity proceeds and returns.

## Key Concepts
- **LBO**: Acquisition financed with substantial debt and sponsor equity, with debt generally repaid from target cash generation.
- **Sponsor equity**: Residual capital invested by the financial sponsor.
- **Debt capacity**: Amount and structure of debt supportable under lender cash-flow, collateral, and risk constraints.
- **Cash sweep**: Contractual use of excess cash to repay debt.
- **Sources and uses**: Transaction funding and deployment schedule that must balance.
- **CIM**: Confidential information memorandum presenting company, industry, and financial information to prospective buyers/lenders.
- **Take-private**: Acquisition that removes a public company from exchange listing and public ownership.

## Mental Models
- Debt is a claim on future cash; model the downside path before celebrating equity returns.
- A good LBO combines business improvement with disciplined purchase price and financing.
- Management contributes through execution and information, not through a spreadsheet label.

## Anti-patterns
- **Leverage as value creation by itself**: Debt can magnify losses and constrain operations.
- **EBITDA-only debt sizing**: Ignore capex, taxes, working capital, cyclicality, and cash interest at your peril.
- **Unbalanced sources and uses**: A model that does not balance is not a transaction model.
- **Assuming a favorable exit multiple** without market, business, and time-horizon justification.

## Reference Tables
| Candidate signal | Why it may help | What to test |
|---|---|---|
| Stable recurring cash flow | Supports interest and amortization | Renewal, concentration, cyclicality |
| Low maintenance capex | More cash available for debt paydown | Required catch-up investment |
| Strong market position | May protect margins | Competitive threats and customer power |
| Non-core assets / improvement opportunity | Potential proceeds or operating gains | Execution, separation, and timing costs |
| Fragmented market | Possible consolidation | Integration, competition, and price discipline |

## Worked Example
A sponsor drafts uses for purchase equity consideration, refinancing of existing debt, and transaction fees. Sources include new debt tranches and sponsor equity. First reconcile sources to uses; the sponsor check is the residual after debt and other sources. Then forecast operating cash flow after tax, capex, working-capital needs, and interest to estimate repayment. If the business misses plan, rerun debt service and covenant headroom before assuming the same exit. This is the screening logic; exact financing terms must come from the transaction's documentation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen cash conversion and downside durability before leverage.
2. Balance sources and uses and make the equity check explicit.
3. Model debt service from cash flow after essential reinvestment.
4. Separate operational improvements, deleveraging, and multiple change as return drivers.
5. Treat financing capacity and exit assumptions as constraints to test, not facts to assume.
